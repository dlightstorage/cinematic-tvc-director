## 1.1 CREATIVE BRIEF TEMPLATE

```
CREATIVE BRIEF
Project:                        Version:            Date:
Prepared by:                    Approved by:

SOURCE TAGS: [FROM DOC p.N] verified from supplied material
             [DERIVED]      interpreted by us
             [ASSUMED]      not evidenced, flagged

1. THE PROBLEM
   Diagnosis, one paragraph. Not a wish.
   What has been tried, and what happened.
   Why this is a communication problem and not another kind.

2. THE AUDIENCE
   All audiences named in source material:
   Primary audience, described as one person:
   Currently believes:
   Currently does:
   Must come to believe:
   Secondary audiences and how they are served (or not):

3. OBSERVATIONS
   O1. [statement]                      source:
   O2. [statement]                      source:

4. INSIGHT
   I1. [explanation]                    explains: O1, O3
       reversal test:
   I2. [explanation]                    explains: O2

5. SINGLE-MINDED PROPOSITION
   [one sentence]
   Descends from: I1

6. SUPPORT (reason to believe)

7. NON-NEGOTIABLES
   Runtime(s):
   Cutdowns:
   Aspect ratios and placements:
   Languages and register per language:
   Mandatory elements:
   Endframe:
   Budget envelope:
   Territory:
   Approval chain and order:

8. CLAIMS
   | Claim | Qualification | Evidence | Cleared? | Use in this execution? |

9. TONE
   Brand voice, with verbatim examples:
   Off limits:
   Must not resemble:

10. SUCCESS MEASURE
    How judged, by whom, against what:
    What would make this a failure even if well made:

11. OPEN QUESTIONS AND ASSUMPTIONS
    Blocking (work should not proceed):
    Non-blocking (proceeding on stated assumption):

12. CONTRADICTIONS FOUND IN SOURCE MATERIAL
    | Statement A | Statement B | Proceeding on | Why |
```

### The six things a good brief contains (field reference)

| Element | The question it answers | Failure signature when weak |
|---|---|---|
| **The problem** | What is actually wrong, in business terms, that communication can fix? | "Raise awareness." A goal with no diagnosis attached. |
| **The audience** | Who specifically, described as a person and not a demographic bracket? | "Males 25 to 45, ABC1." A media buy description masquerading as a human. |
| **The insight** | What do we know about that person that gives us leverage? | A restated product feature, or a platitude. |
| **The single-minded proposition** | The one thing we want them to take away? | Three things joined by commas. |
| **The non-negotiables** | Mandatories, legal constraints, brand rules, runtime, formats, budget envelope | Discovered late, in the edit, at cost. |
| **The success measure** | How will we know this worked? | Absent, or "engagement." |

## 1.3 VO SCRIPT TEMPLATE WITH RUNTIME ACCOUNTING

```
SCRIPT
Project:            Duration: 30s          Language:          Version:

CONTINUOUS COPY (written first, timed aloud before any beat break)
"................................................................
 ................................................................
 ................................................................"
Word count:            Timed read (aloud, stopwatch):        s
Read pace:             words per second
Available for VO after endframe and supers:                  s
Headroom / deficit:                                          s

BEAT BREAKDOWN
| # | Duration | Cum.  | VO (verbatim)      | Action | Supers | Job of beat |
|---|----------|-------|--------------------|--------|--------|-------------|
| 1 | 4.0s     | 4.0   |                    |        |        |             |
| 2 | 5.5s     | 9.5   |                    |        |        |             |
| 3 | 1.5s     | 11.0  | [SILENCE, deliberate: the pause before the turn] |  |  | |
| 4 | 8.0s     | 19.0  |                    |        |        |             |
| 5 | 8.0s     | 27.0  |                    |        |        |             |
| 6 | 3.0s     | 30.0  | endframe           | logo   | legal  | landing     |

RUNTIME ACCOUNTING
   Total VO time (sum of timed reads):              s
   Total deliberate silence (labelled):             s
   Endframe:                                        s
   Legal super read time (if voiced):               s
   Unaccounted time:                                s   <- must be 0.0
   Total:                                           s   <- must equal duration

PER LANGUAGE
   This script is language ___ of ___.
   Candidates generated natively for this language: yes / no
   If no, STOP. This is a translation, not a version.
   Timed read for this language:               s (do NOT infer from other language)
   Register decision:
   Register decision made relative to another version? If yes, name it.

SILENCE DECLARATION
   Every non-VO stretch above 0.7s is either listed as a labelled silence
   or is a defect. Unlabelled gaps will be filled or cut by someone else.
```

**Governing arithmetic rules for this template:**
- The correct order is: proposition, then continuous voiceover, then timed read, then beat structure, then shots. Never shots first.
- Spoken voiceover in a commercial register runs, as a working figure, at roughly two to two and a half words per second in English, slower for a warm or intimate read, faster for a listing or urgent read. Never estimate a foreign language runtime from the word count of the source language.
- A thirty second film holds roughly fifty five to seventy words of comfortable voiceover, less if there is an endframe, less again if there is a legal super to be read, less again if any moment is meant to breathe.
- A logo and line endframe consumes two to three seconds. Those seconds come out of the story, not out of nowhere.
- Silence appears in the script as an explicit item with a duration, labelled as such: "1.5s, no VO, sound design only, the pause before the turn."

## 1.4 STAGE-BY-STAGE CONCEPT DEVELOPMENT STRUCTURE

Each stage has a required output. A stage is not complete until its output exists in writing. No stage may begin before the previous stage's output is fixed.

| Stage | Name | Must produce | Gate before proceeding |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | Mine and intake | Ten mining lists, answered question set with source tags, blocking questions list | No unanswered non-negotiable |
| **1** | Problem statement | One paragraph diagnosis. Not a wish | A reader can name what is wrong without reading further |
| **2** | Audience decision | Primary audience named as a person, with current belief, current behaviour, required belief. All other named audiences listed as secondary with an explicit note | Exactly one primary named |
| **3** | Observation set | Numbered observations, each with its evidence source | Each is verifiable and none is an interpretation |
| **4** | Insight set | Numbered insights, each explicitly linked to the observation numbers it explains | Each passes the reversal test |
| **5** | Proposition | One sentence. Plus the support | No "and also" can be inserted |
| **6** | Territories | Two to four directions, each naming the insight number it descends from | Each territory is genuinely different in kind, not in decoration |
| **7** | Concepts | One to three concepts per selected territory. Each stated in one sentence, executable more than one way | Each concept names its territory and its insight number |
| **8** | Structure | Beat map for the chosen concept: beat number, job of the beat, provisional duration. Breadth beat identified and labelled | Durations sum to runtime including endframe |
| **9** | Continuous copy | Full voiceover written as one unbroken paragraph, per language, timed aloud | Timed read fits the beat budget from stage 8 |
| **10** | Script | Copy broken into beats, action per beat, supers, endframe, runtime accounting table | Every second accounted for. Silence is explicit and labelled |
| **11** | Audits | Traceability table, list device audit both directions, claim audit, per language audit | All green or all exceptions declared |
| **12** | Concept document | Assembled deliverable, all stages visible, all tags intact | Passes the pre release audit |

**Two governing principles on this structure:**
- Stages 3, 4 and 5 must be separate written artifacts even when they feel obvious. Collapsing them is what produces the frame shift break, because when observation, insight and proposition are written in one motion, the joins are never articulated and therefore can never be checked.
- Stage 6 exists to prevent premature commitment. A single territory taken straight from proposition to concept means the first idea is the only idea, and it will be defended rather than tested.

### The ten-pass mining structure feeding Stage 0

| Pass | What you are looking for, and nothing else | What you write down |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Facts** | Numbers, dates, market positions, product specifications, prices, distribution, competitor names | A flat list of verifiable statements with page references |
| **2. Constraints** | Anything stated as fixed: runtime, formats, languages, legal wording, mandatory elements, budget, territory, approval chain | The non-negotiables list |
| **3. Claims** | Everything the brand is permitted to say, and the qualification attached to each | Claim, qualification, evidence source, expiry if any |
| **4. Stated audience** | Every group named anywhere in the document, including ones mentioned once in passing | Audience list, with the sentence each was named in |
| **5. Language of the client** | The exact words the client uses for their own product, benefits and users | Vocabulary list, verbatim |
| **6. Contradictions** | Places where the document says two incompatible things | Contradiction log, with both quotes |
| **7. Silences** | What a document of this type would normally contain and this one does not | Gap list. This is the highest value pass |
| **8. Observations** | Statements of consumer behaviour, whether or not the document calls them insights | Raw observation list |
| **9. Candidate insights** | Your own interpretation of the pass 8 material | Insight candidates, each linked to the observation it explains |
| **10. Success measures** | Anything that implies how this will be judged | Measure list |

**Three rules govern self answering from a document:**
1. Every self answered item carries a tag in the output document: `[FROM DOC p.N]`, `[DERIVED]`, or `[ASSUMED]`.
2. Non-negotiables may never be assumed. Runtime, legal wording, claim qualifications and mandatory elements are the four categories where a wrong assumption is not recoverable in revision. If they are absent from the document, they are blocking questions, and the work proceeds with the gap stated in writing at the top of the document.
3. Contradictions are surfaced, not resolved silently. When the document says two incompatible things, quote both, state which one the work proceeds on, and state why.

## 1.5 OBSERVATION vs INSIGHT vs PROPOSITION, WITH TESTS

| | Observation | Insight | Proposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Something true that can be seen or measured | An explanation of *why* the observation is true, at the level of human motivation | What we will say or promise, given the insight |
| **Source** | Research, data, fieldwork, listening | Interpretation of the observation | Strategic decision by the briefer |
| **Test** | Is it true? | Does it surprise, and does it explain? | Is it single, ownable and actionable? |
| **Owned by** | Anyone. It is public | The planner or strategist | The brief |
| **Failure** | Being mistaken for an insight | Being a platitude | Being a list |

**An observation describes. An insight explains. A proposition commits.**

### The three tests

1. **The reversal test for insights.** State the opposite. If the opposite is obviously absurd, you have a platitude, not an insight. "People want quality" reverses to "people want poor quality." Useless. "People abandon rather than risk being refused" reverses to "people would rather be refused than abandon," which is a real, arguable alternative. That means the insight carries information.
2. **The stranger test for observations.** If a competitor's planner would have written the same sentence, it is an observation and it is shared property. Insight is the interpretation layer where advantage lives.
3. **The single-minded test for propositions.** Read it aloud and count the ideas. If you can insert "and also" anywhere, it is not single-minded.

**A good proposition should feel obvious in hindsight and impossible to derive from the observation alone.**

### Worked example pattern (reference for generation)

- **Observation.** People spend longer choosing a large appliance than they spend choosing a phone, and they visit the store an average of three times before buying.
- **Insight.** They are not researching the machine. They are rehearsing the argument they will have to make to the person who shares the household, because a wrong choice will be visible in the kitchen for the next ten years and will be blamed on whoever picked it.
- **Proposition.** *Nobody ever regretted this one.*

## 1.6 THE TRACEABILITY REQUIREMENT (RULE)

**Rule: every final line in the deliverable names the insight it descends from, explicitly, in the document.** Not in a comment. Not in a separate file. In the document, visible to the reader, in a table.

The chain has a fixed number of links:
**Research finding** goes to **observation** goes to **insight** goes to **proposition** goes to **territory or concept** goes to **script beat** goes to **final line**.

### The required table

| Line ID | Final line | Beat | Descends from | Chain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | *the opening line* | 1 | Insight 2 | Obs 4 (doc p.11) to Ins 2 to Prop to Territory B to Concept B1 to Beat 1 |
| L2 | *the turn line* | 3 | Insight 2 | Obs 4 to Ins 2 to Prop to Territory B to Concept B1 to Beat 3 |
| L3 | *the landing line* | 5 | Insight 1 | Obs 1, Obs 7 to Ins 1 to Prop to Territory B to Concept B1 to Beat 5 |
| L4 | *the endframe line* | 6 | Proposition directly | Prop to Beat 6. **Declared: does not descend from a single insight** |

### Rules for filling it

1. **Every line gets a row.** Including supers, including the endframe line, including any spoken product name that is doing persuasive work.
2. **"Descends from" must name a numbered insight, or explicitly declare that it does not.** A blank is a defect. "Insight generally" is a blank wearing a word.
3. **Every insight must appear in the table at least once, or be marked as unused.** An insight that appears nowhere is an orphaned insight and must be either deleted from the document or explicitly labelled: *"Insight 3: developed, not used in this execution. Retained for campaign extension."*
4. **When a line is changed, its row is re-derived, not edited.** Changing the line and leaving the chain untouched is symptom patching.

### The break repair procedure

| Situation | Action | What must be written |
|---|---|---|
| The line is right, the chain is missing an intermediate step | Repair the link. Add the missing step | The added step, in its stage, with its own derivation |
| The line is right, the upstream insight is wrong | Replace the insight. Re-derive every downstream stage | New insight, plus a re-run of stages 5 to 11 for everything that touched the old one |
| The line is right and genuinely does not descend from any insight | Declare the break | *"L7 does not descend from an insight. It descends from [brand mandatory / product truth / legal requirement]."* |

**Never rewrite a line to make it sound more like the strategy. That is the symptom patch, and it destroys the document's diagnostic value, because it makes a broken chain look intact.**

### The five ways the chain breaks
1. **The frame shift.** A strategic stage is framed one way, the final line another way, both good, no join.
2. **The orphaned insight.** An insight developed at length that feeds nothing downstream.
3. **The substituted proposition.** A second, more filmable idea quietly replaced the approved one.
4. **The lost qualification.** "The fastest in its class, when measured on the standard cycle" becomes "the fastest." A legal exposure.
5. **The inheritance gap after an edit.** Stage 4 was rewritten. Stage 5 still inherits from the previous version of stage 4.

## 1.7 THE AUDIENCE-NAMING REQUIREMENT (RULE)

**Rule: when the brief names more than one audience and the execution can serve only one, the document must state which one it serves and name the gap explicitly.**

### The required statement

Placed in the concept document immediately after the audience stage, in this form:

> **Audiences named in the brief:** [list all, with the sentence each was named in]
> **This execution serves:** [one]
> **Because:** [one sentence of reasoning tied to the problem statement]
> **Not served by this execution:** [each remaining audience, named]
> **The gap this leaves:** [what that audience does not receive from this work]
> **Where that gap is addressed:** [another asset, another phase, or explicitly: nowhere, and this is a known and accepted exposure]

### Why the gap must be named rather than quietly accepted

1. **It converts an omission into a decision.** An unnamed gap is read by a reviewer as an oversight, and the standard fix a reviewer proposes is to add a line for the missing audience. That line dilutes the proposition. A named gap is read as a scope boundary and is argued about on the merits.
2. **It protects the work at the review that happens without you.** A stakeholder who represents the unserved audience will see the work and ask why they are absent. The document must answer that question in your absence.
3. **It creates the brief for the next asset.** A named gap is a ready made brief for the piece that closes it.

**If the document cannot name a primary audience, the concept is not ready, regardless of how good the idea is.** An idea that works for "everyone in the brief" has not been tested against anyone.

## 1.8 THE REMOVAL PROTOCOL

**Rule: no section, language version, territory, concept, beat or claim is ever deleted forward. Every removal triggers a backward audit of the whole document.**

### The eight step protocol. Run all eight, in order, every time.

| Step | Audit for | Concrete check |
|---|---|---|
| **1. References** | Any sentence that points at the removed thing | Search for its name, its number, and phrases like "as above", "the other version", "both", "either", "respectively" |
| **2. Labels** | Headings, table columns, list numbering, section numbers that implied its existence | "Version A" with no version B. A table column now empty. Numbered items with a gap. Any label containing a count |
| **3. Counts** | Every number in the document that counted the removed thing | "Three territories", "two languages", "five beats", "both markets". Counts hide in prose |
| **4. Compliance** | Claims, disclaimers, approvals and mandatories that travelled with the removed thing | Approval scoped to a removed market. Mandatory disclosure required only by the removed market but still listed as global. Claim qualification that lived only in the removed version |
| **5. Inheritance forward** | Any later stage that consumed the removed thing's output | Which stages listed it as an input? Do they still have all their inputs? |
| **6. Inheritance backward** | Any earlier stage that existed only to feed the removed thing | An insight developed solely for the removed territory is now orphaned. Either delete it or mark it unused |
| **7. Traceability table** | Every row whose chain passed through the removed thing | Re-derive. Do not edit in place |
| **8. Runtime and budget** | Any arithmetic that included the removed thing | Beat durations, word counts, deliverable lists, format matrices |

### Language removal, specifically (touches all eight steps at once)

| What breaks | Specific check |
|---|---|
| **Orphaned labels** | Section titles naming the version. Table columns. Any "per language" heading now describing one language. Bilingual layout structures with an empty side |
| **Broken compliance claims** | Legal wording approved per market. Mandatory disclosures required only in the removed market. Claim qualifications that existed only in that language's regulatory context |
| **Broken inheritance** | Any stage written to consume both versions. Casting and voice direction specifying two reads. Deliverable matrices assuming two audio stems. Runtime accounting that reconciled two different timed reads |
| **Register decisions** | A dialect or register decision made *relative to* the removed version ("the formal version carries the legal line, the spoken version carries the emotion") is now incoherent |
| **Structural dependency** | If the concept itself used the two languages as a device, the concept dies with the removal and must be rebuilt, not trimmed |

### Removal record template

Every removal is logged in the document, not just performed:

> **Removed:** [what]
> **Date and reason:** [why]
> **Backward audit run:** steps 1 to 8, [date]
> **Consequences found and fixed:** [list]
> **Consequences accepted:** [list, with reasoning]

**The tell that a removal was done forward rather than backward is that the document still reads perfectly. Forward deletion produces a document with no visible seams and several invisible false statements.**

## 1.9 THE LIST-DEVICE WORD-FOR-WORD AUDIT RULE

**When a line is built as a list, the audience does not hear it as poetry. They hear it as an inventory.** The list form makes an implicit claim of completeness. The viewer reads the enumerated items as the full set of what this product does, and reads anything outside the enumeration as outside the offer.

Any list device in a script requires a line by line reconciliation table before the script is approved. Not a general check. A literal enumeration.

**Direction one, words to screen:**

| Word in the list | Shown on screen? | Shot reference | Named in supers? | Compliance status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| item 1 | yes | 04 | no | claim approved |
| item 2 | yes | 06, 07 | yes | claim approved |
| item 3 | **no** | none | no | **unsupported** |

**Direction two, screen to words (the one that gets skipped):**

| Thing shown on screen | Named in the list? | If no, why is it there? |
|---|---|---|
| element A | yes | |
| element B | **no** | **decision required: cut it, or add it to the list, or accept it reads as afterthought** |

**Both directions must be run. Auditing only from the words to the screen catches unsupported claims and misses orphaned visuals, which are the ones that make a film feel unfocused without anyone being able to say why.**

**The scaling hazard.** A three item list is a rhythm. A five item list is a specification sheet. When a stakeholder asks for one more item in the list, the correct response is not to add it. It is to state what it costs in runtime and what it takes away from the other items.

## 1.10 TRIGGER TABLE: WHICH UPSTREAM CHANGES FORCE A REBUILD

| Upstream change | Severity | Stages to rebuild | Audits to re-run |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem statement changed by client** | Critical. Full rebuild | 1 through 12 | All |
| **Proposition changed** | Critical | 5 through 12 | Traceability, claims, list device, all languages |
| **Insight replaced or invalidated** | Critical | 4 through 12 | Traceability (full re-derive), territory selection |
| **Primary audience changed** | Critical | 2 through 12 | Audience statement, traceability, register decisions |
| **Runtime changed** | Critical | 8 through 11 | Runtime accounting, continuous copy re-timed, beat durations, all languages |
| **A language version added** | High | 9 through 11 for the new language only | Native candidates, timed read, list device audit, register decision, deliverable matrix |
| **A language version removed** | High | Removal protocol, then 10, 11 | All eight removal steps, plus compliance, plus inheritance both directions |
| **Register or dialect decision changed** | High | 9 through 11 for that language | Native candidates regenerated, timed read, casting and voice implications |
| **Claim withdrawn or qualification added by legal** | High | 10, 11 | Claim audit, list device audit, traceability for any line carrying it, supers |
| **Claim added to the permission set** | Low | None automatically | Add to "approved but not used here" unless the proposition changes. **Do not insert into the script by default** |
| **Scope broadened ("can it also cover X")** | Medium to high | 8, then 9 and 10 for the affected beat only | Identify breadth beat, expand only it, restate trade numerically, re-run runtime accounting and list device audit |
| **A beat cut** | Medium to high | Removal protocol, then 8 through 11 | Runtime accounting, traceability rows for removed lines, counts in prose |
| **A concept or territory dropped** | Medium | Removal protocol, then 5 through 7 | Orphaned insight check, counts, inheritance backward |
| **Mandatory element added (new endframe, new legal super)** | Medium to high | 8, 10 | Runtime accounting. Time must come from a named beat |
| **Aspect ratio or placement added** | Medium | 8, 10 | Supers legibility, endframe, whether beats survive the crop |
| **Cutdown required (30s to 15s or 6s)** | High. Treat as new script, not as a trim | 8 through 11 for the cutdown | New continuous copy, new timing, own traceability table, own list device audit |
| **Success measure changed** | Medium | 1, 12 | Whether the concept still answers what is now being measured |
| **Brand tone guideline updated** | Medium | 9, 10 | Copy pass, vocabulary check against new verbatim examples |
| **Competitor launches similar work** | Medium | 6, 7 | Territory differentiation. May not require copy change |
| **Budget reduced** | Medium | 7, 8 | Whether the concept is still executable. Concept survives if it was written execution-agnostic |
| **A stakeholder adds a claim late** | Medium | 10, 11 | Two lists argument. Do not dilute by default |
| **Source brief document superseded by a newer version** | Critical | 0, then propagate | Full re-mine, diff the ten lists, re-run every stage touching a changed item |

**Read the severity column as a rule about who must be told, not only about how much work it is.** Critical changes require the client to see a revised brief or concept document. High changes require the account team and every affected downstream document (treatment, shot list, voice direction). Medium changes require the affected owners plus a document update. Nothing is too small to update.

## 2.1 SECTION-BY-SECTION TREATMENT TEMPLATE

Write in first person, present or near-future tense. No dashes as punctuation. Every executional claim must be traceable to an intake answer or to a decision recorded in the production bible.

---

### 0. COVER
Film or campaign title. Director name. Production company. Date. Version number.

*Guidance: version number is not optional. Treatments circulate and get renamed by other people.*

---

### 1. WHY THIS FILM
**Target: 100 to 200 words. No images.**

Three moves: the human truth under the brief, the trap most executions fall into, the one move this film makes instead.

*Guidance: this is the only section guaranteed to be read by everyone. Write it last, after the walkthrough has taught you what the film actually is. Do not summarise the script. Do not compliment the agency. State a point of view that could be disagreed with.*

---

### 2. THE IDEA
**Target: 150 to 300 words plus 1 to 3 images.**

State the governing rule. One sentence, italicised or pulled out. Then explain what obeying it produces and what it costs.

*Guidance: the test of a real governing rule is that it forbids something. A rule that permits everything is a mood, not a rule. Name what this rule rules out.*

---

### 3. TONE AND WORLD
**Target: 150 to 250 words plus 3 to 6 images.**

Two labelled subsections. **Tone:** how it feels to watch. **World:** what is true inside it.

*Guidance: give three to five adjectives maximum and then immediately qualify each with a behaviour. "Warm, but never sentimental: nobody in this film cries." Adjectives alone are unbriefable.*

---

### 4. CHARACTERS AND CASTING
**Target: one page per significant character.**

Per character: who they are, behaviour and quality, what we believe about them within two seconds, and how they will be found (agency casting, street casting, real people, specialist).

*Guidance: never write demographics as a substitute for character. Always state professional versus real person explicitly, because that single word changes casting budget, shoot ratio and clearance. Cross-check against the casting brief in the production bible.*

---

### 5. THE FILM, BEAT BY BEAT
**Target: the largest section. One page or spread per beat.**

Per beat, five fields:
- **What happens** (from the board, unchanged)
- **Camera** (position, lens character, movement or stillness)
- **Frame** (what is in it, what is deliberately not)
- **Sound** (what we hear, including silence)
- **Purpose** (what this beat is for)

*Guidance: if two consecutive beats have the same Purpose, one is redundant and should be cut here rather than discovered in the edit. If the total beat count multiplied by realistic screen time exceeds the film's duration minus the endframe, the film does not fit and must be reduced now. Every beat here must correspond to an entry in the shot list.*

---

### 6. VISUAL LANGUAGE
**Target: 200 to 400 words plus 4 to 8 captioned references.**

Format and capture medium. Lens philosophy and why. Movement grammar: when the camera moves, when it does not, and what motivates it. Lighting approach: source logic, contrast, what the light is pretending to be.

*Guidance: state decisions, never options. Every reference here is a technical reference and must carry a caption naming the isolated attribute. Cross-check camera, lens set and format against the camera plan in the production bible.*

---

### 7. COLOUR AND GRADE
**Target: 150 to 250 words plus a palette strip and 3 to 5 references.**

The palette. What is achieved in camera versus what is achieved in the grade. Contrast and density. Skin rendering. Any deliberate deviation across the film.

*Guidance: always separate in-camera from post. Failing to separate them causes double-application, where design and costume build toward a look the colourist then adds again. Cross-check the palette against production design and costume in the production bible.*

---

### 8. WARDROBE AND PRODUCTION DESIGN
**Target: 200 to 350 words plus references per character or location.**

Material world, texture, period, condition, level of styling. Locations: type, scale, whether real, built or found. Props and product handling.

*Guidance: "condition" is the costable word. Clothes that look owned imply ageing and breakdown. Say it, because it is a budget line.*

---

### 9. MUSIC AND SOUND
**Target: 150 to 300 words.**

Three labelled declarations. **Music:** original, licensed, library or none, with the intended emotional function. **Sound design:** naturalistic or constructed, and what the world sounds like. **Voice:** voiceover, dialogue, sync or silence, and the casting quality if voiced.

*Guidance: any named track must be captioned "reference only" unless it is confirmed licensable within budget. This is the highest-frequency false promise in the format.*

---

### 10. EDIT AND PACE
**Target: 120 to 250 words.**

Rhythm and cut density. Where the film breathes and where it accelerates. Approximate cut count. What the final five seconds do.

*Guidance: in any timed format this section is arithmetic and must be checked against the beat count in section 5 and the durations in the shot list.*

---

### 11. THE ENDING
**Target: 100 to 200 words plus an endframe visual.**

How the film resolves into the brand. Endframe treatment, line placement, supers, and how much time each consumes.

*Guidance: treat this with the same seriousness as the opening. It is where the client's non-negotiables live and where an otherwise strong treatment signals carelessness.*

---

### 12. PRODUCTION AND TECHNICAL
**Target: a table, not prose.**

Shoot days. Location versus studio. Territory. Units. Cast count. VFX and CG scope. Specialty rigs, drone, stunts, animals, minors, water, weather dependency. Aspect ratios. Full deliverables list. Known risks and their mitigations.

*Guidance: this is what makes the treatment costable, and it is the section a producer reads first. Every entry must match the production bible exactly. Where something is unknown, write "to be confirmed" rather than omitting it, because omission reads as zero.*

---

### 13. OPTIONS APPENDIX (only if genuinely required)
Costed creative variants, clearly labelled as variants.

*Guidance: never put alternatives in the body of the treatment. Options in the body read as indecision. Options in a labelled appendix read as producer-mindedness.*

## 2.2 THE TREATMENT-TO-PRODUCTION-BIBLE SYNC CHECKLIST

Run this after **any** change to either document. These are the exact fields that exist in both and are therefore capable of diverging.

| # | Field | In the treatment as | In the production bible as | Check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Cast: count** | Characters described in section 4 | Cast list, call sheet, casting brief | Same number of speaking and featured roles. Background counts implied by the treatment's crowd descriptions are budgeted |
| 2 | **Cast: type** | "Real people" / "actors" | Casting method, agency versus street, usage terms | Professional versus non-professional matches. Usage and territory match media plan |

| 4 | **Wardrobe** | Palette, texture, period, condition | Costume breakdown per character per scene, ageing and breakdown notes | Every treatment wardrobe adjective has a costume line. "Condition" language is budgeted |
| 5 | **Locations** | Described type and mood | Named or scouted locations, permits, unit moves | Location type matches. Number of distinct locations matches the unit move count in the schedule |
| 6 | **Camera body and format** | Named in section 6 | Camera package, recording format, resolution, frame rates | Identical. Any high-speed implied by the treatment appears in the package |
| 7 | **Lenses** | Lens philosophy and focal character | Lens set and per-shot focal lengths | The stated philosophy is achievable with the booked set. Long-lens language is not contradicted by wide-lens shots |
| 8 | **Movement** | Movement grammar | Grip package, dolly, crane, gimbal, drone | Every movement type described has equipment booked and time scheduled |
| 9 | **Lighting** | Source logic and contrast | Lighting plan, gaffer's package, power | Golden-hour, night, or heavy-source claims have matching schedule and package |
| 10 | **Palette** | Colour statement and strip | Design palette, costume palette, grade LUT or reference | One palette across all three. In-camera versus post split is consistent |
| 11 | **Grade** | Described finish | Colourist brief, LUT, on-set look | The described finish is the one the LUT produces |
| 12 | **Shot descriptions** | Beat-by-beat Camera and Frame fields | Shot list entries with numbers | One-to-one mapping. No treatment beat without a shot. No shot serving a beat the treatment never mentions |
| 13 | **Timings** | Beat count, cut count, pace | Shot durations, running time, edit assembly target | Total durations fit the format minus endframe and supers |
| 14 | **Endframe and supers** | Described ending | Endframe asset, legal copy, super durations | Seconds consumed match the timing calculation |
| 15 | **Music** | Named approach and any reference track | Music brief, licence status, composer or library agreement | Reference tracks flagged as reference. Licensed tracks confirmed and budgeted |
| 16 | **Sound** | Sonic world | Sound design brief, sync recording plan, foley scope | Constructed soundscapes have post time booked |
| 17 | **VFX and CG** | Any implied invisible or visible effects | VFX breakdown, vendor, shot count | Every implied effect appears in the breakdown. "Nothing digital" claims are consistent with cleanup needs |
| 18 | **Product handling** | How product appears | Product unit, macro package, specialist, product supply | Hero product shots have a plan |
| 19 | **Shoot days and units** | Stated in section 12 | Schedule, call sheets | Identical numbers |
| 20 | **Deliverables** | Listed in section 12 | Post schedule, versioning plan, aspect ratio matrix | Every deliverable has coverage planned. Vertical crops have been considered in framing |

**Fields 12 and 13 are the highest-risk pair.** They are the ones most often written for effect in the treatment and most often quietly renegotiated in the shot list, and the divergence is invisible until the assembly does not fit.

**The governing rule: a treatment and its production bible are updated in the same pass or neither is updated.** A change to one without the other is not a partial update. It is a defect introduced into both.

Two directions must both hold:
1. Every creative claim in the treatment must be traceable to a decision that exists in the production bible.
2. Every material decision in the production bible must be consistent with, or at minimum not contradicted by, the treatment.

**Read the severity column as a rule about who must be told, not only about how much work it is.** Critical changes require the client to see a revised treatment or a written change note. Nothing is "too small to update," because the cost of divergence is not proportional to the size of the change.

### Pre-submission audit (run before the treatment leaves the building)

1. **The stranger test.** Would someone who never met the director understand what film this is, from the PDF alone, on a phone?
2. **The governing rule test.** Can the rule be stated in one sentence, and does it forbid something?
3. **The arithmetic test.** Beats multiplied by realistic durations, plus endframe and supers, fits the format.
4. **The department test.** Every sentence has a nameable executing department and a nameable resource, or is explicitly marked as atmosphere.
5. **The caption test.** Every reference image has a caption naming the isolated attribute.
6. **The competitor test.** No reference sourced from the client's direct competitors.
7. **The board fidelity test.** The story is still the agency's story. Nothing has been added to or removed from what happens.
8. **The bible parity test.** Full sync checklist, all twenty items, green.
9. **The defence test.** The director can state the governing rule and the three most consequential technical decisions from memory.
10. **The file test.** PDF, versioned filename, under 20 MB, opens and reads on a phone, no embedded video, no broken links.

Items 1, 4 and 8 are the ones that get skipped, and they are the ones that cause the expensive failures.

## 3.2 THE COMPLETENESS AUDIT

Run this on any shot list, including your own, before releasing it. It is a mechanical pass, not a judgment pass.

### The four mandatory attributes

Every single shot must carry all four. A shot missing any one is **under-specified** and must be reported as such.

| Attribute | The question it answers | The department that is blocked without it |
|---|---|---|
| **Duration or timecode** | How long is this on screen? | Editor, AD, VFX, animation |
| **Lens or lens family** | How far away does the camera stand? | DP, camera rental, grip, location scout |
| **Character or subject** | Who or what is in frame? | Casting, wardrobe, makeup, continuity, production |
| **Location or zone** | Where does this happen? | AD, art department, locations, gaffer |

### How to run it

1. Build a matrix. Rows are shot numbers, columns are the four attributes. Fill in every cell from the document as written, not as you assume it.
2. Mark every empty cell.
3. Report the count of under-specified shots as a headline number before discussing anything else.
4. For each gap, name the blocked department. A gap with no named consequence gets ignored. A gap with a named consequence gets fixed.

### How gaps typically appear

**Pattern 1: the shot that falls between two named ranges.** A document says "shots 1 to 6 are in the workshop" and later "shots 8 to 14 are in the showroom." Shot 7 has no location. Nobody notices because both sentences are individually correct and the gap only exists in the space between them. This is the single most reliable place to find under-specification, and the way to find it is to **enumerate every shot number individually and check membership, never to read the ranges as prose.**

The same pattern produces lens gaps ("the opening is on long glass, the finale is on wides"), character gaps ("the first half follows person A, the last third is the group"), and timing gaps ("the first ten seconds are the setup, the final five are the end frame," which leaves fifteen seconds unaccounted).

**Pattern 2: the attribute stated for a group and never distributed.** A note says "all product shots are macro." Which shots are product shots? If that set is never enumerated, no individual shot carries the attribute, and the camera assistant has to guess.

### The audit output format

Report as a table, not prose:

| Shot | Missing | Blocked department | Suggested resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| 07 | Location/zone | Art, AD, gaffer | Falls between the workshop range and the showroom range. Assign explicitly. |
| 11 | Lens | Camera, grip | No focal length stated anywhere. Nearest neighbours are 85mm. Propose 85mm. |
| 15, 16 | Character | Casting, wardrobe, makeup | "People" is not a name. Assign named ensemble members. |

## 3.3 THE WORD-FOR-WORD AUDIT RULE

Applies whenever the copy, voice over or on-screen text contains an **explicit enumerated list**. Constructions to watch for: "every X, every Y, every Z," "from A to B to C," "whether you are a P, a Q or an R," "for the M, the N and the O."

**The rule.** When copy enumerates a list, three things must be true simultaneously:

1. **Every enumerated word has a matching visual beat.** If the copy says three things, three shots must show those three things.
2. **Every visual beat has a matching enumerated word.** If a fourth thing gets a shot but is not named in the copy, it is an orphan.
3. **The shot order matches the word order.** If the copy names A, B, C, the shots must appear A, B, C.

**Why the third condition matters as much as the first two.** An audience processes voice over and picture simultaneously. When the picture arrives a beat before or after its word, the item shown but never named reads as an afterthought, a filler shot, or worse, a group that the film included visually but could not be bothered to mention. In representational copy about people, that reading is not a craft error. It is a message.

### The audit procedure

1. Extract the enumerated list from the copy verbatim. Write out the items in order, numbered.
2. Extract every shot that visually presents one of those items, in shot order.
3. Build a two-column alignment table.
4. Flag three failure classes:

| Failure class | Definition | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| **Said, not shown** | An item is named in copy and has no visual beat | High. The line has nothing under it |
| **Shown, not said** | An item gets a visual beat and no word | High. Reads as an afterthought |
| **Out of order** | Both exist but the sequence differs | Medium to high. Reads as sloppy or as a deliberate demotion |

5. Propose the cheapest fix. Reordering existing shots is cheaper than rewriting copy. Rewriting one word of copy is cheaper than adding a shot. **Adding a shot is the last resort.**

### Output format

| # | Copy word (verbatim) | Word position | Shot showing it | Shot position | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "the first item" | 1 | 04 | 1 | Aligned |
| 2 | "the second item" | 2 | 09 | 3 | Out of order |
| 3 | "the third item" | 3 | 06 | 2 | Out of order |
| 4 | (none) | n/a | 11 | 4 | Shown, not said |

## 3.4 THE SHOT-COUNT DISCIPLINE RULE

**The law. A locked shot count is a structural fact that other documents are built on. Changing it invalidates every one of them.**

### What is built on the shot count and the timecodes

| Downstream artifact | What it inherits | What breaks on a shot insert |
|---|---|---|
| The animatic | Frame count and durations | The whole timeline reflows |
| The client approval | The approved film | Approval no longer covers what is being made |
| The AD's setup count and day plan | Number of camera positions | The day gets longer, silently |
| The budget and the bid | Setups, crew hours, rental days | The bid no longer matches the plan |
| Every shot number after the insertion point | Their own identity | Slate numbers, VFX pull lists and editor bins all shift by one |
| The VFX shot list and bid | Which shot numbers are VFX shots | Every reference points at the wrong shot |
| The music and sound design | Hit points against timecodes | Every cue lands in the wrong place |
| Storyboard panel numbering | Panel-to-shot mapping | The board and the list disagree |

That is eight documents from one added shot, and on a fixed-runtime piece there is a ninth consequence: the film does not get longer, so every other shot gets shorter.

### The cheap alternative: attribute reassignment

| Change type | Structural cost | Departments re-opened |
|---|---|---|
| **Change who is in an existing shot** | Zero. Count, numbering and timecodes unchanged | Casting, wardrobe, makeup, continuity |
| **Change the framing of an existing shot** | Zero structurally | DP, art, gaffer, and any framing-rule dependency |
| **Change the location of an existing shot** | Zero structurally, real scheduling cost | AD, locations, art, gaffer |
| **Reorder existing shots** | Low. Timecodes reassign but the count holds | Editor, animatic, sound cue map |
| **Add a shot** | High. Everything above, plus renumbering, plus duration redistribution | Effectively all |
| **Remove a shot** | High, same reason | Effectively all |

### The agent's default behavior

When asked to solve a content problem in a locked shot list, propose solutions **in this order** and state explicitly which tier is being proposed:

1. Reassign an attribute in an existing shot (who, what, where, how framed).
2. Reorder existing shots.
3. Rewrite copy to match what already exists visually.
4. Split an existing shot's duration into two shots only if the beat genuinely requires two pieces of information.
5. Add a shot, and only with an explicit statement of every artifact that must now be revised.

**The agent must never silently perform step 5.** If a shot is added, the response includes the sentence: "This changes the shot count from N to N+1. The following must be re-derived:" followed by the list.

**Corollary rule on representation:** when the problem is representation, reassign. When the problem is genuinely missing narrative information, add, and accept the rebuild.

## 3.5 THE EXCEPTION DISCIPLINE RULE

**Every deliberate breach of a standing rule must be labelled as an exception, in the document, at the point of breach, with a stated reason.**

Format:

> `EXCEPTION: This shot widens to a full shot. The rule "never widens past a medium" is deliberately broken here because the resolution requires the whole group to be visible in one frame. Consequence: the full room must be dressed and lit for this setup, and all six ensemble members must be present and costumed.`

Three components, all required:
1. **The rule being broken**, quoted.
2. **The reason**, stated as a creative or practical necessity, not as an accident.
3. **The consequence**, naming every department that must now re-open a decision.

**Why the consequence line is the important one.** A labelled exception without its consequence is only marginally better than a silent one. The value of the label is that it triggers notification. Anyone can read the exception, see their department named, and act.

### What the agent does when it detects an unlabelled contradiction

It does not resolve it. It **surfaces it as a choice**, in this form:

> Shot 17 is a wide shot. The document states "the camera never widens past a medium." These cannot both be true. Either:
> (a) Shot 17 changes to a medium, in which case the rule stands and no department is affected, or
> (b) The rule becomes a preference and shot 17 is labelled an exception, in which case art, gaffer, wardrobe and casting must be re-notified because they have closed decisions on the absolute version.
> This is a director's call. Say which.

**The agent's authority is informational, not executive. It does not choose. It makes sure the choice is made knowingly.**

**Corollary: never write a framing rule you do not intend to honor.** If the rule is soft, write it as a preference: "predominantly tight, with the option of one wide at the resolution." That sentence causes the art department to keep planning for a wide. The absolute version does not.

## 3.6 SHOT SIZE, ANGLE AND MOVEMENT NOMENCLATURE

### Shot size

| Term | Abbrev. | Definition | Framing landmark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extreme long shot / extreme wide | ELS / EWS | Subject tiny or absent, environment dominant | Figure occupies a small fraction of frame height |
| Long shot / wide shot | LS / WS | Full figure with headroom and space around | Head to feet, air above and below |
| Full shot | FS | Full figure filling the frame | Head at top of frame, feet at bottom |
| Medium long shot / American shot | MLS / Cowboy | Cut around mid-thigh or knee | Historically framed to include a holstered gun, hence "cowboy" |
| Medium shot | MS | Waist up | Cut at or just below the waist |
| Medium close-up | MCU | Chest up | Cut mid-chest, shoulders included |
| Close-up | CU | Head and a little shoulder | Cut just below the collarbone, top of head at or near frame top |
| Big close-up | BCU | Face fills frame | Chin to forehead |
| Extreme close-up | ECU | A feature or a detail | Eyes only, mouth only, a hand, a logo, a switch |
| Two shot / three shot | 2S / 3S | Number of people in frame | Named by headcount rather than by body cut |
| Over the shoulder | OTS | Subject framed past the near shoulder of another | Foreground shoulder occupies a defined corner of frame |
| Insert | INS | A detail shot of an object or action, usually shot separately | Typically no principal face involved |
| Cutaway | CA | A shot of something outside the main action | Used for time compression and repair in the edit |
| Establishing shot | EST | Any size, functional label. Orients the viewer in place | Usually wide, but a detail can establish |

**Size is not a stylistic note.** A wide shot means the whole set is dressed, the whole floor is lit, every extra is costumed, and every visible surface is finished. A close-up means one face is fully made up, one small area is lit precisely, and the rest of the room can be a mess.

### Angle

| Term | Definition | Consequence for other departments |
|---|---|---|
| **Eye level** | Lens at subject's eye height | Neutral. Baseline for everything else. |
| **High angle** | Camera above, looking down | Floor and set dressing become visible. Floor must be finished and clean. |
| **Low angle** | Camera below, looking up | Ceiling, lighting rig and grid become visible. Lamps have to hide. This single choice can double a lighting plan. |
| **Overhead / top shot / bird's eye** | Directly above | Requires rigging, a crane or a drone. Almost always a separate setup with its own crew time. |
| **Worm's eye** | Extreme low, from the ground | Requires floor cut, low hat or a hole. Often a build. |
| **Dutch angle / canted** | Camera rolled off horizontal | Changes what enters frame corners. Frequently exposes edge-of-set. |
| **Over the shoulder (OTS)** | Framed past a foreground figure | Requires the foreground performer to be present and dressed even though barely seen. A commonly missed casting and wardrobe cost. |
| **Point of view (POV)** | Camera stands in for a character's eyes | Often requires a matching eyeline shot to make it read. A POV without its bracketing look does not cut. |
| **Profile / three-quarter / frontal** | Angle of the subject relative to lens | Determines which side of makeup and hair is finished, and which side of a set is dressed. |
| **Reverse** | The opposing angle across the axis of a conversation | The single largest driver of relighting time on a dialogue day. |

### Camera movement

| Term | Definition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Static / locked off** | Camera does not move | Cheapest and fastest. Also the required state for most clean VFX plates and split-screen work. |
| **Pan** | Rotation on the vertical axis, camera body stationary | |
| **Tilt** | Rotation on the horizontal axis | |
| **Roll / canting** | Rotation on the lens axis | |
| **Dolly / track** | Camera physically translates on wheels or rails | Requires flat floor or laid track. Floor prep time is the hidden cost. |
| **Push in / pull out** | Dolly toward or away from subject | Distinct from a zoom. A push changes perspective, a zoom does not. |
| **Truck / crab** | Lateral translation, camera stays parallel to subject | |
| **Pedestal / boom** | Vertical translation of the camera head | |
| **Crane / jib** | Camera moves through an arc on an arm | Crew and rigging heavy. |
| **Steadicam** | Stabilized body-mounted rig, operator walks | Buys movement without track. Costs an operator and rig day rate. |
| **Gimbal** | Motorized stabilizer, handheld or vehicle mounted | |
| **Handheld** | Operator-held, unstabilized | Cheap, fast, and a specific aesthetic statement. Not neutral. |
| **Zoom** | Focal length changes during the shot | Perspective does not change. Reads differently from a push. |
| **Dolly zoom / Vertigo shot** | Dolly and zoom in opposite directions | Perspective distorts while subject size stays constant. |
| **Whip pan** | Very fast pan, usually as a transition | Requires a matching whip on the other side to cut cleanly. |
| **Snorricam** | Camera rigged to the actor's body, actor static in frame while world moves | |
| **Arc** | Camera circles the subject | High floor-prep and lighting cost, because every background gets seen. |
| **Follow / lead** | Camera behind or ahead of a moving subject | |
| **Rack focus** | Focus shifts between planes within the shot | Not a movement, but conventionally logged in the movement column. Requires a focus puller and rehearsal. |

Movement is the field most often written aspirationally and least often costed. Whoever writes a movement line owes the AD an honest setup-time estimate.

## 3.7 TRIGGER LIST: WHAT FORCES A REBUILD AND WHAT MUST BE RE-DERIVED

| Trigger (upstream change) | Scope of invalidation | What must be re-derived |
|---|---|---|
| **Runtime changes** (30s becomes 20s) | Total | Every duration, every in and out, the shot count itself, the animatic, the copy timing, all sound cue points |
| **Copy or VO rewritten** | High | Copy column on every affected shot, all timings for those lines, the word-for-word alignment audit, shot order if the enumerated list changed |
| **A line of copy added or cut** | High | Durations of surrounding shots, the sum against target, possibly the shot count |
| **A framing rule added or removed** | High | Every shot's size and angle checked against the new rule, every exception relabelled, and re-notification of art, gaffer, wardrobe, makeup and casting |
| **An ensemble member added or removed** | Medium | The Character column across all shots, the representation check, casting, wardrobe and makeup schedules. Prefer reassignment over addition |
| **Casting changes** (a role recast or doubled) | Medium | Character column, makeup and wardrobe prep budget, any shot where that person's face is or is not seen |
| **A location changes or is lost** | Medium to high | Location and zone columns for the affected block, lens choices if the new space has different dimensions, movement if track no longer fits, the AD's grouping and any company move |
| **A zone within a location becomes unavailable** | Medium | Zone assignment for affected shots, possibly the shot order |
| **Lens package changes** | Medium | Lens column, camera-to-subject distances, whether every shot still fits the space |
| **Aspect ratio or delivery set changes** | Medium | Crop safety per shot, framing on every shot with edge-critical content, end-frame layout |
| **The board or animatic is approved** | Locking event | Nothing is re-derived, but the shot count and order become externally committed. All subsequent changes become client conversations |
| **A shot is added or removed** | Total on fixed runtime | Shot numbering from the insertion point, every duration, the animatic, the AD setup count, the VFX list, sound cue points, the budget |
| **Budget or shoot days reduced** | High | Setup count must fall. Decide which shots merge, which become inserts, which are cut. Then re-derive durations |
| **A VFX shot is added or descoped** | Medium | The VFX flag column, plate requirements, whether the shot must now be locked off, the post schedule |
| **Frame rate or format change on any shot** | Low but sharp | That shot's capture spec, the camera order, and the editor's conform notes |
| **Product, prop or hero item changes** | Medium | Every insert and ECU featuring it, macro lens requirements, whether a partial prop is still acceptable under the framing rules |
| **Weather, permit or safety restriction** | Variable | The affected block's location, movement (drone and crane are the first casualties), and the AD's day plan |

### The re-derivation statement

Whenever any trigger is applied, output this block in this exact shape before doing the work:

> **Trigger:** [what changed]
> **Scope:** [total / high / medium / low]
> **Now stale:** [list of columns, shots and downstream artifacts]
> **Re-deriving:** [what the agent is about to recompute]
> **Requires a decision from a human:** [anything the agent cannot resolve alone]

## 4.1 THE PER-DEPARTMENT ENTRY TEMPLATE

Every department entry uses these fields, in this order, with these names, every time, with no exceptions. Fields that do not apply carry an intentional non-entry, never a blank.

| Field | What it holds | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Department** | The department name as used everywhere else in the document | Must match the name used in every table and the contact sheet exactly |
| **Head of department** | One name, one contact, one deputy | If unfilled, state "vacant, owner of the fill: X, due: date". Never blank |
| **Scope: owns** | What this department is responsible for delivering | Written as deliverables, not activities. "Six dressed zones" not "set dressing" |
| **Scope: does not own** | What a reader might reasonably assume this department owns and it does not | This field prevents the most common on-set argument. It is not optional |
| **Governing rules** | The non-negotiables that constrain this department | Brand mandatories quoted verbatim with attribution and date. Safety rules stated in full |
| **Entities involved** | Which rows of which master tables this department touches | By stable identifier, so the sync protocol can find them |
| **Key decisions made** | The settled creative and technical decisions | Each one stated as an instruction, not as taste. Convert "warm" into a value |
| **Open decisions** | What is still undecided | Each with an owner and a due date. Anything without an owner is escalated, not recorded |
| **Dependencies: needs from others** | What this department cannot start without, and from whom | Named department, named artifact, named date |
| **Dependencies: owes others** | What other departments are waiting on from this one | Same format |
| **Lead times** | The longest lead item and its ordering deadline | This is what makes a schedule real. One line minimum |
| **Budget owner and envelope** | Who holds the money and what the number is | If confidential, state "held by X, not published here", never blank |
| **Risk flags** | Things that can go wrong, with likelihood and consequence | Each flag names the department that would absorb the consequence |
| **Prep milestones** | What must be true by when | Dates, not phases |
| **On-day protocol** | What this department does on a shoot day, and who they report to | Written for someone who has never worked with this crew |
| **Anything else** | The open field | Exists so that real content is never smuggled into the wrong field to satisfy the form |

### Enforcement rules

1. Field names never vary between departments. Not "HOD" in one and "Department head" in another.
2. Field order never varies.
3. A department entry with a blank field is incomplete, not finished. Blanks are findings.
4. If a department genuinely has no risk flags, the entry says "No risk flags identified as of [date], reviewed by [name]." That is an intentional non-entry with an owner, and it is auditable. A blank is not.

## 4.2 THE CAST AND CHARACTER MASTER TABLE

One row per person or character who appears on camera. Featured background gets rows. Crowd background gets a single summary row per crowd group with a count.

| Column | Definition | Why it exists | Self-containment rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ID** | Stable identifier, never reused, never changed | The join key for every other table and every department entry | Must be identical in every table |
| **Name / character** | Both the fictional name and the performer name once cast | Casting, wardrobe, makeup and the AD all address the person by one of these two | Carry both. "TBC" in the performer field is a deferred non-entry with an owner |
| **Role and function** | What this person is in the story and what they do | Casting brief, direction, and the justification for their screen time | Written in full. Never "as above" |
| **Screen time** | Total intended seconds or minutes on camera | Governs how much prep is justified, and it must sum to the runtime across the table | A number, not "brief" or "significant" |
| **Shot references** | The shot numbers or timecode ranges this person appears in | The lookup that tells every department when this person is needed | Full list. Never a range that hides gaps |
| **Spaces appeared in** | The IDs of every space this person appears in | The cross-axis link. Drives unit scheduling and the sync protocol | By ID plus name, so the cell is readable without opening the other table |
| **Wardrobe: description** | What this person actually wears, per change, described concretely | Costume sourcing, fittings, budget | Materials, colors, condition, fit. Never "casual" or "standard" |
| **Wardrobe: number of changes** | The count of distinct looks | Drives fittings, duplicates, laundry and continuity. Folding this into the description loses it | An integer |
| **Wardrobe: continuity notes** | What must match between which shots, and any damage, wetting or aging progression | Script supervisor and costume standby | States the shots by number |
| **Makeup and hair treatment** | The specific treatment: base, aging, prosthetics, condition, hair state | Prep time on the day, prosthetic lead times, artist count | Never "natural". State what natural means here |
| **Makeup and hair: prep time** | Minutes required in the chair, per look | The number that sets the call time | An integer per look |
| **Location treatment for this person** | How this person is handled in each space they appear in: where they stand, what the space does to them, any per-space variation | This is the cell most often collapsed into the space table and lost. A person can be treated differently in two spaces | One line per space, each naming the space |
| **Physical and demographic requirements** | Age range, build, skills, languages, physical capability required | Casting brief and safety | Concrete. "Must be able to run 40 metres repeatedly" not "athletic" |
| **Availability and booking** | Days booked, days available, agent or contact, chaperone requirements for minors | AD scheduling and legal compliance | Dates, not "flexible" |
| **Cost** | Fee, buyout, usage, overtime terms | Producer. Also the answer to "can we add a day" | Currency and terms, or "held by producer, not published here" |
| **Doubles / stand-ins / stunts** | Whether this person requires a double, a stand-in, or a stunt performer, and who | Separate bookings, separate wardrobe duplicates, separate insurance | Names or "none required, because [reason]" |
| **Risk flag** | Anything that can go wrong with this person | Producer and AD | Names the consequence and the department that absorbs it |
| **Prep note** | What must happen before shoot day | The action list | Owner and date |
| **Status** | Locked / provisional / deferred / cut | Tells the reader how much to trust the row | One of four values, never blank |

### Rules of use

1. **Every cell is self-contained.** No "same as row 4". If two performers wear the same jacket, both rows describe the jacket.
2. **Screen time across the table is reconciled against the runtime and the reconciliation is stated below the table.** An unstated sum is an unchecked sum.
3. **A cut character is not deleted.** The row remains with status "cut" and a date. Deleting it causes existence drift with every other table that still names them.
4. **A crowd summary row carries a count, a description and a wardrobe brief.** "Extras" is not a row.

## 4.3 THE LOCATIONS AND SETS MASTER TABLE

One row per space that is separately dressed, separately lit, or separately scheduled. A location with three such areas produces three rows, each named as a zone of that location.

| Column | Definition | Why it exists | Self-containment rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ID** | Stable identifier, never reused | Join key for the cast table, the shot list and every department entry | Identical everywhere |
| **Zone name** | The name this space is called on every document, including the call sheet | Everyone must use one name for one place. Naming drift starts here | One canonical name. Aliases listed once, in this cell |
| **Parent location** | The physical location this zone belongs to | Drives unit moves, permits, transport and catering | Full address or "studio, stage X" |
| **Physical description** | What this place actually looks like: architecture, dimensions, materials, floor, walls, ceiling, windows, condition, scale, what is in it | **This is the column most often missing entirely.** It is what set dec, buyers and construction act on | Concrete and buyable. Never "generic", never "standard", never "typical" |
| **Dressing detail** | The specific items to be sourced, built or removed | The shopping list | Itemized. A count and a description per item |
| **Shots / timecode** | The shot numbers or timecode ranges that occur here | The link to the shot list. Drives how much of the space is seen | Enumerated, not summarized |
| **Screen time** | Total seconds this space is on camera | Justifies build spend. A space with four seconds does not get a full build | A number |
| **Extent seen** | Which walls, which directions, what is in frame and what never is | The single largest cost lever in art department. A room seen on one wall is a wall, not a room | Named directions or wall references |
| **Lighting recipe** | The intended lighting approach: key direction, quality, contrast, color temperature values, practical sources, time of day feel | Gaffer and DP prep, rig orders, power | Values and sources. Never "moody" alone |
| **Practical sources** | The in-shot light sources that must exist physically | Art must supply them, electrics must control them. Separate from the recipe because two departments own them | Itemized with type and count |
| **Color accents** | The specific palette for this space: dominant, secondary, accent, with values where the brand or grade requires it | Art, wardrobe conflict checking, color grade | Named colors or values. Never "warm tones" alone |
| **People present** | The IDs and names of everyone appearing in this space | Cross-axis link. Drives the AD's cast-per-unit calculation | ID plus name, readable without the cast table |
| **Story day / time of day** | The in-fiction day and time | Continuity, wardrobe state, light state | Explicit. A space used across two story days gets two rows or a clearly split cell |
| **Shoot day / unit** | The real day and unit | Scheduling and logistics | Date or "unscheduled, owner: AD" |
| **Access and permissions** | Permit status, access windows, restrictions, noise limits, who the contact is | The constraint that most often breaks a schedule late | Status plus contact plus date |
| **Prep note** | What must happen before the crew arrives: build, dress, strip, pre-light, recce | The action list | Owner and date per item |
| **Strike and restore** | What must be returned to its original state and to what standard | The obligation that gets forgotten and costs money | Explicit, or "none required, because [reason]" |
| **Risk flag** | Safety, weather, access, structural, crowd, legal, or cost risk | Producer, AD, safety officer | Names the risk, the likelihood, the consequence and the department that absorbs it |
| **Cost and lead time** | Build or hire cost and the longest lead item | Producer and construction | Number plus the ordering deadline |
| **Status** | Locked / provisional / scouting / deferred / dropped | How much to trust the row | One of five values, never blank |

### Rules of use

1. **A dropped space keeps its row with status "dropped" and a date.** Deleting it causes existence drift.
2. **The physical description column is mandatory and is audited separately**, because it is the column most likely to be missing or folded.
3. **Screen time across the space table and screen time across the cast table are two different sums** and both are reconciled against the runtime. They will not match each other and should not be expected to, because more than one person can be in a space at once.
4. **Zone splitting is decided once and recorded.** If a location becomes three zones halfway through prep, that is a change that triggers the sync protocol against every table and every department entry.

### The reconciliation block (carried under the master tables in every version)

```
RECONCILIATION, version [n], [date]
  Runtime target:            [value]
  Screen time, cast table:   [sum]  (people overlap, sum exceeds runtime by design)
  Screen time, space table:  [sum]  vs runtime: [delta]
  Cast rows:                 [n active] [n cut, retained]
  Space rows:                [n active] [n dropped, retained]
  Shots referenced, cast:    [n]  vs shot list: [n]  [match / MISMATCH]
  Shots referenced, spaces:  [n]  vs shot list: [n]  [match / MISMATCH]
  Open deferrals:            [n]  ([n] without owner  <-- escalate)
  Blanks:                    [n]  (target: 0)
  Pointer cells:             [n]  (target: 0)
```

A mismatch left in this block is acceptable. A missing block is not, because it means nobody checked.

## 4.4 THE SELF-CONTAINED CELL RULE AND THE MISSING-COLUMN RULE

### The self-contained cell rule

**In a reference table that is looked up out of order, every cell must be readable on its own.**

No cell may say "same as row 3." No cell may say "see above." No cell may say "as previously described." No cell may say "standard treatment" unless "standard treatment" is defined in a named place that the cell names explicitly, and even then the cell should carry the substance.

Stated as a single sentence: **repeat the content, never the pointer.**

| | What it is | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Repeating a shape** | Every row has the same seven columns, and similar rows have similar-looking content | **Correct and required.** This is the template working |
| **Pointing rows at each other** | A cell whose content is a reference to another cell | **Incorrect.** A shortcut for the writer paid for by every reader, and it hides real differences |

**The information-loss argument.** "Same as row 3" is usually information loss disguised as brevity. When a writer writes "same as the previous space," they almost never mean literally identical. They mean similar in the respect I was thinking about at the time. The shortcut collapses a real difference into a false equivalence, invisibly.

**The working boundary:** facts that are looked up per entity are duplicated into the entity's row. Facts that govern the whole production are stated once and referenced.

### Detection patterns for the self-contained cell audit

| Pattern class | Example phrasings |
|---|---|
| **Explicit pointers** | "same as", "as above", "as below", "see row", "see section", "per the", "refer to", "ditto", "idem" |
| **Implicit pointers** | "likewise", "similarly", "also", "the same", "unchanged", "as before", "as previously", "matching" |
| **Placeholder vagueness** | "generic", "standard", "typical", "usual", "normal", "basic", "simple", "appropriate", "as needed", "as required", "suitable" |
| **Empty qualifiers** | "natural", "casual", "clean", "modern", "warm", "cool", "neutral" used alone with no value or specification |
| **Deferred without owner** | "TBC", "TBD", "pending", "to come", "?" with no name and no date |
| **Blanks** | Any empty cell |

### Expansion procedure

1. **Find what the pointer refers to.** If it cannot be determined, that is a finding in itself and it is escalated, not guessed.
2. **Ask in what respects the two entities are actually the same**, attribute by attribute. This is the step that surfaces the hidden difference.
3. **Write the full content into the cell.** Not a summary of it. The content.
4. **Record the differences discovered** as a finding, because a discovered difference means somebody would have acted on a false equivalence.
5. **For placeholder vagueness, do not expand by inventing.** "Generic dressing" means nobody decided. The correct output is a question to the owner, not a plausible-sounding invention.
6. **For deferred entries, attach an owner and a date** and convert the cell to the deferred non-entry form.
7. **For blanks, determine which of the four flavors applies** and write the corresponding intentional non-entry.

### Audit output format

```
SELF-CONTAINED-CELL AUDIT, [document], [version], [date]

POINTER CELLS FOUND: n
  [Table] [Row ID] [Column]: "[cell content]"
    Refers to: [target]
    Actually identical in: [attributes]
    Actually differs in: [attributes]  <-- FINDING
    Expanded to: [new content]

VAGUE CELLS FOUND: n
  [Table] [Row ID] [Column]: "[cell content]"
    Blocked department: [who cannot act on this]
    Question to owner: [the specific question]
    Owner: [name]  Due: [date]

BLANKS FOUND: n
  [Table] [Row ID] [Column]
    Resolved as: [not applicable / deliberately none / deferred / unknown]

DIFFERENCES DISCOVERED DURING EXPANSION: n
  [each one, because each was a latent wrong decision]
```

### The missing-column rule

**Every distinct question gets its own column, even when the answer will sometimes be short and even when two columns will sometimes contain related content.** A column is cheap. A folded question is a department working blind.

A missing column is invisible: there is no blank to notice, and a table with no physical-description column will pass its own completeness check with a perfect score. The only reliable detection method is external.

### The missing-column audit procedure

1. **Take the reader inventory** (the named departmental readers of this table).
2. **For each reader, list the questions they will bring to this table.** Not what they might like to know. What they cannot act without.
3. **Map each question to a column.** Any question with no column is a missing column.
4. **For each mapped question, verify the column actually answers it rather than a neighbouring question.** This detects folding.
5. **Add the columns.** If the table becomes too wide to scan, split into a scannable master plus a detail sheet per row. Do not fold.

### The standing question checklist for every master table

| Class | The question | Commonly folded into |
|---|---|---|
| **Identity** | What is this thing called, everywhere, unambiguously | Nothing. Usually present |
| **Physical reality** | What does it actually look like, consist of, or wear | Palette, mood, or omitted entirely |
| **Quantity** | How many, how long, how much | Description |
| **Time** | When on the schedule, when in the story | Each other |
| **Ownership** | Who is responsible, who pays, who approves | Notes |
| **Consequence** | What breaks, what it risks, what must happen before | Notes |

If a table has no column that answers "what does this actually look like" and no column that answers "who is responsible", it is not finished regardless of how complete it appears.

### Known folding errors to check for

| Folded into | What got lost | Who suffers |
|---|---|---|
| Palette absorbs physical description | Materials, condition, scale, layout, what is actually in the room | Set dec, buyers, construction |
| Lighting recipe absorbs time of day and story day | Which story day this state belongs to, so continuity cannot be checked | Script supervisor, editor |
| Screen time absorbs shot count | Nine short shots and one long shot are the same total and completely different days | 1st AD, DP |
| Risk flag absorbs prep note | What to do about the risk, as opposed to that a risk exists | Whoever owns the fix |
| Wardrobe absorbs number of changes | The count that drives fittings, duplicates and laundry | Costume supervisor |
| Location absorbs zone | Which part of the location, which drives dressing extent and unit moves | Art, locations, AD |

## 4.5 THE CROSS-TABLE SYNC PROTOCOL

Run after **any** change to any fact about any entity. Not weekly, not at milestones. After every change.

### Procedure

| Step | Action | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Identify the entity by ID**, not by name. Names drift, IDs do not | The ID |
| 2 | **Search every table for that ID** and for every alias of that name recorded in the document | The full list of appearances |
| 3 | **Search every department entry** for the ID and the name | The full list of narrative appearances |
| 4 | **Search the shot list, schedule, call sheets and any external artifact** derived from the bible | The downstream list |
| 5 | **For each appearance, ask whether the changed fact is present there.** Presence is not always literal. A change to a person's number of wardrobe changes affects the cast table cell, the wardrobe department entry, the fittings schedule and the budget | The affected set |
| 6 | **Update every affected appearance in the same working session.** Never defer one | Updated document |
| 7 | **Check for consequential facts.** A change to screen time changes the sum. A change to a space's extent seen changes the dressing budget. A change to a story day changes continuity | The consequence list |
| 8 | **Re-run the sums.** Screen time totals, shot counts, change counts, cast counts per space | Reconciliation statement |
| 9 | **Write one change log entry** naming the entity, the attribute, the old value, the new value, the date, the requester and every location updated | Change log entry |
| 10 | **Notify every department named in the affected set** in a single message that states the change and its consequence for them specifically | Notification |

### The cross-axis rule

A change on one axis almost always has a consequence on the other. Check the paired axis by default.

| Change on the person axis | Mandatory check on the space axis |
|---|---|
| A person is added, cut or recast | Every space's "people present" cell |
| A person's shots change | Every space's shot list and screen time |
| A person's wardrobe changes | Any space whose palette could now clash |
| A person's availability changes | Every space scheduled on those days |

| Change on the space axis | Mandatory check on the person axis |
|---|---|
| A space is added, dropped or re-zoned | Every person's "spaces appeared in" and "location treatment" cells |
| A space's shoot day changes | Every person's booking |
| A space's palette changes | Every person's wardrobe in that space |
| A space's lighting recipe changes | Every person's makeup treatment where the light drives it |

### The naming defence

If any appearance is found under a different name for the same entity, the sync protocol does not just update it. **It normalizes the name to the canonical one everywhere and records the alias in the entity's row.** Leaving an alias in place guarantees the next sync will miss it.

## 4.6 THE CONSOLIDATION TRIGGER LIST

Propose a consolidation pass when any of these signals is present. Two or more signals makes it mandatory before the next external distribution.

| # | Signal | Why it indicates consolidation rather than another patch |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Answering one question requires reading two or more locations | The defining diagnostic. Nothing else needs to be true |
| 2 | The document contains a section whose title includes "update", "revision", "addendum", "correction" or "note on" | These are patch artifacts by definition |
| 3 | A section exists whose content is entirely findings about another section | The audit section pattern. Findings belong merged into the entries they concern |
| 4 | Any cell or paragraph points to another cell or paragraph instead of stating a fact | Pointer content is a symptom of a writer avoiding a rewrite |
| 5 | The same entity is described in three or more places | Beyond two, drift is a near certainty |
| 6 | Two locations state different values for the same attribute | A live contradiction. Consolidation is the fix, not another cross-reference |
| 7 | Strike-through, "superseded", "was X now Y" or "ignore the above" appears anywhere | History has leaked into the body. It belongs in the change log |
| 8 | The table of contents no longer predicts where a reader will find something | The architecture has drifted from the map |
| 9 | New content is being appended to the end because the writer is unsure where it belongs | The document has stopped having a shape |
| 10 | A reader has asked "which version of this is right" more than once | The document has lost authority, which is its only real asset |
| 11 | Field names or column names vary between comparable sections | Shape consistency has broken. The comparing reader is now paying a tax |
| 12 | The change log has entries that were never reflected in the body | Changes are being recorded instead of applied |

### The consolidation output

Every consolidation pass produces, in this order:

1. **The list of facts consolidated**, by entity ID and attribute.
2. **The locations collapsed**, explicitly named, so a reader looking for an old section knows it was deliberately removed.
3. **The conflicts found and how each was resolved**, with the basis for resolution.
4. **The conflicts found and escalated**, with the owner and the decision required.
5. **The change log entries written.**
6. **The sync protocol result** run after consolidation.

### The consolidation pass itself (six steps)

1. **Collect every location where the fact currently appears.** Original entry, patch note, audit section, cross-reference, and any department entry that restated it.
2. **Determine the current truth.** By recency of approval, not by which version is best written. Where two approved versions conflict, escalate rather than choose.
3. **Write it once, in the document's canonical shape**, in the place the architecture says it belongs.
4. **Delete the other locations entirely.** Not strike through, not "superseded, see above." Delete.
5. **Record what changed in the change log**, which is the one place where history is allowed to live.
6. **Re-run the sync protocol**, because consolidation is itself a change to the facts.

**When not to consolidate:** not mid-shoot on a live document that crew are actively reading, unless the contradiction is causing harm. Consolidate at natural boundaries: end of prep, between blocks, between episodes, before a client review.

## 4.7 THE INTENTIONAL NON-ENTRY RULE

**A blank cell and a cell that says "not applicable, because X" carry completely different information, and the difference is the difference between an open question and a closed one.**

A blank means one of at least four things, and the reader cannot tell which:
1. Nobody has decided yet.
2. Somebody decided it does not apply.
3. Somebody decided it does apply but forgot to write it.
4. It was deleted during an edit and nobody noticed.

Three of those four require action. One does not. A blank forces the reader to find out which, and under time pressure they will guess.

### The form of a good non-entry

A good non-entry has two parts: **the assertion that it does not apply, and the reason.**

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| (blank) | "No makeup treatment. Character is never seen closer than a wide two-shot" |
| "N/A" | "No wardrobe change. Single continuous story day" |
| "None" | "No practical sources. Space is lit entirely from outside the set through the window wall" |
| "TBC" | "Deferred. Awaiting client product decision, owner: producer, due: end of prep week 2" |

The reason is what makes the entry survive a change. If the framing rule in row one changes and someone widens that shot, the entry tells the reader immediately that the makeup decision was contingent on the framing and must now be revisited.

### The four flavors worth distinguishing

| Flavor | Meaning | What the reader should do |
|---|---|---|
| **Not applicable** | Structurally does not exist for this row, and here is why | Nothing. Closed |
| **Deliberately none** | It could exist, and it was decided against | Nothing, but do not add it back without asking |
| **Deferred** | It will exist, decision pending, with owner and date | Chase the owner if the date passes |
| **Unknown** | Nobody has looked at this yet | Escalate. This is a live gap |

Collapsing all four into "N/A" or into a blank destroys the distinction between a closed decision and an open risk, which is the single most useful thing a bible can tell a producer.

### The third-axis decision procedure (run before adding any table beyond people and spaces)

| Step | Question | Pass condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is there a set of entities here that is neither a person nor a space? | Enumerable, with at least three members |
| 2 | Does it have at least three attributes that appear in no existing table? | Listed explicitly |
| 3 | Does at least one department schedule, budget or book against it directly? | Named department, named activity |
| 4 | Would folding it into an existing table produce a column that is empty for most rows? | Yes means it needs its own table |
| 5 | Is the sync cost acceptable? | Count the new sync relationships: adding the Nth table adds N minus 1 relationships |

| Axis | Build it when |
|---|---|
| Story day | The story spans more than one day, or wardrobe or light state changes within a location |
| Hero product / prop / vehicle | Any item has variants, a procurement lead time, handling rules or its own approval chain |
| Effects and stunts | Any beat requires separate safety documentation, rehearsal or specialist crew |
| Deliverables | More than one final cut, ratio, duration or language is owed |
| Non-human entities | Creatures, robots or animals that carry both person-like and prop-like facts |

## 5.1 THE LOCK RECORD TEMPLATE

Every lock is written in this form. It is short deliberately. A lock that takes a paragraph to state is not a lock, it is a discussion.

```
LOCK-[NN]  [Short noun-phrase title]

RULE:        [The rule, stated once, in full, as a constraint that can be
             violated. One or two sentences. No cross-references.]

SCOPE:       [Exactly who and what this applies to. If it applies to a group,
             name the group's boundary. If there are exceptions, they are
             listed here or they do not exist.]

DEPENDS ON:  [Any other lock this rule was derived from, by number.
             "None" if it is a root premise.]

DEPENDENTS:  [Every department and every named section that has reasoned from
             this rule. This is the change-impact checklist.]

STATUS:      LIVE | SUPERSEDED BY LOCK-NN | RETIRED [date]

DERIVED
FIGURES:     [Only for technical locks. Every number elsewhere in the document
             that was calculated from this one. Blank means "this lock has no
             arithmetic downstream", which must be true, not assumed.]
```

Blank fill-in form:

```
LOCK-[NN]  [Title]

RULE:        
SCOPE:       
DEPENDS ON:  
DEPENDENTS:  
STATUS:      LIVE
DERIVED FIGURES:  
```

### Field notes

**Number.** Sequential, permanent, never reused, never resequenced. If LOCK-07 is retired, the number stays retired. Renumbering breaks every external reference and fails silently.

**Rule.** Must be falsifiable. Write it so a person can point at a plan, a frame or a line and say yes or no. Reject any rule phrased as an adjective. "Grounded and warm" is not a lock. "No practical fixture warmer than 3200K in any exterior night scene" is.

**Scope.** The single most under-written field. Never write "all" without asking "all of what, bounded how". If the answer is "all of the four things that exist today", write the boundary, not the word "all".

**Dependents.** Converts an invisible dependency graph into a checklist. Write it at the moment the lock is created, when the dependencies are known, not later when they must be reconstructed.

**Status.** During development, superseded locks remain visible with their status set. At handoff, they leave.

**Derived figures.** Mandatory for any lock that states a number, a dimension, a duration, a format or a physical constant. If this field is blank on a technical lock, the lock is not finished.

### The six rules for writing a lock so it does not drift

1. **State it once.** Exactly once, in full. If another section needs the rule, it references the lock number, it does not restate the rule.
2. **Put it where the reader would actually look.** A locks appendix at the back is a good index and a bad home. If the rule governs costume, it lives in costume and the appendix points to it.
3. **Make it self-contained.** No "as described above", no "consistent with the palette section", no "per the treatment".
4. **State the scope inside the rule sentence.** Not in a nearby note. In the sentence.
5. **Phrase it as a constraint, not a description.** A constraint has a violation condition.
6. **Give a number, a threshold or an enumerated set wherever possible.** "Minimal on-screen text" cannot be checked. "No more than one text element on screen at any time, maximum four words" can.

## 5.3 THE LOCK AUDIT PROTOCOL, EVERY STEP

Run in full at every trigger, and never partially, because the value of the audit is entirely in its exhaustiveness. A partial lock audit produces false confidence, which is worse than no audit.

Run the seven steps in order. Do not skip a step because it "obviously has no findings". The steps are ordered so that each one uses the output of the previous.

### Step 1: Enumerate every lock

Produce a complete numbered list of every locked rule currently in the document, including rules that function as locks without being labelled as such.

Most documents contain unlabelled locks: a sentence in the treatment that four departments have quietly treated as binding, a technical parameter stated once in a production note, a client constraint mentioned in a brief.

**How to find unlabelled locks:** scan for the linguistic markers of commitment. Words like *always, never, all, every, only, must, no, none, at least, no more than, exactly*, and any bare number, dimension, duration, ratio, colour value or format name. Each one is a candidate lock. Each candidate gets a number in the working list even if it is not yet formalised.

Output: a numbered list, each entry with its current location in the document.

### Step 2: For each lock, list every dependent department and section

For every lock, answer: **who reasoned from this?**

Work through the department list explicitly rather than from memory, because memory under-counts: camera, lighting, art, props, wardrobe, hair and makeup, sound, music, cast, locations, schedule, budget, post, VFX, grade, delivery, legal and clearance, client and brand.

For each department, the question is not "does this rule mention them" but **"would a competent head of that department have changed anything about their plan if this rule were different?"** If yes, they are a dependent, whether or not the document says so.

Write the dependents into the lock record. If the lock record already has a dependents field, compare it to what you just derived. **A gap between the recorded dependents and the derived dependents is itself a finding**, because it means past change impact was assessed against an incomplete list.

### Step 3: Check whether anything added AFTER the lock was written intersects with it

This is the step that catches the new-entity failure and it is the step most often skipped, because it requires knowing the order in which the document was built.

Establish, as well as possible, what was added late: new characters, new locations, new products, new deliverables, new scenes, new formats, new partners, new sequences. Sources for this include the process log, the revision history, section ordering anomalies, and material that reads in a different register from the rest.

Then, for every lock, ask: **does this late addition fall inside the rule's scope?**

For each intersection found, force one of the three resolutions and record it:

| Resolution | Recorded as |
|---|---|
| **Re-scope** | Rewrite the rule to state the group it was actually written for. |
| **Extend** | Leave the rule, and add the new entity to the scope line explicitly so the check is visible. |
| **Except** | Write a numbered exception with reason and consequence. |

An intersection left unresolved is reported as a finding, never left to the reader's judgement.

### Step 4: Check every count against the current structure

Find every number in the document that is a total, a headcount, a range or a summary. For each one, locate the enumerable list it is supposed to summarise, and count that list.

If there is no enumerable list, that is a finding in itself: an unauditable count. Either produce the list or remove the number.

The standard sweep:

- Characters and cast against the named character list
- Scenes against the scene list
- Setups and shots against the shot list
- Locations and sets against the location list
- Builds against the design list
- Hero props against the prop list
- Deliverables and cutdowns against the delivery schedule
- Shoot days against the schedule
- Sum of beat durations against the stated runtime
- Sum of line items against any stated budget total
- Word count of voiceover against the runtime at the stated delivery rate

Report every mismatch with both numbers and both locations.

### Step 5: Check every tightened rule for surviving old exceptions

Identify every rule that has been tightened at any point: a permission withdrawn, a maximum lowered, a minimum raised, an option removed, a technique restricted, a colour reserved.

For each one, run the hunt explicitly through the five hiding places, in this order:

1. Reference tables and appendices
2. Examples and worked illustrations
3. Summary and overview sections
4. Cross-references inside other departments' sections
5. Templates and boilerplate

Search for the *old permitted usage*, not for the rule text. The fossil does not quote the rule. It exercises it. If the rule now forbids a saturated accent colour, search for the accent colour, not for the word "saturated".

**Stopping condition:** all five locations checked for every tightened rule. Not "no findings so far". Finding one survivor and stopping is the characteristic failure of this step.

### Step 6: Check every blanket rule for a new entity it was never scoped for

Steps 3 and 6 overlap deliberately, and the overlap is the point. Step 3 approaches from the additions. Step 6 approaches from the rules. Each catches items the other misses, because Step 3 depends on correctly identifying what was added late, which is often incomplete.

Take every rule containing a universal quantifier: *all, every, always, never, no, none, each, any*.

For each, enumerate every entity in the document that the quantifier now captures, including the ones nobody was thinking about when the rule was written. Then ask, per entity, whether the rule makes sense applied to it.

The characteristic finding is a rule that produces an absurd or impossible result for one entity: a wardrobe rule applied to an animal, a dialogue rule applied to a character with no lines, a framing rule applied to a graphic-only sequence, a colour rule applied to a partner brand with mandated colours, a technical rule applied to a format it was never designed for.

### Step 7: Report each finding with its exact location

Every finding gets all seven fields, and a finding missing a location is not a finding, it is a complaint.

```
FINDING [N]  [CLASS]

WHERE:        [Exact section, subsection and, where possible, the quoted line]
LOCK:         [Which lock number this violates, or "unlabelled lock" plus the rule]
WHAT IT SAYS: [The current text, quoted]
WHY IT IS WRONG: [Which premise, count or scope it contradicts, and where the
              contradicting statement lives]
CLASS:        FOSSIL | STALE COUNT | UNSCOPED RULE | UN-RE-DERIVED FIGURE |
              SILENT EXCEPTION | DUPLICATE STATEMENT | ORPHAN | HISTORY LEAK
SEVERITY:     BLOCKING (something will be built wrong) |
              CORRECTING (a reader will be misled) |
              HYGIENE (correct but degrades the document)
FIX:          [The exact replacement text, or the exact re-derivation required]
```

Sort the report by severity, then by section order. Blocking findings first, always, because reports are read from the top and abandoned from the bottom.

## 5.4 PREMISE vs DESCRIPTION, AND RE-DERIVE vs RELABEL

### Premise vs description, as this file states it

**A fact stated in a production document exists in two forms at once.**

It exists as a **description**: a statement about how something is. "The location is a polished modern office." "The film runs sixty seconds." "The talent is a woman in her early thirties." "The palette is cool with a single warm accent."

And it exists as a **premise**: an input that other reasoning was built on top of. Somebody read the description, reasoned from it, and wrote down conclusions. Those conclusions do not carry a visible label saying which premise produced them. They read as independent facts.

When the description changes, every derived decision is now unsupported, and not one of them looks wrong. They mention downlights, mid-tones, long lenses, room tone. They read as independent departmental decisions. **They are not. They are conclusions in an argument whose first premise has been quietly deleted.**

This is the characteristic failure of a well-maintained document. Badly maintained documents fail obviously: they still say "office" in three places. Well-maintained documents fail invisibly: every instance of the word "office" was found and replaced, the document reads as fully updated, and it is now arguing from something that no longer exists.

**The test.** For any fact you are about to change, ask: **is this fact only a description, or has anyone reasoned from it?** If anyone has reasoned from it, the change is not a change to one line. It is an instruction to re-run a set of derivations. The correct unit of work is not "update the fact" but "update the fact and re-derive every conclusion that took it as input".

**The general form:**

> A description is a fact. A premise is a fact plus everything somebody built on it. Changing a description is one edit. Changing a premise is an audit.

### The premise change protocol (run before making any edit)

1. **Classify the fact.** Is it a pure description, or has anyone reasoned from it? If any department's plan would have been different had this fact been different, it is a premise.
2. **If it is a premise, list the derivations before touching anything.** Go department by department. For each, write the sentence "because [old fact], we decided [X]". Every X on that list is now in play.
3. **Classify the change.** Is it a change to a *characterisation* (a mood, a register, a style) or to a *constant* (a number, a dimension, a format, a physical parameter)?
4. **If it is a constant, switch to the re-derivation protocol.** Do not proceed with an edit.
5. **If it is a characterisation, re-derive each conclusion in turn.** Not "check whether it still works". Re-derive it: pretend the old premise never existed, read the new one, and write what follows. Then compare to what is on the page. Anything that differs must be rewritten. Anything that survives should be noted as having survived, so the next audit does not redo the work.
6. **Re-run the count check.** Premise changes frequently add or remove entities as a side effect.
7. **Record the change as a numbered decision** with the list of re-derived sections attached.

### Re-derive vs relabel, as this file states it

The premise problem has a particularly dangerous special case: **changes that look like naming changes but are actually changes to a physical or technical constant.**

When a change touches a physical or technical premise, the dependent figures cannot be edited by find-and-replace. They have to be **re-derived from first principles**, because they were never really about the name. They were arithmetic against a constant.

The class of premises this applies to:

| Premise type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Sensor and format** | Sensor dimensions, capture resolution, crop factor, recording codec and its data rate |
| **Optical** | Focal length set, maximum aperture, minimum focus distance, close-focus capability |
| **Spatial** | Location dimensions, ceiling height, throw distance, available power |
| **Temporal** | Runtime, frame rate, shutter angle, playback speed |
| **Photometric** | Base sensitivity, exposure index, dynamic range, colour temperature of the key |
| **Colour** | Working colour space, transfer function, reserved colour values, gamut |
| **Framing** | Aspect ratio, safe areas, minimum headroom, extraction crop allowances |

**The wrong response is to open the document and replace the camera name in eleven places.** That produces a document that is superficially current and technically incoherent. **The right response is to identify every figure that was derived from sensor width and recompute it.** The re-derivation list is the deliverable, not the renamed document.

**The generalised rule:**

> If a change alters a number that other numbers were calculated from, the other numbers are not out of date. They are wrong. Recompute them, do not rename them.

**The tell.** A document in which a technical name has been consistently updated and no dependent number has changed. If the sensor changed and not one focal length, distance, depth of field note, safe area or data figure moved, the change was cosmetic and the document is now lying.

### The re-derivation protocol

Output is **a list of recomputed numbers**, not an edited document. If the protocol produces no changed numbers, the protocol was not run.

**Step 1. Name the constant that actually changed.** Not the product. The constant. "The camera changed" is not usable. "The sensor width changed from approximately 24.9mm to approximately 36mm" is usable.

**Step 2. Find every figure derived from that constant.**

| Constant changed | Sweep for |
|---|---|
| **Sensor size or format** | Focal lengths, fields of view, camera-to-subject distances, floor plans, depth of field notes, lens coverage, safe areas, extraction crops, resolution, data rate, media count, backup time |
| **Aspect ratio** | Framing rules, headroom, safe areas, title and graphic layouts, number of figures per frame, viability of wide shots, logo lockup variant, crop strategy for other deliverables |
| **Runtime** | Beat structure and per-beat durations, shot count, voiceover word count, music bar count and tempo, endframe hold, slot length, cutdown structure |
| **Frame rate** | Shutter angle, exposure, motion rendering, playback speed of any effect, data rate, sync sound viability, conform and delivery specification |
| **Location or space** | Camera distances, lens choices, lighting positions and fixture count, power requirement, set dressing extent, crew movement, company move time, acoustic treatment, schedule per setup |
| **Colour rule or colour space** | Every dependent palette, wardrobe, set finish, grade note, graphic element, reserved-colour compliance, brand lockup usage |
| **Framing rule** | Every shot in the shot list, board panel, coverage plan, and any count of setups |
| **Base sensitivity or dynamic range** | Exposure plan, fixture wattage and count, ND requirement, night exterior viability, grade latitude claims |

**Step 3. Recompute from first principles.** Do not scale the old answer. Do not adjust it. Derive it again from the new constant.

**Step 4. Flag the second-order consequences.** Every re-derivation must be walked out to the department that pays for it.

**Step 5. Report as a re-derivation table.**

```
CONSTANT CHANGED: [old value] -> [new value]

| Figure | Section | Old value | New value | Second-order consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
```

**Step 6. Verify no relabelling survived.** Search the document for the old product name and the old constant value. Any survivor is a finding. If the name changed and no dependent number changed, reject the change as incomplete.

## 5.5 THE TIGHTENED vs LOOSENED RULE ASYMMETRY

A **loosened** rule expands what is permitted. The consequence is that all existing work remains compliant. Nothing in the archive is now illegal. The loosening only matters going forward, and it gets applied deliberately, case by case, by someone actively deciding to use the new freedom. Loosened rules therefore tend to propagate broadly and safely, because applying them is an act of intent and non-application is harmless.

A **tightened** rule contracts what is permitted. The consequence is the exact inverse: **every existing instance is now potentially in violation, and none of them will announce themselves.** The tightened rule requires a hunt. It requires someone to go back through every application, every template, every reference sheet, every appendix, and find the survivors.

**If you have limited time to audit, audit the tightened rules first, because a tightened rule is always the one most likely to still be violated somewhere.** A loosened rule leaves the document merely conservative. A tightened rule leaves the document self-contradictory, and self-contradiction is what gets built wrong.

| | Loosened | Tightened |
|---|---|---|
| **Effect** | Expands what is permitted | Contracts what is permitted |
| **Enforcement mechanism** | An act of will. Someone reaches for the new freedom | An act of search. Nobody is motivated to find their own violations |
| **Existing work** | Remains compliant | Potentially in violation, silently |
| **Typical examples** | A colour restriction lifted, a permitted shot type added, an approved variant expanded, a duration ceiling raised, a previously forbidden technique allowed in a defined context | A colour now reserved, a shot type now forbidden, a maximum count reduced, an exception withdrawn, a technique restricted to one scene only, a minimum raised |
| **The audit question** | Mild: has anyone actually taken up the new freedom, and if not, was that deliberate? | Severe: where does the old permitted usage still survive? The answer is almost never "nowhere" |

### Where the survivors hide, in order of likelihood

1. **Reference tables and appendices.** The main body gets rewritten. The lookup table at the back does not, because the rewrite was reasoned through prose, and nobody re-read a grid.
2. **Examples and worked illustrations.** An example written to demonstrate the old rule now demonstrates a violation, with the full authority of being labelled "example".
3. **Summary or overview sections.** Often written early, rarely revisited, and read first by exactly the people who will not read the detail.
4. **Cross-references in other departments' sections.** The rule lives in one department's section; another department quoted it in passing months ago.
5. **Templates and boilerplate.** The most durable fossils of all, because a template is copied forward into new work and re-injects the retired rule into documents written after the tightening.

### The operational instruction

> When a rule is tightened, the rewrite is not the work. **The hunt is the work.** Enumerate every location the old permission could have been exercised, including every table, example, summary, appendix and template, and check each one explicitly.

And the corollary:

> **If you find exactly one surviving violation of a tightened rule, you have not finished. You have confirmed the hunt was never run.**

## 5.6 THE SCOPED-RULE RULE

A **blanket rule** is one written without an explicit scope. "All talent wears mid-tone earth colours." "Every product shot uses the macro package." "No on-screen text." "All environments are interior."

Blanket rules are written blanket for a good reason: at the time of writing, the scope was obvious and universal.

Then the document grows. A new entity is added from a different world: a fifth character who is a child, or an animal, or a period figure; a second product with different physical properties; an additional location in a different climate; a partner brand with its own identity constraints; a second format with different rules.

**The blanket rule silently stops being universal at the moment that entity is added, and nothing in the document registers the event.** The rule still reads as universal because it still says "all". The new entity either gets forced into a rule that was never designed for it, or gets tacitly exempted by whoever is doing the work, with no record that an exemption was granted.

Forcing produces absurdity with the authority of a written rule. Tacit exemption produces a live contradiction between the written rule and the actual plan, which the next reader will resolve arbitrarily.

### The three-way fix

When a blanket rule meets a new entity, exactly one of three things must be recorded explicitly:

| Resolution | What it means | How it is recorded |
|---|---|---|
| **Re-scope** | The rule was always about a specific group. Say so. "All adult principal talent wears mid-tone earth colours" rather than "all talent". | Rewrite the rule with its real scope, in the one place it lives. |
| **Extend** | The rule genuinely should cover the new entity too. Confirm it and check feasibility. | Leave the rule blanket, and note explicitly that the new entity was checked against it. |
| **Except** | The rule does not apply here, deliberately. | Record the exception as a numbered decision with its reason and its consequence. Never as a silent omission. |

The one thing that is not permitted is leaving the contradiction in place, because a contradiction in a production document is resolved on the day by whoever is standing there, under time pressure, without the context.

### The audit form

> For every blanket rule, list every entity currently in the document. For each entity added after the rule was written, state which of re-scope, extend or except applies.

The phrase "added after the rule was written" is doing the work. Entities that predate the rule were considered when it was drafted. Entities that arrived afterwards were not, by definition.

### Related: the stale count rule

> **Re-total after every structural addition.** Not at the end. At the moment of addition, because at the end nobody remembers what was added.

> Every stated count should be recomputable from an enumerable list somewhere else in the document. A count with no corresponding list cannot be audited, only believed.

Recurring stale-count offenders: cast or character headcount, set or build count, shot count or setup range, scene count, prop or hero item count, deliverable count, location day count, runtime totals.

## 5.7 THE HANDOFF PROTOCOL AND HANDOFF CHECKLIST

Run once, at the transition from development document to deliverable.

### The reader change that makes the protocol necessary

| | Development document | Handoff document |
|---|---|---|
| **Reader** | The people making the decisions | The people executing them |
| **Question being answered** | "Why are we doing it this way, and what did we already rule out?" | "What am I building, exactly?" |
| **Value of history** | High. Prevents relitigating, preserves context. | Low to negative. Reads as uncertainty. |
| **Tone** | Deliberative. Options, trade-offs, open questions. | Declarative. Statements of fact. |
| **Presence of superseded records** | Essential | Harmful |
| **Presence of "changed from" language** | Useful | Corrosive |

> **At handoff, the audit trail moves to a process log. It is not archived inside the deliverable.**

### Pass 1: Strip revision-history language

Remove from the deliverable:

- Every "changed from", "previously", "originally", "updated to", "revised", "no longer", "we had considered"
- Every superseded lock record, moved to the process log with its status intact
- Every confirmation-only resolution
- Every open-question marker, either resolved or escalated, never shipped as a comment
- Every parenthetical alternative and every "or possibly"
- Every editorial aside about the document itself rather than about the work
- Every date-stamped note that only made sense during development

Rewrite each affected passage as a **declarative statement of fact**. Not "the location was changed to a warehouse space" but "the location is a warehouse space".

### Pass 2: Verify the strip independently

**This is the non-optional part, and it is the part that gets cut.**

> **The most dangerous leftover errors are the ones old enough that every previous audit already read past them.**

A fossil that has survived four audits did not survive because it is well hidden. It survived because each auditor had already read it, in a previous pass, in a context where it was correct, and their eye now treats it as verified background. Familiarity is the camouflage.

The independent verification must break the reading habit. Practical methods, in descending order of effectiveness:

| Method | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **A verifier with no memory of the document's history** | Cannot recognise anything as already-checked. Nothing is familiar, so nothing is skipped. |
| **Read in reverse section order** | Breaks the narrative flow that carries the eye past known passages. |
| **Audit by artefact type, not by section** | Read all the tables in sequence, then all the examples, then all the summaries. Fossils cluster in these and this reading order makes the cluster visible. |
| **Mechanical search for the old values** | Search for the retired constant, the retired name, the retired count. Search does not get tired and does not recognise. |
| **Check every number against its enumerable source** | Arithmetic does not care whether you have read the sentence before. |
| **Read each department's sections as that department** | Ask only "can I execute this", which surfaces contradictions that a reader checking for consistency will rationalise. |

The verification pass reports in the Step 7 finding format, with the **HISTORY LEAK** class used for surviving revision language.

### Pass 3: Final consistency sweep

Re-run lock audit Steps 4, 5 and 6 (counts, tightened rules, blanket rules) after the strip, because the stripping itself edits text and every edit is a chance to create a new inconsistency. A handoff document is not finished when it is stripped. It is finished when it has been audited *after* being stripped.

### The handoff checklist template

```
HANDOFF CHECKLIST

STRUCTURE
[ ] Every lock stated exactly once, in the section a reader would look in
[ ] No lock relies on a cross-reference to be understood
[ ] Every lock has an explicit scope, no bare "all"
[ ] Every lock is falsifiable, no adjective-only rules
[ ] Lock numbering intact, gaps preserved, nothing resequenced

COUNTS
[ ] Every stated total recomputed against its enumerable list
[ ] Every count has an enumerable list to be checked against
[ ] Sum of parts equals every stated whole (runtime, budget, beats)

RULES
[ ] Every tightened rule hunted through all five hiding places
[ ] Every blanket rule checked against every current entity
[ ] Every exception recorded with reason and consequence
[ ] No contradiction left for a reader to resolve

TECHNICAL
[ ] Every changed constant re-derived, not renamed
[ ] Old constants and old product names searched for and absent
[ ] Second-order consequences walked out to the paying department

HISTORY
[ ] All revision-history language removed
[ ] All superseded locks moved to the process log
[ ] All confirmation-only records folded in or deleted
[ ] All open questions closed or escalated, none shipped
[ ] Document reads declaratively throughout

VERIFICATION
[ ] Strip pass verified INDEPENDENTLY, not by the stripper
[ ] Verification used a broken reading order or a fresh reader
[ ] All tables, examples and summaries read as a group
[ ] Counts, tightened rules and blanket rules re-audited AFTER the strip
[ ] Findings report produced, blocking items resolved
```

## 5.8 THE CONFIRMATION-ONLY-RECORD DELETION TEST

As a document is developed, decisions accumulate in a resolutions list. Not all of them are decisions. Some of them are **confirmations**: records that say, in effect, "sections 4 and 9 were checked against each other and were already consistent, no change required."

At the moment it was written, that record had real value. Once the underlying alignment is stated as fact in both places, the record has no standalone value. It carries no information the reader needs. It is a receipt for a check, not a statement of what is true.

### The test, applied to every entry in any resolutions or decisions list at handoff time

> **Does this record carry information a reader of the final document needs, that is not already stated as fact elsewhere?**

| Answer | Action |
|---|---|
| **Yes, it states a rule or constraint** | It is a lock. Move it into the locks list, phrased as a rule, and delete the resolution entry. |
| **Yes, but the fact is stated ambiguously elsewhere** | Fix the ambiguity at the source, then delete the record. Fold in, then remove. |
| **No, both sides already state it correctly** | Delete it outright. It is a confirmation-only record. |
| **No, but it records why a tempting alternative was rejected** | Keep it, but move it to the process log, not the deliverable. |

The fourth row is the only real exception: the record of a rejected path has value precisely because the path is tempting. But it belongs in the process log, not in the production document, because the production document is read by people who need to know what to build, not what was considered.

The failure mode is a resolutions list that grows longer than the rules it resolved, in which four real constraints are buried among twenty confirmations that nothing was wrong. A locks list that nobody reads provides negative value, because it creates the appearance of governance without the function.

### The consolidation procedure for merged locks and resolutions lists

1. **Extract every entry from both lists into one working table.** Columns: source list, entry text, what it asserts, whether it asserts a rule or reports an event.
2. **Classify each entry into one of five types.**

| Type | Definition | Disposition |
|---|---|---|
| **Rule** | Asserts a constraint on what may be built | Becomes or merges into a numbered lock |
| **Fact** | States something true about the project, not a constraint | Belongs in the relevant department section as prose. Delete from the list. |
| **Confirmation** | Reports that two things were checked and already agreed | Apply the deletion test. Almost always delete outright. |
| **Rejection** | Records a path considered and not taken | Move to the process log. Keep only if the path is genuinely tempting and likely to be re-proposed. |
| **Open question** | Not actually resolved | Escalate. This is the most valuable thing the consolidation finds, because it was hiding in a list labelled "resolutions". |

3. **Merge duplicates by taking the strictest version, not the newest.** Newness is not evidence of intent. Strictness is at least a safe default, and if it is wrong someone will object, which is the outcome you want.
4. **Fold in what belongs in the body.** Fold in, then delete. Never delete first.
5. **Delete outright.** Confirmations that survive the test, duplicates that have been merged, and entries about the document's own process rather than about the work.
6. **Renumber nothing.** New locks take fresh numbers at the end of the sequence. Numbers that no longer have entries are left as gaps. A gap is information. A resequenced list is a broken reference.
7. **Produce a disposition report** so the change is visible: for every entry that existed before, state whether it became a lock, was folded into a section, moved to the process log, or was deleted, and why.

## 5.9 TRIGGER TABLE: WHAT FORCES A FULL LOCK AUDIT

| Trigger | Why | Minimum audit |
|---|---|---|
| **Any location, set or environment change** | Root premise. Drives art, lighting, wardrobe, camera, sound, schedule. | Full lock audit plus premise change protocol |
| **Any camera, sensor, format or codec change** | Physical constant. Every derived figure invalid. | Full lock audit plus re-derivation protocol |
| **Any aspect ratio or delivery format change** | Physical constant. Framing, layout, graphics, crops. | Full lock audit plus re-derivation protocol |
| **Any runtime or duration change** | Physical constant. Beats, shots, copy, music, endframe. | Full lock audit plus re-derivation protocol |
| **Any frame rate change** | Physical constant. Exposure, motion, data, conform. | Re-derivation protocol plus schedule and post review |
| **Adding a character, product, brand or entity** | New entity meets blanket rules. Counts change. | Lock audit Steps 3, 4 and 6 |
| **Removing a character, product or entity** | Orphan risk. Counts change. Rules may now over-scope. | Lock audit Steps 1, 2 and 4 |
| **Adding or removing a scene or sequence** | Counts, runtime, beat structure, coverage. | Lock audit Steps 4 and 6 |
| **Any rule being tightened** | Highest-yield failure class. Survivors are certain. | Lock audit Step 5, exhaustively, all five locations |
| **Any rule being loosened** | Low risk, but check deliberate non-application. | Lock audit Steps 1 and 2 |
| **Any colour, palette or reserved-value change** | Cascades into wardrobe, art, grade, graphics, brand compliance. | Full lock audit |
| **Any framing or coverage rule change** | Every shot and board panel is a dependent. | Full lock audit plus shot list rebuild |
| **Adding a deliverable, cutdown or format** | New entity plus stale delivery counts plus new scope questions. | Lock audit Steps 3, 4 and 6 |
| **Any budget or schedule constraint change** | Forces re-derivation of counts that were feasibility-bounded. | Lock audit Step 4 plus feasibility review |

| **Merging two documents or two lists** | Guaranteed duplication and divergence. | Consolidation protocol then full lock audit |
| **Handoff to execution** | Reader changes. History becomes harmful. | Handoff protocol, all three passes |
| **Any prior audit that found exactly one violation** | Evidence the audit was never run. | Full lock audit from Step 1 |
