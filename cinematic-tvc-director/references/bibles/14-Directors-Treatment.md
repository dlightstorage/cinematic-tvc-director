# PART A: CRAFT REFERENCE

---

## A1. WHAT A TREATMENT ACTUALLY IS

A director's treatment is the document a director writes, in response to an agency script or board, that describes the film they intend to make. It is submitted as part of a competitive bid, usually alongside two or three rival directors working from the identical brief, and it is the thing that decides who gets the job.

That is the first-order definition and it is incomplete in a way that causes most treatment failures.

**[PRINCIPLE] A treatment is a specification wearing a sales document's clothes.** It is written to persuade, so it is designed, image-led, confident and short on hedging. But every sentence in it describes something a department will later be asked to physically produce. The moment the treatment wins, it stops being a pitch and becomes the closest thing the production has to a creative contract. Nothing about the format changes at that moment. The reader changes.

This dual nature is the entire subject of this bible. Almost every serious treatment problem is a problem of one nature contaminating the other: a document written purely to win, which then cannot be executed, or a document written purely to be executable, which does not win.

---

## A2. THREE READERS, THREE NEEDS, ONE DOCUMENT

A treatment is normally read by three constituencies with genuinely different questions, and it is read by all three from the same PDF.

| Reader | The question they are actually asking | What convinces them | What loses them |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Agency creative team** (CD, copywriter, art director, agency producer) | "Is this still our idea, and is it better than we wrote it?" | Evidence the director understood the strategic point, not just the visual opportunity. One interpretive move they did not think of. | A director who rewrote the idea into a different idea. A director who ignored the line the whole campaign hangs on. |
| **Client / brand** | "Is this safe, on brand, and will it work for us?" | Clarity, product handling, tonal fit with the brand, a sense that this person is not a risk | Ambiguity. References that look like a competitor's advertising. Anything they cannot picture. |
| **Production company and crew** (EP, line producer, then every department head) | "What exactly am I being asked to build, light, dress, cast and cut?" | Specificity. Numbers. Named locations, named formats, named finishes | Poetry with no underlying decision. A page of atmosphere that no department can act on. |

**[PRINCIPLE]** These three needs are in tension, and the tension is structural rather than solvable. Specificity that reassures a producer can read as rigidity to a creative team. Evocation that thrills a creative team can read as vagueness to a client and as an unanswerable question to a gaffer. The craft of treatment writing is the ordering and layering that lets one document serve all three: emotional argument at the front, interpretive logic in the middle, executional specificity at the back, with the specificity always present but never leading.

A fourth reader is often forgotten and matters enormously: **the person who was not in the room**. Treatments circulate. Regional marketing teams, legal, brand guardians and procurement all read the deck without ever hearing the director speak. **[PRINCIPLE] A treatment must be able to survive being read silently by a stranger.** Anything that only works when the director is present to explain it is not in the treatment yet.

## A3. NARRATIVE FILM TREATMENT VERSUS COMMERCIAL DIRECTOR'S TREATMENT

These share a name and almost nothing else. Confusing them produces a document that fails at both jobs.

The narrative film treatment is a **writer's** document. It is prose, typically the step between scene cards and the first draft of a screenplay, longer and more detailed than a one-page synopsis, written in the present tense and describing events as they happen. Draft treatments run roughly thirty to eighty pages, with the draft treatment of *The Terminator* running forty-eight pages. The shorter "presentation treatment" used as selling material runs roughly seven to twelve pages, and treatments in this tradition are used across the motion picture industry as selling documents to outline story and character for a screenplay that may not yet exist. **[SOURCED: standard industry definition as summarised in the film treatment entry drawing on The American Film Institute Desk Reference, 2002]**

The commercial director's treatment is a **director's** document. The story already exists. The agency wrote it, the client approved it, and it is usually thirty or sixty seconds long. Nothing in the treatment is proposing what happens. Everything in the treatment is proposing *how it will look, sound, feel, be cast, be shot, be cut and be finished*.

| | Narrative film treatment | Commercial director's treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Author** | Writer, or writer-director | Director, usually with a producer and often a treatment writer and designer |
| **Purpose** | Sell a story that does not yet have a script | Win a job where the script already exists and is approved |
| **Owns** | Plot, character, structure, theme | Interpretation, tone, casting, visual language, sound, post, deliverables |
| **Competes against** | Other projects for a slot and a budget | Two or three other directors on the identical script |
| **Form** | Prose, present tense, minimal or no imagery | Designed deck or long-form PDF, heavily image-led |
| **Length** | Seven to eighty pages of text | Typically twelve to thirty-five pages of mostly image |
| **After approval** | Becomes a screenplay | Becomes the production bible's parent document and the crew's brief |
| **Fatal error** | Story does not work | Story was changed |

**[PRINCIPLE] The single most important inherited-habit failure is a director writing a narrative treatment for a commercial brief:** retelling the agency's script back to them in evocative prose, adding character backstory nobody asked for, and never once saying what lens, what light, what location, what cut. The agency already knows the story. They wrote it. What they do not know is what it will feel like when it exists, and that is the only question the treatment is being paid to answer.

## A4. THE STANDARD SECTIONS AND WHAT EACH MUST ACCOMPLISH

The conventional contents of a commercial treatment are well established across the industry: director's vision and references, narrative or scene flow, casting direction, location and production design, lighting and camera and movement approach, wardrobe and props, post approach covering edit, sound and colour, and deliverables including formats, durations and variants. **[SOURCED: standard commercial treatment structure as documented across industry guides and the widely used slide-by-slide commercial treatment template convention]**

What follows is that structure with the *job* of each section made explicit, because a section that is present but does no work is worse than an absent one. It occupies the reader's attention and returns nothing.

### 1. Opening statement, or "why this film"

**Job:** establish in under 150 words that the director understood the brief at the level of feeling, and stake one point of view.

This is the section that most often decides the outcome, because it is the only section every reader definitely reads. It is not a summary of the script. It is the answer to "what is this film really about," expressed in a way the agency will recognise as their own intention and be slightly surprised by.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The reliable structure is: name the human truth under the brief, name the trap most executions of it fall into, then name the one move this film will make instead. Three moves, one paragraph each, no images. A director who cannot do this in one page has not found the film yet, and everything downstream will be decoration.

### 2. The idea, or the interpretation

**Job:** convert the point of view into a governing rule the whole film obeys.

This is where the director declares the organising principle: the film is one continuous shot; the film never shows a face until the last frame; the film is entirely handheld until the product arrives and then locks off; the film is a series of tableaux that only move when the voice moves. **[PRINCIPLE] A treatment with no governing rule is a mood board with headings.** The rule is what makes the rest of the document coherent instead of merely pretty, and it is the thing a department can check its own work against later.

### 3. Tone and world

**Job:** define the emotional register and the reality the film lives inside.

The distinction that matters: *tone* is how the film feels to watch (warm, dry, breathless, ceremonial, deadpan). *World* is what is true inside it (is this our world, a heightened version of it, a fable, an ad that knows it is an ad). Clients confuse the two constantly. A treatment that separates them prevents a category of note that otherwise arrives in the edit.

### 4. Characters and casting

**Job:** describe who is in the film precisely enough that a casting director can brief a session and a client can picture the person.

Real casting direction names behaviour and quality, not demographics. "Late thirties, warm, has clearly not slept, the kind of person who apologises to furniture" is a brief. "Female, 35 to 40, aspirational" is a box to be filled by a stock photograph. **[PRINCIPLE]** Where the film uses real people rather than actors, the treatment must say so explicitly and say how they will be found, because that single sentence changes casting budget, shoot ratio, schedule and legal clearance downstream.

### 5. Scene-by-scene walkthrough

**Job:** take the reader through the film in order and make them see it.

This is the spine and usually the largest section. Each beat gets its own page or spread: what happens, what the camera is doing, what the frame contains, what we hear, and what the beat is *for*. **[PRINCIPLE] The discipline that separates a professional walkthrough from an amateur one is stating purpose per beat.** Any two consecutive beats serving the same purpose is a redundancy the director should find in the treatment rather than in the edit.

### 6. Visual language and cinematography

**Job:** state camera, format, lens philosophy, movement grammar and lighting approach as decisions, not options.

Not a gear list. A logic. Why this format, why this focal range, why the camera moves when it moves and stops when it stops, where the light comes from and what it is pretending to be. A treatment that says "we will shoot on Alexa with Cooke lenses" has said nothing. A treatment that says "long lenses throughout so we are always slightly further away than we want to be, and the film never gives us the close-up until she gives it to herself" has stated a rule that the DP, editor and colourist can all serve.

### 7. Colour and grade

**Job:** define the palette and the finish, and separate what is achieved in camera from what is achieved in post.

**[PRINCIPLE]** This separation is load-bearing. If the treatment shows a grade look, production design and costume will build toward it and the colourist will be asked to add it on top, and the film will overshoot the target. The treatment should say which of the two paths each element takes.

### 8. Wardrobe and production design

**Job:** describe the material world with enough specificity that a designer can cost it and a client can approve it.

Texture, period, condition, palette, and crucially the *level of styling*. "Clothes that look owned rather than bought" is a brief. It also implies an ageing and breakdown budget, which is the point.

### 9. Music and sound

**Job:** commit to a sonic approach and be honest about licensing.

Three separate declarations that get collapsed and should not be: **music** (original score, licensed track, library, or none), **sound design** (what the world sounds like, whether it is naturalistic or constructed), and **voice** (voiceover, dialogue, sync, silence). **[PRINCIPLE]** A named famous track in a treatment is the single most reliable way to create an expectation the budget cannot buy. If it is a temp reference, the treatment must say the word "reference" next to it.

### 10. Editing and pace

**Job:** describe the rhythm and, in a timed format, prove the film fits.

Cut count, average shot length, where the film breathes, where it accelerates, and what the last five seconds do. In a thirty-second commercial this section is also arithmetic. **[PRINCIPLE] A treatment that describes eleven beats plus a five second endframe in a thirty second film has already failed and the failure is checkable before submission.**

### 11. The ending

**Job:** land the film and hand over to the brand cleanly.

Often the weakest section because directors find endframes and logo resolutions uninteresting. Clients do not. **[PRINCIPLE]** The ending is where the agency's line, the client's product, the legal supers and the director's film all have to occupy the same few seconds, and a treatment that treats it as an afterthought signals that the director will treat it as an afterthought on the day.

### 12. Technical and logistical notes

**Job:** tell the producer the truth.

Shoot days, location versus studio, territory, unit count, VFX and CG scope, drone or specialty rigs, stunts, animals, minors, water, weather dependency, aspect ratios and deliverable variants. **[PRINCIPLE] This section is what makes the treatment costable.** Only after treatment approval can a producer calculate an accurate budget, because until then nobody knows what is actually being made. **[SOURCED: Dima Svichkar, "From Vision to Final Render," 2023]** A treatment that omits it is asking the production company to bid on a guess.

## A5. REFERENCE IMAGERY, AND THE TRAP INSIDE IT

Reference imagery is the load-bearing structure of the modern treatment. It is also the most frequently mishandled element in the document.

**[PRINCIPLE] There are two functionally different kinds of reference and they must never be presented the same way.**

| | **Technical reference** | **Mood reference** |
|---|---|---|
| **Says** | "This exact thing. This lens compression, this contrast ratio, this skin rendering, this grain" | "Something in the region of this feeling" |
| **Read by** | DP, colourist, designer, costume | Client, agency, everyone |
| **Risk if misread** | Under-delivery against a named technical target | Client believes they were promised a specific image |
| **Required caption** | The specific attribute being referenced, isolated | An explicit statement of what is *not* being referenced |

The failure mode is universal and it has a name in practice: **the reference read more literally than the director intended**. A director includes a still because of the way light falls across a wall. The client reads the whole frame: the actress, the room, the era, the wardrobe, the colour of the sofa. Six weeks later, in a costume fitting or on a set visit, someone says "it doesn't look like the reference." They are right, and the treatment is the reason.

**[PRINCIPLE] The defence is caption discipline.** Every reference carries a caption naming the single attribute being borrowed, and where necessary naming what is not being borrowed. "For the quality of the window light only. Not the period, not the wardrobe, not the palette." This looks pedantic in a pitch document and it prevents a specific, expensive, recurring argument.

Two further reference hazards worth naming:

- **Competitor contamination.** A reference lifted from a film made for a competing brand will be recognised by the client, and it will read as a plan to make their competitor's ad. Reference sources should be checked against the client's own category.
- **The unachievable reference.** Referencing a frame from a feature film shot over three days with a hundred-person crew, inside a treatment for a one-day commercial, sets a target the budget cannot reach. **[PRINCIPLE]** Every reference should pass a simple test: can this attribute be achieved on the days and money implied by this treatment. If not, either the reference goes or the schedule changes.

## A6. VOICE AND REGISTER: WHY IT IS WRITTEN IN FIRST PERSON

A treatment is written in the director's voice, in the first person, in the present or near-future tense: "I want to open on...", "We stay with her longer than is comfortable...", "The camera never leaves the room."

This is not a stylistic preference. It is functional.

**[PRINCIPLE] The client is not buying a plan. They are buying a person's judgement for the duration of a production.** They will hand over a significant budget to someone they cannot supervise, to make hundreds of decisions in a day that nobody in the room will be qualified to second-guess. The treatment is the only evidence of how that person thinks. Register is therefore part of the product.

Three consequences follow:

1. **Conviction, not menu.** A treatment that offers options ("we could go handheld, or we could shoot this locked off, depending on preference") reads as a director who has not decided. Options are what a client asks for when they do not trust the director. Providing them pre-emptively signals that the director does not trust themselves. **[PRINCIPLE] Where a genuine alternative exists, it belongs in a clearly separated "options" appendix, framed as a costed variant, not as creative indecision.**

2. **Specificity is the proof of conviction.** Vague confidence reads as bluffing. "It will be beautiful and emotional" is a claim anyone can make. "We shoot at the last twenty minutes of light, which gives us one take per setup and means the whole film is made of first takes" is a claim only someone who has decided can make.

3. **The voice must be the director's actual voice.** Because the same person has to walk into a pre-production meeting and sound like the document. **[PRINCIPLE]** A treatment ghostwritten in a register the director cannot sustain in a room creates a credibility gap that surfaces at exactly the wrong moment, in the PPM, in front of the client.

A note on humility: conviction is not arrogance, and treatments that attack the script or lecture the agency lose. The agency is being asked to hand their idea to this person. **[PRINCIPLE] The correct emotional posture is: I love this idea, I understand why it works, and here is the one thing I will add that you cannot get from anyone else.**

## A7. WHAT SEPARATES A WINNING TREATMENT FROM A LOSING ONE

Judged by the people who actually award the jobs, agency creative directors and agency producers, the differentiators cluster consistently.

**Wins:**

- **One idea, stated early and held.** The winning treatment usually has a single interpretive move that is visible on page two and still governing on page twenty.
- **Evidence of reading the strategy, not just the script.** Referencing what the campaign is trying to achieve, not only what happens in the film.
- **Solving the agency's known problem.** Every board has a weak beat that the agency privately worries about. A director who identifies it and solves it wins on that alone.
- **Answering the practical questions before they are asked.** Where, how many days, how the product is handled, how the versions are made.
- **Casting that feels like a real point of view.** Casting is the single most visible director decision in a finished commercial and the one clients feel most.
- **Being obviously executable.** The producer in the room is scoring feasibility while the creatives score ambition, and the producer's veto is quieter but real.

**Loses:**

- **Restating the script back.** A treatment that describes what happens without describing how it will feel says the director had nothing to add.
- **Generic craft language.** "Beautiful natural light, authentic performances, cinematic feel." Present in every losing treatment and no winning one.
- **A different idea.** Directors who "improve" the board into something else are not bold, they have breached the brief.
- **Reference decks with no argument.** Forty images and eight hundred words means the images are doing work the director should have done.
- **Length as a substitute for clarity.** A long treatment that could have been short reads as uncertainty.
- **Anything the client cannot picture.** Ambiguity that excites an agency terrifies a brand.

**[PRINCIPLE] The blunt version:** a treatment wins on interpretation and loses on either vagueness or infeasibility. Interpretation is what only this director offers. Vagueness and infeasibility are the two ways of being disqualified regardless of how good the interpretation was.

## A8. THE SECOND LIFE: WHEN THE PITCH BECOMES THE BRIEF

The treatment wins. Within days it is forwarded to a production company, and from there to a DP, a designer, a costume designer, a casting director, an editor, a colourist and a composer. In most commercial productions **the treatment is the first and sometimes only creative document any of those people receive.**

**[PRINCIPLE] From the moment of the award, every claim in the treatment becomes a work order.** Departments do not read a treatment as persuasion. They read it as instruction, and they are correct to, because it is the document the client approved and the document the client will measure the finished film against.

This produces the defining hazard of the form: **the evocative line read as a commitment.**

| Written in the treatment to feel good | Read by a department as | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| "A city that never quite stops raining" | Rain towers on every exterior setup | Special effects unit, extended setups, wet-down continuity, costume doubles |
| "Shot entirely at golden hour" | Two usable hours per day | Shoot day count doubles or the schedule breaks |
| "Faces we have never seen before" | Street casting, not agency casting | Casting timeline extends by weeks, usage and clearance risk rises |
| "The world is entirely handmade, nothing digital" | No VFX cleanup, no wire removal, practical builds only | Art department build budget, and post cannot fix mistakes |
| "A single unbroken take" | Oner, no cutting points, rehearsal-heavy day | Loss of all editorial safety, one failure kills the day |
| "Music is a track everyone knows" | Licensed master and publishing | Six figure licence, or a client conversation nobody planned for |

None of those lines is wrong. Each is a legitimate creative decision. The failure is writing them *without knowing they are decisions*.

**[PRINCIPLE] The working rule: before any sentence goes into a treatment, the writer must be able to name the department that will execute it and the resource it will consume.** If neither can be named, the sentence is atmosphere and should be marked as such or removed. A treatment is the last cheap place to discover that an intention cannot be shot. Every subsequent discovery point costs money.

There is a converse hazard, less discussed and equally real: **the treatment that under-promises.** A director who writes defensively, committing to nothing, wins nothing, and if they do win they have given the crew no rules to work from and the client no agreed standard. Departments then invent their own interpretation and the film arrives as five departments' separate opinions.

## A9. NAMED PRACTITIONERS, COMPANIES AND REAL PRACTICE

The treatment is a competitive artifact of the production company system, and that system has recognisable institutions.

| Entity | Relevance to the treatment as a document |
|---|---|
| **RSA Films** (founded by Ridley Scott, a director whose own storyboarding practice is the industry's reference case for pre-visualised intent) | The lineage of commercial directing as a design discipline where the image is specified before it is shot |
| **Somesuch** (UK, home of directors including Kim Gehrig and Daniel Wolfe) | Reputation built on authored, point-of-view driven work, which is precisely what a treatment's opening statement has to demonstrate |
| **Biscuit Filmworks, MJZ, Smuggler, Academy Films, Rattling Stick, Hungry Man, Stink, Iconoclast, Caviar, Pulse Films** | The competitive set. On any significant brief, several of these companies are bidding the same script simultaneously, each with a treatment |
| **Kim Gehrig**, Somesuch director and 2023 Cannes Lions Film Craft Jury President, who has said "As a director, I am only as good as my collaborators... It's the alchemy of these people and their crafts that elevates a piece of film" **[SOURCED: Cannes Lions announcement, 23 February 2023]** | Directly relevant: a treatment is the instrument by which collaborators are given something coherent to be alchemical about |
| **AICP** (Association of Independent Commercial Producers, US) and the **APA** (Advertising Producers Association, UK) | The trade bodies whose bid and production standards sit downstream of the treatment. The AICP bid form is the structure into which a treatment's promises get priced |
| **Cannes Lions Film Craft** | Judges "the translation of the creative brief through a director's vision and how well that vision has been achieved" **[SOURCED: Cannes Lions Film Craft category definitions, canneslions.com]**, which is a description of a treatment successfully executed |
| **Genero** and similar bid platforms | Formalised treatment submission for a wider pool of directors, which standardised the format and raised expectations of design quality **[SOURCED: Dima Svichkar's published account of pitching through Genero, 2023]** |
| **Free The Work** (founded by director Alma Har'el, originally as Free The Bid) | Directly concerned with who gets invited to submit treatments at all, which is the access question sitting underneath the whole artifact |

**[PRINCIPLE]** The uncomfortable fact of the format: on a three-way pitch, two directors write a complete treatment for nothing. Unpaid or under-paid pitching is a live structural argument in the industry, raised repeatedly through trade press including Little Black Book, shots and Ad Age, and through the trade bodies. The practical consequence for anyone writing treatments is economic: **treatment production has to be efficient, because most of it is written for jobs that will not be won.**

## A10. TREATMENT WRITING AS AN INDUSTRY

**[PRINCIPLE]** The commercial treatment has generated its own professional layer, and pretending otherwise leads to misunderstanding how the documents that win are actually made.

Three distinct roles now exist around it:

1. **The treatment writer.** A specialist, often a freelancer, who interviews the director, extracts the intention, and writes the prose in the director's voice. The director's ideas, the writer's articulation. The output is judged by whether the director sounds like themselves.
2. **The treatment designer.** A graphic designer or design studio who builds the layout, typography, image research, grids and the final PDF or deck. Design quality became a competitive variable once every submission was a designed document, and it has continued to escalate.
3. **The producer or EP.** Who supplies the feasibility and logistics sections, keeps promises inside the budget, and vetoes anything the company cannot deliver.

**[PRINCIPLE] This layer creates two risks worth naming.** First, an arms race in production values, where treatments become more expensive to produce than the fee for producing them, which pushes cost onto directors and companies for jobs they mostly will not win. Second, and more serious for the craft, **voice laundering**: a well-written treatment can make a director sound like a thinker they are not, and the gap shows up at the pre-production meeting when the client asks a follow-up question the document answered and the director cannot.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The defence against the second risk is the same as the defence against most treatment problems: the director must be able to defend every sentence without the document in front of them. Anything they cannot defend from memory was written by someone else about a film that does not exist.

## A11. FORMATS, LENGTH AND DELIVERY CONVENTIONS

| Element | Convention | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **File format** | PDF, landscape, 16:9 | Universally readable, layout locked, no font substitution. Interactive web treatments exist and are still the minority |
| **Page count** | Roughly 12 to 35 pages for a standard commercial | Below 10 reads as thin. Above 40 reads as padding. The walkthrough should be the longest section |
| **Word count** | Roughly 1,200 to 3,000 words of actual prose across the whole document | The rest is image. A treatment is read in fifteen minutes |
| **File size** | Keep under roughly 20 MB | It has to arrive by email and be opened on a phone. A treatment that will not download does not get read |
| **Orientation and layout** | Full-bleed imagery, minimal text per page, generous white space, one idea per spread | It is read on a laptop in a meeting, projected in a room, and scrolled on a phone. All three must work |
| **Typography** | One or two typefaces, large enough to read projected, high contrast against imagery | Text laid over a busy image at 10pt is the most common design failure |
| **Cover** | Brand, campaign or film title, director name, production company, date | Version control starts here |
| **Naming** | Project, director, version, date | Treatments get forwarded and renamed. An unversioned file circulating is how two versions end up in one approval chain |
| **Video** | Reference films as links, not embeds | Embedded video breaks the file size and often the reader's PDF viewer |
| **Accompanying materials** | Director's reel or relevant credits, sometimes a separate lookbook or casting deck | Kept separate so the treatment itself stays light |

**[PRINCIPLE]** A single hard convention worth obeying: the treatment must be readable on a phone screen with no zooming. Decisions on it are frequently made by someone reading it in transit.

## A12. VOCABULARY GLOSSARY

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Board** | The agency's approved storyboard or script. In a commercial it functions as the contract. The director interprets it, does not replace it |
| **Triple bid / three-way** | Three directors from three production companies bidding the same board. The normal competitive structure |
| **Bid** | The production company's costed proposal. The treatment is the creative half, the AICP or APA bid form is the financial half |
| **Awarded** | The job has been given to a director. The moment the treatment changes function from pitch to brief |
| **PPM (pre-production meeting)** | The formal meeting where the client and agency approve casting, wardrobe, locations, props and schedule. The treatment is the reference document against which every PPM decision is checked |
| **Endframe** | The final frames carrying logo, line and legal. Governed by brand guidelines, usually non-negotiable |
| **Supers** | On-screen text and legal disclaimers. Occupy time and screen real estate and must be planned in the treatment's timing |
| **Oner** | A single unbroken take covering an entire scene or film. A major treatment commitment with schedule and risk consequences |
| **Lookbook** | Image-led companion document focused purely on visual reference, sometimes separated from the treatment |
| **Mood board** | Loose collection of tonal images. A treatment is not one, and the confusion is common |
| **Governing rule** | The single formal constraint the film obeys throughout. The thing that makes a treatment coherent |
| **Technical reference** | An image cited for a specific, isolatable technical attribute |
| **Mood reference** | An image cited for feeling only, with no technical claim |
| **Temp track** | A reference music track used to communicate intent, not licensed. Must be labelled as such |
| **Deliverables** | The full list of cuts, durations, aspect ratios and versions that must be produced |
| **Versioning / cutdowns** | Shorter or market-specific edits derived from the master. Their existence changes coverage requirements and must appear in the treatment |
| **Production bible** | The internal document set that carries the executable detail: shot list, cast, locations, wardrobe, camera and lens plan, palette, schedule. The treatment's parallel document |
| **Treatment writer** | Specialist who articulates the director's intent in the director's voice |
| **Treatment designer** | Specialist who lays out and produces the document |
| **PPM book** | The deck presented at the pre-production meeting, effectively the treatment's promises converted into approved specifics |

# PART B: AGENT OPERATING MANUAL

*For an AI subagent writing, revising or auditing a director's treatment inside a multi-department pipeline.*

---

## B1. THE INTAKE: QUESTIONS TO ASK BEFORE WRITING A WORD

Do not begin drafting until these are answered or explicitly marked unknown. **[PRINCIPLE] A treatment written on assumptions produces promises the production cannot honour, and the assumptions are invisible in the finished document.**

### Block 1: The competitive situation

1. Is this a competitive pitch or an awarded job? If competitive, how many directors and are they known?
2. Who decides? Agency creative director, agency producer, client marketing lead, or a committee?
3. Is there a pitch fee, and is there a deadline?
4. Has any director already been shown to the client, formally or informally?

### Block 2: The brief itself

5. What is the approved board or script, verbatim? Is it locked or still moving?
6. What is the campaign strategy behind the film, not just the film?
7. What is the media context: where does this run, at what length, in what aspect ratios, in which markets?
8. What is the endframe, the line, and the legal supers, and how many seconds do they consume?
9. What is explicitly off-limits? Brand guidelines, category regulation, competitor territory, prior campaigns.
10. Which beat of the board is the agency privately unsure about?

### Block 3: Constraints that govern feasibility

11. What is the production budget range, and does it include or exclude post, music, talent and usage?
12. How many shoot days, and are they fixed?
13. What territory, and are there travel, permit, weather or seasonal constraints?
14. Cast: professional actors, real people, talent already attached, or a celebrity?
15. Product: hero product available, in what state, and does it require a specialist product unit?
16. Music: is there an existing brand track, a budget for licensing, or must it be original?
17. Post: is the edit, VFX and grade in-house, at named vendors, or open?
18. What are the full deliverables: master, cutdowns, social crops, market versions?

### Block 4: The director

19. What is the director's actual point of view on this brief, in their own words?
20. What is the one thing they will do that no other director bidding will do?
21. What are they *not* willing to do, so it never enters the document?
22. What in their existing reel supports the claim they are making here?

**[PRINCIPLE] If question 20 has no answer, stop.** There is no treatment yet. There is a formatting exercise.

## B2. SECTION-BY-SECTION OUTPUT TEMPLATE

Write in first person, present or near-future tense. No dashes as punctuation. Every executional claim must be traceable to an answer in B1 or to a decision recorded in the production bible.

---

### 0. COVER
Film or campaign title. Director name. Production company. Date. Version number.
*Guidance: version number is not optional. Treatments circulate and get renamed by other people.*

---

### 1. WHY THIS FILM
**Target: 100 to 200 words. No images.**

Three moves: the human truth under the brief, the trap most executions fall into, the one move this film makes instead.

*Guidance: this is the only section guaranteed to be read by everyone. Write it last, after the walkthrough has taught you what the film actually is. Do not summarise the script. Do not compliment the agency. State a point of view that could be disagreed with.*

---

### 2. THE IDEA
**Target: 150 to 300 words plus 1 to 3 images.**

State the governing rule. One sentence, italicised or pulled out. Then explain what obeying it produces and what it costs.

*Guidance: the test of a real governing rule is that it forbids something. A rule that permits everything is a mood, not a rule. Name what this rule rules out.*

---

### 3. TONE AND WORLD
**Target: 150 to 250 words plus 3 to 6 images.**

Two labelled subsections. **Tone:** how it feels to watch. **World:** what is true inside it.

*Guidance: give three to five adjectives maximum and then immediately qualify each with a behaviour. "Warm, but never sentimental: nobody in this film cries." Adjectives alone are unbriefable.*

---

### 4. CHARACTERS AND CASTING
**Target: one page per significant character.**

Per character: who they are, behaviour and quality, what we believe about them within two seconds, and how they will be found (agency casting, street casting, real people, specialist).

*Guidance: never write demographics as a substitute for character. Always state professional versus real person explicitly, because that single word changes casting budget, shoot ratio and clearance. Cross-check against the casting brief in the production bible.*

---

### 5. THE FILM, BEAT BY BEAT
**Target: the largest section. One page or spread per beat.**

Per beat, five fields:
- **What happens** (from the board, unchanged)
- **Camera** (position, lens character, movement or stillness)
- **Frame** (what is in it, what is deliberately not)
- **Sound** (what we hear, including silence)
- **Purpose** (what this beat is for)

*Guidance: if two consecutive beats have the same Purpose, one is redundant and should be cut here rather than discovered in the edit. If the total beat count multiplied by realistic screen time exceeds the film's duration minus the endframe, the film does not fit and must be reduced now. Every beat here must correspond to an entry in the shot list.*

---

### 6. VISUAL LANGUAGE
**Target: 200 to 400 words plus 4 to 8 captioned references.**

Format and capture medium. Lens philosophy and why. Movement grammar: when the camera moves, when it does not, and what motivates it. Lighting approach: source logic, contrast, what the light is pretending to be.

*Guidance: state decisions, never options. Every reference here is a technical reference and must carry a caption naming the isolated attribute. Cross-check camera, lens set and format against the camera plan in the production bible.*

---

### 7. COLOUR AND GRADE
**Target: 150 to 250 words plus a palette strip and 3 to 5 references.**

The palette. What is achieved in camera versus what is achieved in the grade. Contrast and density. Skin rendering. Any deliberate deviation across the film.

*Guidance: always separate in-camera from post. Failing to separate them causes double-application, where design and costume build toward a look the colourist then adds again. Cross-check the palette against production design and costume in the production bible.*

---

### 8. WARDROBE AND PRODUCTION DESIGN
**Target: 200 to 350 words plus references per character or location.**

Material world, texture, period, condition, level of styling. Locations: type, scale, whether real, built or found. Props and product handling.

*Guidance: "condition" is the costable word. Clothes that look owned imply ageing and breakdown. Say it, because it is a budget line.*

---

### 9. MUSIC AND SOUND
**Target: 150 to 300 words.**

Three labelled declarations. **Music:** original, licensed, library or none, with the intended emotional function. **Sound design:** naturalistic or constructed, and what the world sounds like. **Voice:** voiceover, dialogue, sync or silence, and the casting quality if voiced.

*Guidance: any named track must be captioned "reference only" unless it is confirmed licensable within budget. This is the highest-frequency false promise in the format.*

---

### 10. EDIT AND PACE
**Target: 120 to 250 words.**

Rhythm and cut density. Where the film breathes and where it accelerates. Approximate cut count. What the final five seconds do.

*Guidance: in any timed format this section is arithmetic and must be checked against the beat count in section 5 and the durations in the shot list.*

---

### 11. THE ENDING
**Target: 100 to 200 words plus an endframe visual.**

How the film resolves into the brand. Endframe treatment, line placement, supers, and how much time each consumes.

*Guidance: treat this with the same seriousness as the opening. It is where the client's non-negotiables live and where an otherwise strong treatment signals carelessness.*

---

### 12. PRODUCTION AND TECHNICAL
**Target: a table, not prose.**

Shoot days. Location versus studio. Territory. Units. Cast count. VFX and CG scope. Specialty rigs, drone, stunts, animals, minors, water, weather dependency. Aspect ratios. Full deliverables list. Known risks and their mitigations.

*Guidance: this is what makes the treatment costable, and it is the section a producer reads first. Every entry must match the production bible exactly. Where something is unknown, write "to be confirmed" rather than omitting it, because omission reads as zero.*

---

### 13. OPTIONS APPENDIX (only if genuinely required)
Costed creative variants, clearly labelled as variants.

*Guidance: never put alternatives in the body of the treatment. Options in the body read as indecision. Options in a labelled appendix read as producer-mindedness.*

---

## B3. THE INTERNAL-CONSISTENCY REQUIREMENT

**[PRINCIPLE] This is the single most important operating rule in Part B.**

A treatment and its production bible are **parallel documents describing the same set of facts from different angles**. The treatment describes those facts persuasively and emotionally, for a client. The production bible describes the identical facts operationally and numerically, for a crew. They are not a summary and an expansion. They are two projections of one dataset.

Two rules follow, and both directions must hold:

1. **Every creative claim in the treatment must be traceable to a decision that exists in the production bible.** If the treatment says the film is shot on long lenses, the camera plan must specify a long lens set. If the treatment says a rain-soaked city, the schedule must carry rain units. A treatment claim with no corresponding production decision is an unfunded promise, and someone will be asked to deliver it anyway.

2. **Every material decision in the production bible must be consistent with, or at minimum not contradicted by, the treatment.** If the shot list contains a crane move, the treatment cannot describe a film that never leaves the actor's eyeline. If casting has booked a professional actor, the treatment cannot promise real people.

**[PRINCIPLE] These two documents drift out of sync exactly the way duplicated content drifts in any system.** The same fact is stored in two places, in two formats, maintained by two different people for two different audiences, and every change updates one of them. The drift is silent, because neither document is wrong on its own terms. It only becomes visible when a department executes the treatment and the producer scheduled the bible, or when a client compares the finished film to the document they approved.

**The operating rule: a treatment and its production bible are updated in the same pass or neither is updated.** A change to one without the other is not a partial update. It is a defect introduced into both.

## B4. CROSS-DOCUMENT SYNC CHECKLIST

Run this after **any** change to either document. These are the fields that exist in both and are therefore capable of diverging.

| # | Field | In the treatment as | In the production bible as | Check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Cast: count** | Characters described in section 4 | Cast list, call sheet, casting brief | Same number of speaking and featured roles. Background counts implied by the treatment's crowd descriptions are budgeted |
| 2 | **Cast: type** | "Real people" / "actors" | Casting method, agency versus street, usage terms | Professional versus non-professional matches. Usage and territory match media plan |
| 3 | **Cast: character quality** | Behavioural description | Casting brief adjectives and audition sides | The written character can actually be cast from the brief given |
| 4 | **Wardrobe** | Palette, texture, period, condition | Costume breakdown per character per scene, ageing and breakdown notes | Every treatment wardrobe adjective has a costume line. "Condition" language is budgeted |
| 5 | **Locations** | Described type and mood | Named or scouted locations, permits, unit moves | Location type matches. Number of distinct locations matches the unit move count in the schedule |
| 6 | **Camera body and format** | Named in section 6 | Camera package, recording format, resolution, frame rates | Identical. Any high-speed implied by the treatment appears in the package |
| 7 | **Lenses** | Lens philosophy and focal character | Lens set and per-shot focal lengths | The stated philosophy is achievable with the booked set. Long-lens language is not contradicted by wide-lens shots |
| 8 | **Movement** | Movement grammar | Grip package, dolly, crane, gimbal, drone | Every movement type described has equipment booked and time scheduled |
| 9 | **Lighting** | Source logic and contrast | Lighting plan, gaffer's package, power | Golden-hour, night, or heavy-source claims have matching schedule and package |
| 10 | **Palette** | Colour statement and strip | Design palette, costume palette, grade LUT or reference | One palette across all three. In-camera versus post split is consistent |

| 13 | **Timings** | Beat count, cut count, pace | Shot durations, running time, edit assembly target | Total durations fit the format minus endframe and supers |
| 14 | **Endframe and supers** | Described ending | Endframe asset, legal copy, super durations | Seconds consumed match the timing calculation |
| 15 | **Music** | Named approach and any reference track | Music brief, licence status, composer or library agreement | Reference tracks flagged as reference. Licensed tracks confirmed and budgeted |
| 16 | **Sound** | Sonic world | Sound design brief, sync recording plan, foley scope | Constructed soundscapes have post time booked |
| 17 | **VFX and CG** | Any implied invisible or visible effects | VFX breakdown, vendor, shot count | Every implied effect appears in the breakdown. "Nothing digital" claims are consistent with cleanup needs |
| 18 | **Product handling** | How product appears | Product unit, macro package, specialist, product supply | Hero product shots have a plan |
| 19 | **Shoot days and units** | Stated in section 12 | Schedule, call sheets | Identical numbers |
| 20 | **Deliverables** | Listed in section 12 | Post schedule, versioning plan, aspect ratio matrix | Every deliverable has coverage planned. Vertical crops have been considered in framing |

## B5. FAILURE MODES

### F1. The treatment promises what the shot list cannot deliver
**Signature:** the treatment describes a moment ("we watch her decide, in one unbroken look") that has no corresponding shot, or has a shot allocated fifteen seconds in a thirty second film.
**Cause:** the treatment was written as prose and the shot list was derived later by someone optimising for the day.
**Detection:** run checklist item 12. Every beat must map to a numbered shot.
**Correction:** either add the shot and remove time elsewhere, or amend the treatment. Never leave both standing.

### F2. The treatment was updated, the production bible was not
**Signature:** the client approved a revised treatment. The crew is working from department briefs derived from the previous version. Nobody knows, because both documents are internally coherent.
**Cause:** revision handled as a client-facing task rather than a document-set task.
**Detection:** version numbers. If the treatment is v3 and the bible references v2, stop.
**Correction:** re-run the full B4 checklist against the diff between versions, not against the whole document. Only the changed fields can have introduced drift, but every downstream field touching them must be re-checked.

### F3. The production bible was updated, the treatment was not
**Signature:** a location fell through, a lens set changed, a cast member was recast, and the treatment still describes the original. The client's approved document now describes a film that will not be made.
**Cause:** production changes are handled as operational and not routed back to the creative document.
**Detection:** any change to checklist fields 1 through 20 must trigger a treatment read.
**Correction:** amend the treatment and re-circulate, or issue a written change note referencing the treatment section it supersedes. **[PRINCIPLE] Silent divergence here is the origin of "this is not what we approved" in the edit.**

### F4. An evocative line read as a commitment by a department
**Signature:** a costume designer sourcing a specific era because a reference image contained it. A gaffer scheduling a night unit because the treatment said "we live in the blue hour."
**Cause:** atmosphere written without naming the executing department or the resource consumed.
**Detection:** before submission, pass over every sentence and ask: which department executes this, and what does it consume? Sentences with no answer are atmosphere.
**Correction:** either commit properly (add it to the bible and the budget) or rewrite the line so it cannot be read as a specification. Explicit caption language is the cheapest fix: "for feeling, not for period."

### F5. Reference misread as literal
**Signature:** "it doesn't look like the reference," raised by a client at a fitting, a recce or a grade.
**Cause:** uncaptioned reference imagery.
**Detection:** any reference in the document without a caption naming the isolated attribute.
**Correction:** caption every reference before submission. Retro-fixing after approval requires a client conversation and always costs goodwill.

### F6. The treatment that under-promises
**Signature:** the treatment contains no governing rule, no forbidden thing, and no specific technical decision. Departments each invent their own interpretation.
**Cause:** defensive writing, usually from uncertainty or from fear of committing to something the budget cannot cover.
**Detection:** if the treatment could describe a different brief with three words changed, it is under-specified.
**Correction:** return to B1 question 20.

### F7. Voice laundering
**Signature:** the director cannot answer a follow-up question in the pre-production meeting that the treatment answers on page nine.
**Cause:** the document was written by someone else about a film the director has not fully thought through.
**Detection:** ask the director to state the governing rule and the three most consequential technical decisions from memory.
**Correction:** stop writing and go back to the director. This is not a document problem.

High changes require the agency and every affected department head. Medium and low changes require the affected department heads and a bible update. Nothing is "too small to update," because the cost of divergence is not proportional to the size of the change.

## B7. PRE-SUBMISSION AUDIT

Run before the treatment leaves the building.

1. **The stranger test.** Would someone who never met the director understand what film this is, from the PDF alone, on a phone?
2. **The governing rule test.** Can the rule be stated in one sentence, and does it forbid something?
3. **The arithmetic test.** Beats multiplied by realistic durations, plus endframe and supers, fits the format.
4. **The department test.** Every sentence has a nameable executing department and a nameable resource, or is explicitly marked as atmosphere.
5. **The caption test.** Every reference image has a caption naming the isolated attribute.
6. **The competitor test.** No reference sourced from the client's direct competitors.
7. **The board fidelity test.** The story is still the agency's story. Nothing has been added to or removed from what happens.
8. **The bible parity test.** Full B4 checklist, all twenty items, green.
9. **The defence test.** The director can state the governing rule and the three most consequential technical decisions from memory.
10. **The file test.** PDF, versioned filename, under 20 MB, opens and reads on a phone, no embedded video, no broken links.

**[PRINCIPLE] Items 1, 4 and 8 are the ones that get skipped, and they are the ones that cause the expensive failures.**
