## A1. WHAT A CREATIVE BRIEF ACTUALLY IS

A creative brief is a short written document that converts a business problem into a creative problem. It is the handover artifact between the people who understand the commercial situation and the people who will make something in response to it.

That definition is easy to agree with and easy to fail, because most briefs are written as *summaries of what the client said* rather than as *arguments about what the work must do*. A summary is a transcript with headings. A brief is a decision.

**[PRINCIPLE] The test of a brief is whether it forbids things.** A brief that permits every possible execution has not briefed anything. If two creative teams could read the same brief and produce work with no common property beyond the logo, the brief did no work.

### The six things a good brief contains

| Element | The question it answers | Failure signature when weak |
|---|---|---|
| **The problem** | What is actually wrong, in business terms, that communication can fix? | "Raise awareness." A goal with no diagnosis attached. |
| **The audience** | Who specifically, described as a person and not a demographic bracket? | "Males 25 to 45, ABC1." A media buy description masquerading as a human. |
| **The insight** | What do we know about that person that gives us leverage? | A restated product feature, or a platitude. |
| **The single-minded proposition** | The one thing we want them to take away? | Three things joined by commas. |
| **The non-negotiables** | Mandatories, legal constraints, brand rules, runtime, formats, budget envelope | Discovered late, in the edit, at cost. |
| **The success measure** | How will we know this worked? | Absent, or "engagement." |

**The problem.** A problem is a diagnosis, not a wish. "Sales are flat" is a symptom. "People know us, choose us for one occasion only, and never think of us for the other four occasions in the category" is a problem, and it already implies the shape of the answer. **[PRINCIPLE] A well written problem statement is half a strategy.**

**The audience.** The useful unit is not the segment, it is the person in the moment of decision. Writing an audience well means naming what they currently believe, what they currently do, and what they would have to believe differently for the desired behaviour to happen. That triple (current belief, current behaviour, required belief) is more actionable than any demographic table.

**The single-minded proposition.** The convention of reducing a brief to one proposition is long established in agency practice and is the reason the field is sometimes shorthanded as "single-minded" thinking. **[SOURCED: the single-minded proposition is a standing convention of agency brief formats, present in the classic brief templates used across the industry for decades.]** The discipline is not about simplicity for its own sake. It is about the arithmetic of attention. A viewer gives a film a few seconds of genuine attention and retains, at best, one thing. If the brief carries three things, the work will carry none of them well.

**The non-negotiables.** These are the things that cannot be traded away later without a new conversation: the runtime, the aspect ratios, the legal claim wording, the logo lockup rules, the mandatory endframe, the language versions, the territory restrictions. **[PRINCIPLE] A non-negotiable discovered after concept approval is a rewrite, not a note.** The brief's job is to surface all of them before anyone falls in love with anything.

**The success measure.** A measure keeps the brief honest, because it forces the problem statement to be specific enough to be tested. It also protects the creative work from being judged against a standard nobody stated.

## A2. OBSERVATION, INSIGHT, PROPOSITION: THREE DIFFERENT THINGS

These three words are used interchangeably in most briefs and they are not interchangeable. Confusing them is the single most common cause of a brief that reads well and produces nothing.

| | Observation | Insight | Proposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Something true that can be seen or measured | An explanation of *why* the observation is true, at the level of human motivation | What we will say or promise, given the insight |
| **Source** | Research, data, fieldwork, listening | Interpretation of the observation | Strategic decision by the briefer |
| **Test** | Is it true? | Does it surprise, and does it explain? | Is it single, ownable and actionable? |
| **Owned by** | Anyone. It is public | The planner or strategist | The brief |
| **Failure** | Being mistaken for an insight | Being a platitude | Being a list |

**[PRINCIPLE] An observation describes. An insight explains. A proposition commits.**

### Worked example one: a home appliance category

**Observation.** People spend longer choosing a large appliance than they spend choosing a phone, and they visit the store an average of three times before buying.

That is true, and it is inert. It tells a creative team nothing except that the purchase is considered.

**Insight.** They are not researching the machine. They are rehearsing the argument they will have to make to the person who shares the household, because a wrong choice will be visible in the kitchen for the next ten years and will be blamed on whoever picked it.

That explains the behaviour. It reframes the buyer from "researcher" to "person who fears being blamed." It has leverage, because it names a fear the category never speaks to.

**Proposition.** *Nobody ever regretted this one.*

Notice the chain. The proposition would be arbitrary without the insight. With the insight, it is inevitable. **[PRINCIPLE] A good proposition should feel obvious in hindsight and impossible to derive from the observation alone.**

### Worked example two: a financial service

**Observation.** Sign up rates fall off sharply at the document upload step.

**Weak insight (actually just a restatement).** People find document upload annoying.

That is the observation again, wearing a motivation word. It explains nothing and produces "we made it easy" advertising, which every competitor also says.

**Real insight.** Uploading identity documents is the moment the relationship stops feeling like shopping and starts feeling like being assessed. People do not abandon because it is hard. They abandon because they suddenly feel they might be rejected, and abandoning is cheaper than being refused.

**Proposition.** *You are already approved as a person. This is just paperwork.*

### Worked example three: a food product

**Observation.** Consumption peaks between 3pm and 5pm on weekdays.

**Insight.** The afternoon slump is not a hunger event, it is a dignity event. People want a break they do not have to justify to anyone, and food is the only break at a desk that requires no explanation.

**Proposition.** *The break nobody can question.*

### The three tests

1. **The reversal test for insights.** State the opposite. If the opposite is obviously absurd, you have a platitude, not an insight. "People want quality" reverses to "people want poor quality." Useless. "People abandon rather than risk being refused" reverses to "people would rather be refused than abandon," which is a real, arguable alternative. That means the insight carries information.
2. **The stranger test for observations.** If a competitor's planner would have written the same sentence, it is an observation and it is shared property. Insight is the interpretation layer where advantage lives.
3. **The single-minded test for propositions.** Read it aloud and count the ideas. If you can insert "and also" anywhere, it is not single-minded.

## A3. ACCOUNT PLANNING: THE DISCIPLINE THAT OWNS THE BRIEF

The brief has an owner as a craft, and the craft has a history worth knowing because it explains why briefs are structured the way they are.

**[SOURCED] Account planning as a formal agency discipline emerged in London in 1968, developed in parallel by Stephen King at J. Walter Thompson and Stanley Pollitt at Boase Massimi Pollitt.** The two men approached it from different directions. King came from a research and marketing background and was concerned with building a rigorous framework for what advertising was supposed to do. Pollitt's concern was that research data was being used selectively by account handlers to justify decisions already made, and he argued for a trained person sitting inside the account team whose job was to represent the consumer's actual behaviour honestly. The two strands merged into the role now called planning or strategy.

**[SOURCED] Stephen King is associated with the framework thinking behind the JWT Planning Cycle and its five questions:** where are we, why are we here, where could we be, how could we get there, and are we getting there. **[PRINCIPLE]** Those five questions remain the cleanest available skeleton for a brief, because each one has a different owner and a different evidence type, and skipping any one of them produces a recognisable defect. Skip "why are we here" and you get a brief that treats a distribution problem as a communication problem. Skip "are we getting there" and you get a brief with no success measure.

**[SOURCED] Jon Steel's "Truth, Lies and Advertising: The Art of Account Planning" (1998) is the most widely read single text on the discipline** and is the standard argument for planning as advocacy for the consumer inside the agency rather than as a research function. Steel's central practical contribution for brief writers is the insistence that the planner's job is to make the creative team *want* to work on the brief, which reframes the brief from a specification document into a piece of persuasive writing aimed at an internal audience.

**[SOURCED] Award bodies shape what briefs aspire to.** D&AD, founded in the UK in 1962, judges craft against an absolute standard rather than a relative one, which is why its top awards are not always issued. Cannes Lions, running since 1954, has progressively added categories that require entrants to submit the strategic reasoning alongside the work, which has had the practical effect of making the brief itself a judged artifact. **[PRINCIPLE] The consequence for practitioners is that a written, traceable chain from insight to execution is now a professional expectation, not an internal nicety.**

**[PRINCIPLE] What the planner actually owns.** Not the idea. Not the words. The planner owns the *definition of the problem* and the *evidence that the answer is addressing it*. When a planner starts writing lines, the brief loses its only impartial reader.

## A4. MINING A BRIEF PROPERLY

A rich brief document, a client's own strategy deck, a research report, a category study, a transcript of a stakeholder session: any of these can substitute for a live client question and answer session. But only under one condition, and the condition is almost always violated.

**[PRINCIPLE] The condition is that the document must be mined stage by stage, not skimmed for a usable line.**

Skimming is what happens by default. A writer opens a forty page client document, reads until something sounds like a headline, extracts it, and closes the file. The result looks informed and is not. The extracted line carries none of the constraints, contradictions or measures that surrounded it, and every one of those would have changed the work.

### What "mining stage by stage" actually means in practice

It means passing over the same document multiple times, each pass looking for one category of thing only, and writing down what each pass yields *before* starting the next pass.

| Pass | What you are looking for, and nothing else | What you write down |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Facts** | Numbers, dates, market positions, product specifications, prices, distribution, competitor names | A flat list of verifiable statements with page references |
| **2. Constraints** | Anything stated as fixed: runtime, formats, languages, legal wording, mandatory elements, budget, territory, approval chain | The non-negotiables list |
| **3. Claims** | Everything the brand is permitted to say, and the qualification attached to each | Claim, qualification, evidence source, expiry if any |
| **4. Stated audience** | Every group named anywhere in the document, including ones mentioned once in passing | Audience list, with the sentence each was named in |
| **5. Language of the client** | The exact words the client uses for their own product, benefits and users | Vocabulary list, verbatim |
| **6. Contradictions** | Places where the document says two incompatible things | Contradiction log, with both quotes |
| **7. Silences** | What a document of this type would normally contain and this one does not | Gap list. This is the highest value pass |
| **8. Observations** | Statements of consumer behaviour, whether or not the document calls them insights | Raw observation list |
| **9. Candidate insights** | Your own interpretation of the pass 8 material | Insight candidates, each linked to the observation it explains |
| **10. Success measures** | Anything that implies how this will be judged | Measure list |

**[PRINCIPLE] Passes 6 and 7 are the ones that replace the live client conversation.** A skim cannot find a contradiction, because a contradiction requires holding two separated passages side by side. A skim cannot find a silence, because a silence is invisible unless you have a model of what should be there. Those two passes produce exactly the questions you would otherwise have asked the client, and they let you either answer them from elsewhere in the document or flag them explicitly as assumptions.

**[PRINCIPLE] The stage-by-stage rule exists because single-pass reading collapses categories.** Reading for facts and lines and constraints simultaneously means the interesting sentence wins and the boring constraint is lost. The boring constraint is the one that kills the concept in week three.

**The output of mining is not a summary.** It is ten separate lists plus a written statement of what could not be answered from the document and is therefore an assumption. That statement is what makes a document based process defensible.

## A5. TRACEABILITY FROM INSIGHT TO COPY

**[PRINCIPLE] The requirement: a reader should be able to start at any final line of copy and walk backward, without guessing, to the research finding that produced it.**

The chain has a fixed number of links:

**Research finding** goes to **observation** goes to **insight** goes to **proposition** goes to **territory or concept** goes to **script beat** goes to **final line**.

Each link should be nameable. In a well built document, the final line carries a reference: this line descends from insight 2, which descends from observation 4, which came from page 11 of the client study.

### How the chain breaks

**Break one: the frame shift.** A strategic stage is framed one way, and the final line is framed another way, and nobody notices because both are good. The insight was about *fear of being blamed*. The tagline is about *joy of choosing*. Both are defensible. Neither connects. The document now contains a strategy section that supports work that does not exist, and a line with no support. **[PRINCIPLE] This is the most common break and the hardest to see, because nothing in the document is bad. Only the join is missing.**

**Break two: the orphaned insight.** An insight is developed at length, praised in review, and never actually feeds anything downstream. The concept was arrived at another way, usually visually, and the insight was retro-fitted into the deck to give it a spine. The tell is that the insight could be deleted and no other section would need to change.

**Break three: the substituted proposition.** The brief carried one proposition. During concept development a second, more filmable idea appeared and quietly replaced it. The brief section of the document still shows the original. Every stage after concept is now serving a proposition that no one approved.

**Break four: the lost qualification.** The claim in the brief was "the fastest in its class, when measured on the standard cycle." The line in the script is "the fastest." The qualification lived in the brief and did not travel. This break is a legal exposure, not just a craft one.

**Break five: the inheritance gap after an edit.** Stage 4 was rewritten. Stage 5 was written to inherit from the previous version of stage 4. Both stages read fine in isolation.

### Why you patch the traceability, not the symptom

When a reviewer says "the tagline does not feel connected to the strategy," there are two possible responses. One is to change the tagline until it feels connected. The other is to find where the chain actually broke and repair the link.

**[PRINCIPLE] Patching the visible symptom leaves the break in place, and the break will surface again at the next stage, in a different disguise.** If the insight never fed the tagline, then rewriting the tagline to *sound* more like the insight produces a line that rhymes with the strategy without descending from it. The next thing derived from that stage will break in the same place. Meanwhile the document has lost its diagnostic value: it now looks consistent, so nobody will check it again.

Patching the traceability means doing one of three things explicitly:

1. Repair the link. Show the derivation. Add the missing intermediate step that makes the descent visible.
2. Replace the upstream stage. If the line is right and the insight is wrong, change the insight and re-derive everything downstream of it.
3. Declare the break. Write, in the document, that this execution does not descend from insight 2 and state what it does descend from.

All three are acceptable. Silently smoothing the surface is not.

## A6. WRITING TO A FIXED RUNTIME

A thirty second film is not a story with a length. It is a length with a story fitted into it. **[PRINCIPLE] Runtime is the first constraint and the one most often treated as the last.**

### The arithmetic

Spoken voiceover in a commercial register runs, as a working figure, at roughly two to two and a half words per second in English, slower for a warm or intimate read, faster for a listing or urgent read. Arabic and other languages have different syllable densities and must be timed independently rather than assumed to match. **[PRINCIPLE] Never estimate a foreign language runtime from the word count of the source language.**

A thirty second film therefore holds roughly fifty five to seventy words of comfortable voiceover, less if there is an endframe, less again if there is a legal super to be read, less again if any moment is meant to breathe.

**The endframe is not free.** A logo and line endframe consumes two to three seconds. Legal supers consume time proportional to their length. Those seconds come out of the story, not out of nowhere.

### Why full runtime voiceover must be written as continuous prose first

**[PRINCIPLE] Write the voiceover as one unbroken paragraph, read it aloud against a stopwatch, and only then break it into shots.**

The reason is rhythm and breath. Voiceover is heard as a single utterance by the viewer. It has an argument, a turn, and a landing. Those properties exist at the level of the whole piece, not at the level of the line. When you write the whole thing first, you can hear where it stalls, where it repeats, where the turn lands too early, and you can fix it by moving three words rather than by renegotiating a shot.

Writing it whole also gives you a true duration before any production decision has been made. That number is the budget every subsequent decision spends against.

### What happens when shots are written first

The failure sequence is consistent:

1. The visual sequence is designed first, because it is more exciting to design.
2. Each shot is assigned a duration based on how long it feels like it should be on screen.
3. Copy is then written *into* the shots, one line per shot.
4. The lines are individually fine and collectively arrhythmic.

The two specific defects this produces:

**Gaps.** A shot was allocated three seconds because it is beautiful. The line assigned to it takes one and a half seconds to say. The remaining one and a half seconds contains no voice. In isolation that is a pause. Repeated four times across a thirty second film, it is a film that feels underwritten and slow, and the client note will be "it feels empty," which will be misdiagnosed as a music problem.

**Dead air.** Worse than a gap, dead air is a stretch where neither voice nor visual is advancing the argument, because the shot was placed for beauty and the line for that shot was already said. Dead air at the front of a film loses the viewer. Dead air before the endframe loses the proposition.

**The inverse defect: cramming.** Shots written first, lines dropped in after, frequently produce the opposite problem in the back half, where the remaining copy has to fit into shots that were sized before the copy existed. The read speeds up, the landing is rushed, and the single most important sentence in the film is the one delivered fastest.

**[PRINCIPLE] The correct order is: proposition, then continuous voiceover, then timed read, then beat structure, then shots.** Shots are allocated the time the copy actually needs, plus deliberate silence where silence is a decision rather than a residue.

### Silence as a decision

Silence should appear in the script as an explicit item with a duration, labelled as such. "1.5s, no VO, sound design only, the pause before the turn." Written that way, it survives the edit. Left as a gap between lines, it will be either filled or cut by someone who assumes it was an accident.

## A7. LIST-DEVICE COPY AND THE WORD-FOR-WORD AUDIT

A line structured as an explicit list is a specific rhetorical device with a specific hazard.

"Every surface. Every corner. Every day."
"For the commute, the school run, the weekend."
"Cleans, protects, restores."

**[PRINCIPLE] When a line is built as a list, the audience does not hear it as poetry. They hear it as an inventory.** The list form makes an implicit claim of completeness. The word "every" makes that claim explicit. The comma series makes it structural. The viewer reads the enumerated items as *the full set of what this product does*, and reads anything outside the enumeration as outside the offer.

### The consequence

Anything shown on screen but not named in the list reads as an afterthought at best, and as an unsupported claim at worst. If the voiceover says "for the kitchen, the bathroom, the car" and the film shows a garden, the garden is visually present and verbally excluded. The viewer's brain resolves the mismatch by discounting the garden.

The reverse mismatch is worse. If the list names four things and the film shows three, the fourth item is a promise with no evidence, and in regulated categories that can be a compliance problem rather than a craft one.

### The word-for-word audit this demands

Any list device in a script requires a line by line reconciliation table before the script is approved. Not a general check. A literal enumeration.

| Word in the list | Shown on screen? | Shot reference | Named in supers? | Compliance status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| item 1 | yes | 04 | no | claim approved |
| item 2 | yes | 06, 07 | yes | claim approved |
| item 3 | **no** | none | no | **unsupported** |

And the reverse direction, which is the one that gets skipped:

| Thing shown on screen | Named in the list? | If no, why is it there? |
|---|---|---|
| element A | yes | |
| element B | **no** | **decision required: cut it, or add it to the list, or accept it reads as afterthought** |

**[PRINCIPLE] Both directions must be run. Auditing only from the words to the screen catches unsupported claims and misses orphaned visuals, which are the ones that make a film feel unfocused without anyone being able to say why.**

### The scaling hazard

List devices scale badly. Every item added to a list lengthens the line, consumes runtime, demands a corresponding visual, and dilutes each individual item's weight. **[PRINCIPLE] A three item list is a rhythm. A five item list is a specification sheet.** When a stakeholder asks for one more item in the list, the correct response is not to add it. It is to state what it costs in runtime and what it takes away from the other items.

## A8. BILINGUAL AND MULTI-LANGUAGE WORK

**[PRINCIPLE] The governing rule: each language is its own copywriting pass, with its own candidate lines, judged on its own merits. It is never a translation of a line chosen in another language.**

The reason is that a line is chosen because it works: because it has rhythm, a turn, a double meaning, a familiar cadence, a length that fits a beat. None of those properties survive translation reliably. Choosing the line in language A and then translating it to language B guarantees that language B receives a line optimised for constraints that do not apply to it and stripped of the property that made it good.

### Transcreation

Transcreation is the working name for the practice of re-creating the *intent* of a piece of copy in another language rather than its words. The transcreation brief is not the source line. It is the layer above the source line: the proposition, the insight it descends from, the tone, the function of the line in the film, the runtime available, and any element that must be preserved literally (a product name, a legal phrase, a claim qualification).

**[PRINCIPLE] The correct handover for a second language is the proposition and the beat function, not the finished line.** The finished line may be supplied as reference, clearly labelled as reference and explicitly not as source text.

### What each language pass must produce

Independently, per language:

- Its own set of candidate lines, at least three per key beat
- Its own timed read against the runtime, because word count does not transfer
- Its own check for unintended meaning, idiom collision and register
- Its own list-device audit, because a list that has three balanced items in one language may have three unbalanced ones in another
- Its own dialect decision, where the language has meaningfully different registers by market

### Register and dialect

**[PRINCIPLE] Languages with a formal written standard and widely divergent spoken forms require an explicit register decision at brief stage, not at recording stage.** Choosing the standard written form reads as authoritative, institutional and distant. Choosing a spoken regional form reads as intimate and local, and simultaneously narrows the territory in which the film reads as native. That is a strategic decision with a media consequence, and it belongs in the brief's non-negotiables, not in a voice direction note.

### What breaks when a language version is cut from a document

Removing a language is treated as a deletion. It is not. It is a structural change, and it leaves three classes of debris.

**Orphaned labels.** Headings, table columns, section numbers and cross references that were written to distinguish between versions. A section titled "Version A voiceover" makes no sense once version B is gone, and a reader will assume a version B still exists somewhere. Tables with a column per language now have an empty column or a silently renumbered one. Any sentence that said "as in the other language version" now points at nothing.

**Broken compliance claims.** Legal wording is often approved per market, and markets map to languages. When the language is cut, the approval that travelled with it is cut too, and any surviving reference to "approved wording" may now be describing an approval that applied only to the removed version. The reverse also occurs: a mandatory disclosure required only in the removed market may remain in the document as a global mandatory, imposing a constraint that no longer applies.

**Broken stage-to-stage inheritance.** If stage 5 produced two language versions and stage 6 was written to consume both, removing one leaves stage 6 with a dependency on a stage that no longer produces what it consumes. This is invisible when reading forward, because stage 6 still reads coherently. It is only visible when reading the derivation backward.

**[PRINCIPLE] Every removal is a backward audit, never a forward deletion.** The protocol is in Part B, section B5.

## A9. SCOPE AND BREADTH INSIDE A FIXED RUNTIME

The most common late stage request is some version of "can it also cover X."

The instinctive responses are all wrong:

- **Stretch the whole piece.** Every beat gets slightly shorter to make room. The result is a film where nothing has time to land, and the original strong beat is now as weak as the added one.
- **Add a new beat.** The arc had a shape: setup, turn, landing, or whatever structure was chosen. Adding a beat inserts a step into a structure that was sized for its steps. The turn now arrives later than the viewer's attention allows.
- **Speed up the read.** Discussed in A6. The landing is the casualty.

**[PRINCIPLE] The correct move is to identify the one beat in the existing structure that was built to hold breadth, and expand only that beat.**

Almost every workable short film structure contains exactly one such beat. It is the beat whose job is *range* rather than *depth*: the montage, the enumeration, the "and also" beat, the list device, the sequence of quick cuts that establishes scope. That beat can absorb additional items at a low structural cost, because its function is already plurality. Adding a fourth item to a three item montage does not change the film's shape. Adding a fourth act does.

### How to find the breadth beat

Ask, of each beat: does this beat work because of *what it is*, or because of *how many things it contains*? Beats that work because of what they are cannot absorb additions. Beats that work because of quantity can.

- An opening that establishes a single situation: cannot absorb.
- A turn where the argument pivots: cannot absorb. It has exactly one job.
- A montage establishing range of use: **can absorb**.
- A landing line: cannot absorb.
- An endframe with product variants: **can absorb**, within legibility limits.

### The cost that must be stated

Expanding the breadth beat is not free either. It consumes runtime, which must come from somewhere, and it dilutes the items already in it. **[PRINCIPLE] Always answer a breadth request with a stated trade: "yes, in beat 3, which costs 2 seconds taken from beat 1, and reduces each item in beat 3 from 1.2 seconds to 0.9 seconds."** A trade stated numerically is usually accepted or withdrawn on the spot. A trade left implicit is accepted and then complained about after the edit.

## A10. NOT EVERY APPROVED CLAIM BELONGS IN EVERY EXECUTION

Brand teams maintain a list of claims that legal has cleared. That list is a *permission set*, not a *content requirement*.

**[PRINCIPLE] A short film does not have to say everything the brand is allowed to say. The permission set is the boundary of the sayable, not the checklist of the said.**

This confusion produces a recognisable artifact: the film that recites. Every approved benefit appears, each for one second, none with any weight, and the viewer retains none of them. It is the direct enemy of the single-minded proposition, and it usually enters late, when individual stakeholders each add the claim they are responsible for.

### The argument to make

Three claims delivered at one third weight are not equal to one claim at full weight. They are worth considerably less, because recall is not linear in exposure count. The film's job is to install one thing. Every additional claim reduces the probability that the primary claim is the one retained.

### The practical mechanism

Keep two explicit lists in the concept document:

1. **In this execution.** The claims this film will carry, with the beat each lands in.
2. **Approved but deliberately not used here.** Every other cleared claim, listed by name, with a one line note on where it does belong (product page, longer format, a different film in the campaign, point of sale).

**[PRINCIPLE] Listing the deliberate exclusions is what makes the exclusion survive review.** An unlisted claim looks forgotten and gets added back. A claim listed under "deliberately not used here, belongs on the product page" looks like a decision and gets discussed as one.

## A11. HOW A CONCEPT DIFFERS FROM A SCRIPT, A TREATMENT AND A SHOT LIST

Four documents, four authors, four questions. They are frequently merged, and the merge always costs something.

| Document | Question it answers | Author | Contains | Does not contain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Concept** | What is the idea, and why does it answer the brief? | Creative team, with the planner | The proposition, the insight it descends from, the idea in one sentence, why it works, how it extends | Shot descriptions, lens choices, cast specifics, timings |
| **Script** | What is said and what happens, in order, to time? | Copywriter | Voiceover or dialogue verbatim, action per beat, supers, endframe, running time | How it is lit, how it is cut, who is cast |
| **Treatment** | How will this be made, and what will it feel like? | Director | Interpretation, tone, casting direction, visual language, sound, post approach | New story content. The story is already fixed by the script |
| **Shot list** | What exactly is captured, and in what order? | Director with first AD and DoP | Numbered shots, size, movement, lens, coverage, duration | Argument. It assumes the argument is settled |

**[PRINCIPLE] The concept is the only one of the four that is allowed to be wrong about execution and still be correct.** Its job is to establish that the idea answers the brief. If the concept is right and the execution is later changed, nothing upstream breaks. If the concept is wrong, every downstream document is executing an answer to a question nobody asked.

**The common merge failures:**

- **Concept written as a script.** The idea is only visible through one particular execution. When the execution proves impossible, the idea appears to die with it, and the team starts again from the brief instead of from the idea.
- **Script written as a treatment.** Adjectives about how it will feel occupy space that should carry the actual words. The film has a mood and no copy.
- **Treatment written as a concept.** The director re-proposes the idea. This is out of scope and destabilises an approval that was already given.
- **Shot list written before the script is timed.** Covered in A6. It is the single most reliable source of gaps and dead air.

## A12. VOCABULARY GLOSSARY

| Term | Definition as used in this bible |
|---|---|
| **Brief** | The document converting a business problem into a creative problem. Owns the problem, audience, insight, proposition, non-negotiables and success measure |
| **Observation** | A verifiable statement about behaviour or the market. Public. Shared with competitors |
| **Insight** | An explanation of why an observation is true, at the level of human motivation. Proprietary. Must survive the reversal test |
| **Proposition** | The single thing the work must make the audience take away. Written as a commitment, not a description |
| **Single-minded proposition** | The convention of reducing a brief to exactly one proposition. **[SOURCED: standing convention of agency brief formats]** |
| **Support** | The reason to believe the proposition. Product truth, evidence, demonstration |
| **Non-negotiable** | A constraint that cannot be traded without reopening the brief. Runtime, format, legal wording, mandatory elements |
| **Mandatory** | An element that must appear: logo, endframe, legal super, disclaimer, URL |
| **Success measure** | The stated test of whether the work worked |
| **Territory** | A direction of thought that could produce many concepts. Broader than a concept, narrower than a proposition |
| **Concept** | A specific idea that answers the brief, expressible in one sentence, executable in more than one way |
| **Execution** | One particular realisation of a concept |
| **Script** | The verbatim words plus action plus supers plus timing |
| **Beat** | A structural unit of the film with one job |
| **Breadth beat** | The one beat in a structure whose function is range rather than depth, and which can absorb additional items |
| **List device** | A copy structure that enumerates. Creates an implicit completeness claim |
| **Traceability** | The property that any final line can be walked backward to the research finding it descends from |
| **Orphaned insight** | An insight in the document that nothing downstream actually uses |
| **Orphaned label** | A heading, column or cross reference that survived the removal of the thing it referred to |
| **Frame shift** | A break where an upstream stage is framed one way and a downstream output another, with no join |
| **Transcreation** | Re-creating the intent of copy in another language, briefed from the proposition rather than from the source line |
| **Register** | The formality level and dialect choice of a language version. A strategic decision, not a voice note |
| **Dead air** | A stretch of film where neither voice nor image advances the argument |
| **Gap** | Unfilled time inside a shot whose duration was set before the copy that occupies it |
| **Permission set** | The full list of legally cleared claims. A boundary, not a checklist |
| **Runtime accounting** | The line by line arithmetic that proves a script fits its duration, including endframe and supers |
| **Planning / strategy** | The discipline owning the problem definition and the evidence. **[SOURCED: founded 1968 by Stephen King at JWT and Stanley Pollitt at BMP]** |

## B1. THE INTAKE: QUESTIONS TO ASK WHEN HANDED A BRIEF

Ask these before writing anything. Where a live client is available, ask them directly. Where only a document is available, answer them by mining (A4) and mark each answer with its source.

### B1.1 The question set

**Problem**
1. What is the business problem, stated as a diagnosis rather than a wish?
2. What has been tried already, and what happened?
3. Is this genuinely a communication problem, or a product, price, distribution or service problem being handed to communication?

**Audience**
4. Who exactly is this for? Describe one person, not a bracket.
5. What do they currently believe, currently do, and what would they have to believe differently?
6. Is more than one audience named anywhere in the brief? List every one.
7. If more than one, which is primary, and who decided?

**Insight and proposition**
8. What observations are supplied, and what is their evidence source?
9. What is the insight, and does it survive the reversal test?
10. What is the single proposition? If more than one is offered, which one wins?
11. What is the support, the reason to believe?

**Non-negotiables**
12. Exact runtime or runtimes. Are cutdowns required?
13. Aspect ratios and placements.
14. Languages, and per language, which register or dialect.
15. Mandatory elements: logo, endframe, legal super, disclaimer, URL, campaign line.
16. Which claims are legally cleared, and what qualification attaches to each?
17. Territory and market restrictions.
18. Budget envelope and shoot day count, if known.
19. Who approves, in what order, and who can veto late?

**Tone and brand**
20. What does the brand sound like, in its own words? Supply verbatim examples.
21. What is explicitly off limits in tone?
22. Which competitors must the work not resemble?

**Measure**
23. How will success be judged, and by whom?
24. What would make this work a failure even if it is well made?

**Meta**
25. What is missing from this brief that a brief of this type should contain?

### B1.2 Self-answering from a rich brief document

When no client is available, run the A4 ten pass mine, then map each pass to the questions above.

| Question block | Answered by mining pass | If the pass yields nothing |
|---|---|---|
| Problem (1 to 3) | 1 Facts, 7 Silences | State the problem as an assumption, flagged, derived from the facts available |
| Audience (4 to 7) | 4 Stated audience, 8 Observations | Name every audience found. If no primary is stated, declare one and mark it as an agent decision |
| Insight (8 to 11) | 8 Observations, 9 Candidate insights | Generate candidates yourself, tag them as derived not supplied |
| Non-negotiables (12 to 19) | 2 Constraints, 3 Claims | **Do not assume.** An unstated runtime or unstated claim qualification must be raised as a blocking question |
| Tone (20 to 22) | 5 Client language | Extract verbatim vocabulary and use it as the tone evidence |
| Measure (23 to 24) | 10 Success measures | Propose a measure and mark it as proposed |
| Meta (25) | 6 Contradictions, 7 Silences | This pass *is* the answer |

**[PRINCIPLE] Three rules govern self answering.**

1. **Every self answered item carries a tag** in the output document: `[FROM DOC p.N]`, `[DERIVED]`, or `[ASSUMED]`.
2. **Non-negotiables may never be assumed.** Runtime, legal wording, claim qualifications and mandatory elements are the four categories where a wrong assumption is not recoverable in revision. If they are absent from the document, they are blocking questions, and the work proceeds with the gap stated in writing at the top of the document.
3. **Contradictions are surfaced, not resolved silently.** When the document says two incompatible things, quote both, state which one the work proceeds on, and state why.

## B2. STAGE-BY-STAGE CONCEPT DEVELOPMENT STRUCTURE

Each stage has a required output. A stage is not complete until its output exists in writing. No stage may begin before the previous stage's output is fixed.

| Stage | Name | Must produce | Gate before proceeding |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | Mine and intake | Ten mining lists, answered question set with source tags, blocking questions list | No unanswered non-negotiable |
| **1** | Problem statement | One paragraph diagnosis. Not a wish | A reader can name what is wrong without reading further |
| **2** | Audience decision | Primary audience named as a person, with current belief, current behaviour, required belief. All other named audiences listed as secondary with an explicit note | Exactly one primary named |
| **3** | Observation set | Numbered observations, each with its evidence source | Each is verifiable and none is an interpretation |
| **4** | Insight set | Numbered insights, each explicitly linked to the observation numbers it explains | Each passes the reversal test |
| **5** | Proposition | One sentence. Plus the support | No "and also" can be inserted |
| **6** | Territories | Two to four directions, each naming the insight number it descends from | Each territory is genuinely different in kind, not in decoration |
| **7** | Concepts | One to three concepts per selected territory. Each stated in one sentence, executable more than one way | Each concept names its territory and its insight number |
| **8** | Structure | Beat map for the chosen concept: beat number, job of the beat, provisional duration. Breadth beat identified and labelled | Durations sum to runtime including endframe |
| **9** | Continuous copy | Full voiceover written as one unbroken paragraph, per language, timed aloud | Timed read fits the beat budget from stage 8 |
| **10** | Script | Copy broken into beats, action per beat, supers, endframe, runtime accounting table | Every second accounted for. Silence is explicit and labelled |
| **11** | Audits | Traceability table, list device audit both directions, claim audit, per language audit | All green or all exceptions declared |
| **12** | Concept document | Assembled deliverable, all stages visible, all tags intact | Passes B7 pre release audit |

**[PRINCIPLE] Stages 3, 4 and 5 must be separate written artifacts even when they feel obvious.** Collapsing them is what produces the frame shift break in A5, because when observation, insight and proposition are written in one motion, the joins are never articulated and therefore can never be checked.

**[PRINCIPLE] Stage 6 exists to prevent premature commitment.** A single territory taken straight from proposition to concept means the first idea is the only idea, and it will be defended rather than tested.

## B3. THE TRACEABILITY REQUIREMENT

**Rule: every final line in the deliverable names the insight it descends from, explicitly, in the document.**

Not in a comment. Not in a separate file. In the document, visible to the reader, in a table.

### B3.1 The required table

| Line ID | Final line | Beat | Descends from | Chain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | *the opening line* | 1 | Insight 2 | Obs 4 (doc p.11) to Ins 2 to Prop to Territory B to Concept B1 to Beat 1 |
| L2 | *the turn line* | 3 | Insight 2 | Obs 4 to Ins 2 to Prop to Territory B to Concept B1 to Beat 3 |
| L3 | *the landing line* | 5 | Insight 1 | Obs 1, Obs 7 to Ins 1 to Prop to Territory B to Concept B1 to Beat 5 |
| L4 | *the endframe line* | 6 | Proposition directly | Prop to Beat 6. **Declared: does not descend from a single insight** |

### B3.2 Rules for filling it

1. **Every line gets a row.** Including supers, including the endframe line, including any spoken product name that is doing persuasive work.
2. **"Descends from" must name a numbered insight, or explicitly declare that it does not.** A blank is a defect. "Insight generally" is a blank wearing a word.
3. **Every insight must appear in the table at least once, or be marked as unused.** An insight that appears nowhere is an orphaned insight and must be either deleted from the document or explicitly labelled: *"Insight 3: developed, not used in this execution. Retained for campaign extension."*
4. **When a line is changed, its row is re-derived, not edited.** Changing the line and leaving the chain untouched is exactly the symptom patching described in A5.

### B3.3 The break repair procedure

When the table cannot be filled for a line, one of three actions is taken and written down:

| Situation | Action | What must be written |
|---|---|---|
| The line is right, the chain is missing an intermediate step | Repair the link. Add the missing step | The added step, in its stage, with its own derivation |
| The line is right, the upstream insight is wrong | Replace the insight. Re-derive every downstream stage | New insight, plus a re-run of stages 5 to 11 for everything that touched the old one |
| The line is right and genuinely does not descend from any insight | Declare the break | *"L7 does not descend from an insight. It descends from [brand mandatory / product truth / legal requirement]."* |

**[PRINCIPLE] Never rewrite a line to make it sound more like the strategy. That is the symptom patch, and it destroys the document's diagnostic value, because it makes a broken chain look intact.**

## B4. THE AUDIENCE-NAMING REQUIREMENT

**Rule: when the brief names more than one audience and the execution can serve only one, the document must state which one it serves and name the gap explicitly.**

Multi audience briefs are normal: the buyer and the user, the specifier and the payer, the parent and the child, the institutional partner and the end beneficiary. A thirty second film with one proposition and one register cannot serve two audiences with different current beliefs. Attempting it produces work aimed at the midpoint between them, which is a position no real person occupies.

### B4.1 The required statement

Placed in the concept document immediately after the audience stage, in this form:

> **Audiences named in the brief:** [list all, with the sentence each was named in]
> **This execution serves:** [one]
> **Because:** [one sentence of reasoning tied to the problem statement]
> **Not served by this execution:** [each remaining audience, named]
> **The gap this leaves:** [what that audience does not receive from this work]
> **Where that gap is addressed:** [another asset, another phase, or explicitly: nowhere, and this is a known and accepted exposure]

### B4.2 Why the gap must be named rather than quietly accepted

Three reasons.

1. **It converts an omission into a decision.** An unnamed gap is read by a reviewer as an oversight, and the standard fix a reviewer proposes is to add a line for the missing audience. That line dilutes the proposition. A named gap is read as a scope boundary and is argued about on the merits.
2. **It protects the work at the review that happens without you.** A stakeholder who represents the unserved audience will see the work and ask why they are absent. The document must answer that question in your absence.
3. **It creates the brief for the next asset.** A named gap is a ready made brief for the piece that closes it.

**[PRINCIPLE] If the document cannot name a primary audience, the concept is not ready, regardless of how good the idea is.** An idea that works for "everyone in the brief" has not been tested against anyone.

## B5. THE REMOVAL PROTOCOL

**Rule: no section, language version, territory, concept, beat or claim is ever deleted forward. Every removal triggers a backward audit of the whole document.**

Removal feels like the safest operation in document work and is the most dangerous, because the removed thing leaves a shaped hole that the surrounding text was built around.

### B5.1 The eight step protocol

Run all eight, in order, every time.

| Step | Audit for | Concrete check |
|---|---|---|
| **1. References** | Any sentence that points at the removed thing | Search for its name, its number, and phrases like "as above", "the other version", "both", "either", "respectively" |
| **2. Labels** | Headings, table columns, list numbering, section numbers that implied its existence | "Version A" with no version B. A table column now empty. Numbered items with a gap. Any label containing a count |
| **3. Counts** | Every number in the document that counted the removed thing | "Three territories", "two languages", "five beats", "both markets". Counts hide in prose |
| **4. Compliance** | Claims, disclaimers, approvals and mandatories that travelled with the removed thing | Approval scoped to a removed market. Mandatory disclosure required only by the removed market but still listed as global. Claim qualification that lived only in the removed version |
| **5. Inheritance forward** | Any later stage that consumed the removed thing's output | Which stages listed it as an input? Do they still have all their inputs? |
| **6. Inheritance backward** | Any earlier stage that existed only to feed the removed thing | An insight developed solely for the removed territory is now orphaned. Either delete it or mark it unused |
| **7. Traceability table** | Every row whose chain passed through the removed thing | Re-derive. Do not edit in place |
| **8. Runtime and budget** | Any arithmetic that included the removed thing | Beat durations, word counts, deliverable lists, format matrices |

### B5.2 Language removal, specifically

Removing a language is the highest debris removal in this document type. It touches all eight steps at once.

| What breaks | Specific check |
|---|---|
| **Orphaned labels** | Section titles naming the version. Table columns. Any "per language" heading now describing one language. Bilingual layout structures with an empty side |
| **Broken compliance claims** | Legal wording approved per market. Mandatory disclosures required only in the removed market. Claim qualifications that existed only in that language's regulatory context |
| **Broken inheritance** | Any stage written to consume both versions. Casting and voice direction specifying two reads. Deliverable matrices assuming two audio stems. Runtime accounting that reconciled two different timed reads |
| **Register decisions** | A dialect or register decision made *relative to* the removed version ("the formal version carries the legal line, the spoken version carries the emotion") is now incoherent |
| **Structural dependency** | If the concept itself used the two languages as a device, the concept dies with the removal and must be rebuilt, not trimmed |

**[PRINCIPLE] The tell that a removal was done forward rather than backward is that the document still reads perfectly. Forward deletion produces a document with no visible seams and several invisible false statements.** The audit is the only thing that finds them.

### B5.3 Removal record

Every removal is logged in the document, not just performed:

> **Removed:** [what]
> **Date and reason:** [why]
> **Backward audit run:** steps 1 to 8, [date]
> **Consequences found and fixed:** [list]
> **Consequences accepted:** [list, with reasoning]

## B6. FAILURE MODES AND UNDER-SPECIFICATION TELLS

### B6.1 Failure modes

**F1. The brief that permits everything**
**Signature:** two teams brief off it and produce work with no shared property.
**Cause:** the brief summarised the client instead of deciding anything.
**Detection:** ask what the brief forbids. If the answer is nothing, it is not a brief.
**Correction:** return to stage 1. Write the problem as a diagnosis.

**F2. Observation wearing an insight's clothes**
**Signature:** the insight section restates the behaviour and adds a motivation word.
**Cause:** the observation was interesting enough that nobody interpreted it.
**Detection:** the reversal test. If the opposite is absurd, it is not an insight.
**Correction:** ask "why would a reasonable person do that?" three times in succession.

**F3. The multi-headed proposition**
**Signature:** the proposition contains a comma or an "and".
**Cause:** stakeholder accommodation.
**Detection:** insert "and also" at every juncture. If it fits anywhere, it is a list.
**Correction:** pick one. Move the others to the "approved but not used here" list.

**F4. The orphaned insight**
**Signature:** the insight could be deleted without any other section changing.
**Cause:** the concept was found visually and the insight was retro-fitted for the deck.
**Detection:** run the B3 table. Any insight appearing in zero rows.
**Correction:** delete it, or label it unused, or re-derive the concept from it honestly.

**F5. The frame shift**
**Signature:** strategy framed one way, line framed another, both good, no join.
**Cause:** stages 4, 5 and 7 written by different passes or different people without the join being articulated.
**Detection:** read the insight and the line consecutively, aloud, with nothing in between. If a listener needs a bridge sentence, the bridge is missing from the document.
**Correction:** B3.3, one of the three actions. Not a rewrite of the line.

**F6. Shots first, copy after**
**Signature:** gaps, dead air, and a rushed landing.
**Cause:** the visual sequence was designed before the copy was timed.
**Detection:** compare each beat's allotted duration to its timed read. Any beat with more than about 0.7 seconds of unaccounted time that is not labelled as deliberate silence.
**Correction:** rewrite the voiceover as continuous prose, re-time, re-cut the beat durations to the copy.

**F7. Unaudited list device**
**Signature:** the film shows something the list does not name, or names something the film does not show.
**Cause:** the list was written for rhythm and never reconciled.
**Detection:** the two direction audit table in A7.
**Correction:** cut the visual, add the word, or accept and record the mismatch.

**F8. Translation instead of transcreation**
**Signature:** the second language version is longer, flatter, and has lost the turn.
**Cause:** the line was chosen in language A and handed to language B as source text.
**Detection:** does the second language have its own candidate lines on record? If not, it was translated.
**Correction:** re-brief from the proposition and beat function. Generate at least three candidates natively.

**F9. Forward deletion**
**Signature:** the document reads perfectly and contains a false statement about its own structure.
**Cause:** something was removed without a backward audit.
**Detection:** B5.1, all eight steps.
**Correction:** run the protocol. Log it.

**F10. The recital**
**Signature:** every approved claim appears, none has weight.
**Cause:** the permission set was read as a checklist.
**Detection:** count claims in the script. More than two in a thirty second film is a warning. More than three is a defect.
**Correction:** A10. Two lists, one of them the deliberate exclusions.

**F11. Breadth by stretching**
**Signature:** an added requirement was absorbed by shortening every beat.
**Cause:** the breadth beat was never identified.
**Detection:** compare beat durations before and after the scope change. If all of them moved, the film was stretched.
**Correction:** restore the original durations, identify the breadth beat, expand only it, state the trade numerically.

**F12. The unnamed audience choice**
**Signature:** the brief named three audiences, the work serves one, the document does not say so.
**Cause:** the choice was made intuitively and never written.
**Detection:** count audiences in the brief. Count audiences addressed in B4.1.
**Correction:** write the B4.1 statement.

### B6.2 Under-specification tells

A quick scan table. Each left column item means the document is under specified in the way the right column names.

| Tell in the document | What it actually means |
|---|---|
| "Raise awareness" as the objective | No problem diagnosis exists |
| Audience given as an age range and a class bracket | No audience work was done |
| "Consumers want quality and value" | Platitude in the insight slot |
| Proposition contains a comma | Not single-minded |
| No claim qualifications listed | Legal exposure, or claims not yet mined |
| No runtime stated | Every downstream timing decision is unanchored |
| "Bilingual" with no register decision | Voice direction will make a strategic decision by default |
| No success measure | The work will be judged on taste |
| Insight section and line section both good, no bridge | Frame shift |
| Any beat with no stated job | Redundancy waiting to be found in the edit |
| Numbers in prose ("both versions", "three territories") after an edit | Removal was done forward |
| Reference images but no captions | Reference will be read as literal |
| "TBC" on a non-negotiable | Blocking. Work should not proceed |
| Every approved claim present | Recital |
| A list device with no audit table | Unsupported claim or orphaned visual, one or the other, guaranteed |
| Concept expressible only by describing one execution | The idea and the execution have fused |

   or is a defect. Unlabelled gaps will be filled or cut by someone else.

## B8. TRIGGER TABLE: WHICH UPSTREAM CHANGES FORCE A REBUILD

Any of these events requires the named stages to be reopened. Severity indicates how far the rebuild propagates and who must be told.

| Upstream change | Severity | Stages to rebuild | Audits to re-run |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem statement changed by client** | Critical. Full rebuild | 1 through 12 | All |
| **Proposition changed** | Critical | 5 through 12 | Traceability, claims, list device, all languages |
| **Insight replaced or invalidated** | Critical | 4 through 12 | Traceability (full re-derive), territory selection |
| **Primary audience changed** | Critical | 2 through 12 | Audience statement, traceability, register decisions |
| **Runtime changed** | Critical | 8 through 11 | Runtime accounting, continuous copy re-timed, beat durations, all languages |
| **A language version added** | High | 9 through 11 for the new language only | Native candidates, timed read, list device audit, register decision, deliverable matrix |
| **A language version removed** | High | Removal protocol B5, then 10, 11 | All eight removal steps, plus compliance, plus inheritance both directions |
| **Register or dialect decision changed** | High | 9 through 11 for that language | Native candidates regenerated, timed read, casting and voice implications |
| **Claim withdrawn or qualification added by legal** | High | 10, 11 | Claim audit, list device audit, traceability for any line carrying it, supers |
| **Claim added to the permission set** | Low | None automatically | Add to "approved but not used here" unless the proposition changes. **Do not insert into the script by default** |
| **Scope broadened ("can it also cover X")** | Medium to high | 8, then 9 and 10 for the affected beat only | Identify breadth beat, expand only it, restate trade numerically, re-run runtime accounting and list device audit |
| **A beat cut** | Medium to high | Removal protocol B5, then 8 through 11 | Runtime accounting, traceability rows for removed lines, counts in prose |
| **A concept or territory dropped** | Medium | B5, then 5 through 7 | Orphaned insight check, counts, inheritance backward |
| **Mandatory element added (new endframe, new legal super)** | Medium to high | 8, 10 | Runtime accounting. Time must come from a named beat |
| **Aspect ratio or placement added** | Medium | 8, 10 | Supers legibility, endframe, whether beats survive the crop |
| **Cutdown required (30s to 15s or 6s)** | High. Treat as new script, not as a trim | 8 through 11 for the cutdown | New continuous copy, new timing, own traceability table, own list device audit |
| **Success measure changed** | Medium | 1, 12 | Whether the concept still answers what is now being measured |
| **Brand tone guideline updated** | Medium | 9, 10 | Copy pass, vocabulary check against new verbatim examples |
| **Competitor launches similar work** | Medium | 6, 7 | Territory differentiation. May not require copy change |
| **Budget reduced** | Medium | 7, 8 | Whether the concept is still executable. Concept survives if it was written execution-agnostic |
| **A stakeholder adds a claim late** | Medium | 10, 11 | A10 argument. Two lists. Do not dilute by default |
| **Source brief document superseded by a newer version** | Critical | 0, then propagate | Full re-mine, diff the ten lists, re-run every stage touching a changed item |

**[PRINCIPLE] Read the severity column as a rule about who must be told, not only about how much work it is.** Critical changes require the client to see a revised brief or concept document. High changes require the account team and every affected downstream document (treatment, shot list, voice direction). Medium changes require the affected owners plus a document update. Nothing is too small to update, because the cost of a broken chain is not proportional to the size of the change that broke it.

## B9. PRE-RELEASE AUDIT

Run before the brief or concept document leaves the building.

1. **The forbidding test.** Name three things this brief forbids. If you cannot, it is not a brief.
2. **The reversal test.** Every insight, reversed. None absurd.
3. **The single-minded test.** "And also" fits nowhere in the proposition.
4. **The traceability test.** B3 table complete. No blank "descends from" cells. Every insight either used or explicitly marked unused.
5. **The audience test.** B4.1 statement present. Exactly one primary named. Every unserved audience named with its gap.
6. **The arithmetic test.** Runtime accounting sums exactly. Unaccounted time is 0.0. Every silence over 0.7s is labelled.
7. **The continuous copy test.** The voiceover exists as an unbroken paragraph and was timed aloud before beats were set.
8. **The list device test.** Both audit directions run. Every word matched to a shot, every shot matched to a word or explicitly accepted.
9. **The claim test.** Every claim in the script carries its qualification. Every cleared claim not used is listed under deliberate exclusions.
10. **The language test.** Every language has its own natively generated candidates and its own timed read. No version inherited from another version's word count.
11. **The removal test.** Every removal has a logged backward audit. Search the document for counts in prose ("both", "three", "each of the two") and verify each one.
12. **The tag test.** Every self answered item carries `[FROM DOC]`, `[DERIVED]` or `[ASSUMED]`. No non-negotiable carries `[ASSUMED]`.
13. **The stranger test.** Someone who never attended a meeting can read this document and know what the work is, who it is for, why it is that and not something else, and what it deliberately does not do.

**[PRINCIPLE] Items 4, 5, 8 and 11 are the ones that get skipped, and they are the ones that surface as expensive rework after the shoot.**
