## A1. WHAT THE ROLE ACTUALLY IS

Production design is the only department that builds the thing the camera photographs. Every other department modifies, records, or performs inside a space. The art department makes the space exist.

That gives the role an unusual double character. It is simultaneously the most conceptual job on a crew, responsible for the entire visual world and its internal logic, and the most brutally logistical, responsible for load-in schedules, paint drying times, rental returns, and whether a wall can be removed to fit a dolly.

Rick Carter, who won Academy Awards for *Avatar* and *Lincoln* and has designed eleven features with Steven Spielberg over three decades, frames the job in deliberately anti-glamorous terms. He has said he is not territorial and is not in the business of selling architecture or decoration, and that he does not want the spotlight because there is enough light reflected off the directors he works with. **[SOURCED: Rick Carter, Pushing Pixels interview, 2025]** That is not modesty as a personality trait. It is a working definition. The set is not the deliverable. The story told inside the set is the deliverable.

Stuart Craig, who designed all eight *Harry Potter* films and won three Academy Awards, built his entire practice on credibility rather than spectacle: everything based on reality, architectural styles mixed and matched to serve narrative, with the single hardest problem being to conceive a thousand-year-old castle and then adapt it across four different directors and a still-evolving story. **[SOURCED: Stuart Craig, Empire and Art Departmental coverage]** The lesson embedded there is that a designed world has to survive people changing their minds about the story it houses.

Hannah Beachler, the first Black person to win the Academy Award for Production Design, works at the opposite extreme of documentation. For *Black Panther* she produced a 515-page "Wakanda Bible" and consulted architects, an anthropologist, geologists and physicists to construct a coherent culture, then took a three and a half week research trip to South Africa where real landscape features drove specific set decisions, with Oribi Gorge informing Warrior Falls and a snowy peak relocating an entire tribe from rainforest to mountaintop. She prepared a further 400-page bible over two years for the sequel. **[SOURCED: Hannah Beachler, Motion Picture Association "The Credits," Dwell, IndieWire]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** Between Carter's self-effacement and Beachler's 515 pages sits the actual job: a very large amount of invisible reasoning, delivered as physical space, that the audience is never supposed to consciously notice.

## A2. WHERE THE AUTHORITY BEGINS AND ENDS

The production designer owns **what the world looks like**. That authority is real and broad. It stops at four hard edges.

1. **It stops at the frame.** The DP decides what is photographed and how. A designer who builds a beautiful wall the camera never sees has wasted money, not made art.
2. **It stops at the body.** Costume owns what people wear. The set palette and the wardrobe palette must be negotiated, but neither department can unilaterally set the other.
3. **It stops at the bulb.** The art department owns the light *fixture*. The lighting department owns the bulb, the wiring, the power supply and the exposure. **[SOURCED: Neil Oseman, "5 Tips for Working with Practicals"]**
4. **It stops at the schedule.** The 1st AD owns time. A designer can propose a build; only the schedule can afford one.

### The role split

| Role | Owns | Reports to | Typical question they answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Production Designer** | The overall visual concept of the world, the palette, the build vs location strategy, approval on everything the camera sees | Director | "What does this world look like and why?" |
| **Supervising Art Director / Art Director** | Translating design into buildable drawings, budgets, construction logistics, crew management | Production Designer | "How do we actually build that, for how much, by when?" |
| **Set Designer** | Technical drafting, construction drawings, elevations, models | Art Director | "What are the exact dimensions and joins?" |
| **Set Decorator** | Everything that goes *inside* the built or dressed space: furniture, drapery, artwork, rugs, lighting fixtures as objects, all the layers of stuff | Production Designer | "What is in this room and where does it sit?" |
| **Lead Man / Swing Gang / On-Set Dresser** | Physically moving, placing, resetting and striking dressing | Set Decorator | "Where exactly does this chair go, and does it match yesterday?" |
| **Prop Master** | Anything a performer touches or handles, hero props, action props, consumables | Production Designer (functionally, also the director) | "What does the actor pick up, and do we have three of them?" |
| **Location Manager / Scout** | Finding, securing, permitting and running real-world locations | Producer and Production Designer jointly | "Can we legally, physically and safely shoot here?" |
| **Greensman / Greens Department** | Living plants, trees, grass, landscaping, both real and manufactured | Production Designer | "What is growing in shot and who waters it?" |
| **Construction Coordinator** | Physical build, carpentry, scenic paint, strike | Art Director | "How many build days and how many bodies?" |

The distinctions are institutional, not folkloric. The Art Directors Guild, IATSE Local 800, founded in 1937, represents roughly 3,300 members across four craft classifications: Art Directors (a category that includes Production Designers), Scenic/Title/Graphic Artists, Illustrators/Matte Artists/Previs Artists, and Set Designers/Model Makers. **[SOURCED: Art Directors Guild, adg.org]** Set decorators are represented separately and have been recognised by the Academy since 1947, and have their own professional body, the Set Decorators Society of America, with its own awards. **[SOURCED: SDSA; Academy set decoration credit history]**

The ADG's annual Excellence in Production Design Awards run across roughly fourteen categories spanning theatrical features (split into period, fantasy and contemporary), television (split by format and by period versus contemporary), animated features, music videos, **and commercials**. **[SOURCED: Art Directors Guild awards structure, adg.org]** That last category matters for anyone working in advertising: the industry's own guild treats commercial production design as a real, separately judged discipline, not a lesser version of feature work.

**[PRINCIPLE]** On a small crew, one person holds several of these hats. That is normal. What is not normal, and is the most common structural failure in commercial production, is holding several hats and only doing the fun one. If a single person is designer, decorator and prop master, all three job descriptions still have to be discharged. Deciding a room is "industrial minimal" is the designer's job. Deciding which eleven objects sit on the desk is the decorator's job, and nobody else will do it.

## A3. THE DECISION SET

Every art department decision resolves into a small number of variables. A specification that does not name all of them is incomplete and will produce an argument on the shoot day.

### 1. Build versus location versus hybrid

This is the first and most expensive decision, and it is a spectrum, not a binary.

Jack Fisk, designer for Terrence Malick, David Lynch, Paul Thomas Anderson and Martin Scorsese, has articulated both ends. On Malick's contemporary films he often had no construction department at all and the work became almost entirely the design *of locations*, augmenting interiors with dressing and props. On period work he goes the other way and builds complete structures rather than camera-facing facades, stating: *"We build everything so it can be shot from 360 degrees."* **[SOURCED: Jack Fisk, Film Comment and AwardsWatch interviews]** The reason is not vanity. A 360-degree build removes the constraint that limits every partial build: the director cannot turn around.

Ruth De Jong's *Oppenheimer* is the clearest recent hybrid case. Real Los Alamos was too modernised to shoot as period, so she and her team researched there, then laid out the town with set designer Jim Hewitt using plans, elevations and quarter-inch-scale foam core architectural models, and built the town exteriors at Ghost Ranch on the same mountain range, while shooting certain interiors at the actual Los Alamos where an authentic office interior and exterior already existed. Neither she nor the director wanted CGI set extensions. Over 150 sets were erected across a 57-day shoot. **[SOURCED: Ruth De Jong, BTL News, Deadline, AwardsWatch, Offscreen interviews, 2023 and 2024]**

That is the professional pattern: **build what you cannot find, find what you cannot afford to build, and split a single fictional place across both if the seam can be hidden by an edit.**

| | **Build** | **Location** | **Hybrid** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control of walls, ceiling, floor | Total | None to partial | Partial |
| Control of light | Total, including removable walls and ceilings | Constrained by windows and sun path | Mixed |
| Sound control | Good, if on a proper stage | Often the deciding problem | Mixed |
| Cost profile | High fixed cost, low variable | Low fixed, high variable (fees, permits, restoration) | Both |
| Lead time | Build days plus dressing days | Scout, secure, permit, dress | Longest |
| Risk | Overrun on construction | Weather, noise, access withdrawn, neighbours | Continuity between the two halves |
| Best for | Repeated use, stunt or effects work, extreme palette control | Texture and scale that money cannot fake | A place that needs one impossible feature |

**[PRINCIPLE]** The hybrid's hidden cost is continuity. If a corridor is a location and the room it leads into is a build, then floor finish, skirting profile, door hardware, wall value and light temperature all have to match across two different physical facts. That matching is a design task, and it must be specified before either half is committed.

### 2. Set palette

Palette is the variable with the most cross-departmental blast radius, which is why it is negotiated rather than decreed. See A7.

Sarah Greenwood and set decorator Katie Spencer's *Barbie* is the most instructive modern case study because the process is documented. They had extended conversations with the director and the cinematographer about which pink, running through millennial pinks and salmon pinks, and found that the candidate pinks "started to look really dull and grey" once photographed. They resolved it by starting from a pure fluorescent pigment as the base, which then generated a workable palette of roughly eleven pinks, from which the yellows, blues and greens were derived. Greenwood also described the concept subtractively: they were "taking away black, white, wind, rain, sun, electricity, patina, clutter." **[SOURCED: Sarah Greenwood and Katie Spencer, Gold Derby, IMDb/Filmmaker coverage, A Rabbit's Foot, 2023 and 2024]**

Three transferable lessons sit in that account. First, a palette is chosen against the camera and the lighting, not against a swatch book on a desk. Second, a palette is generated from an anchor, not assembled from a list. Third, a palette is defined as much by what is removed as by what is added, and "clutter" is on that list as a design variable with a dial.

Adam Stockhausen, designing *The Grand Budapest Hotel*, describes the counterweight problem. Once the hotel was committed to pink: *"if you use too much of it, you stop seeing it because it's everywhere and you start taking it for granted."* Yellows and other colours had to be added specifically so the pink could be perceived as pink. **[SOURCED: Adam Stockhausen, KCRW and Backstage interviews]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** A dominant colour needs a contrast colour to remain visible. A palette with only one hue is not a palette, it is a wash.

### 3. Materials and finishes

Materials are what survive compression, small screens and grading after hue has been flattened. They are also what makes a space read as expensive or cheap, new or old, institutional or personal.

The finish decision set for any surface: **sheen** (matte, eggshell, satin, gloss), **value** (how light or dark, which directly drives exposure), **texture** (smooth, brushed, rough, patinated), **age** (fresh, worn, distressed), and **reflectivity** (does it bounce light onto faces, does it show the crew).

Value is the one most often ignored by non-specialists and most often complained about by cinematographers. A wall's paint value is an exposure decision as much as a colour decision. **[PRINCIPLE]** Gloss and high-reflectance surfaces are the two finishes most likely to generate on-day problems: gloss shows the light sources and the crew, and high-reflectance white walls throw uncontrolled fill onto faces and force the DP to flag or negatively fill, costing setup time.

### 4. Furniture and layout

Layout is where design meets blocking, and it is a directorial conversation, not a decorating one. The relevant questions are not "does this look nice." They are: where can the camera stand, how far can a performer walk before hitting a wall, is there a clean eyeline for a two-shot, where does the crew put the equipment cart, and can a door be removed.

Fisk's language for this is worth borrowing: he describes his aim as creating spaces *for actors* rather than for the camera alone, and building fully so performers can cross in and out of real structures. **[SOURCED: Jack Fisk, AwardsWatch interview on *Killers of the Flower Moon*]**

### 5. Practical lighting fixtures as design elements

Any light source that appears in frame is a "practical": a table lamp, a fluorescent strip, a fireplace, a screen, a string of festoon bulbs, headlights, a candle. **[SOURCED: Neil Oseman, RedShark News]** The art department chooses and places them. The lighting department powers, dims and colours them.

Ken Adam's War Room for *Dr. Strangelove* is the canonical demonstration that this decision can *be* the lighting design. Adam sketched while talking with the director, who reacted to a doodle, scrapped three weeks of prior design, and pushed toward a triangle because it is the strongest form in geometry, with concrete, green baize on the table, and, critically, *an unsupported ring above for lighting*. Adam was then asked to design the entire lighting of the room, which became a suspended hoop of harsh fluorescent tubing hung above the table and below the ceiling. He worked out the best angle by sitting up in a chair studying it, and he and the director experimented on successive evenings, standing on chairs, until it was right. The finished set was 130 feet long, 100 feet wide and 35 feet high and took 150 tradesmen to build. **[SOURCED: Ken Adam, BFI Sight and Sound, Web of Stories, AV Club]**

The ring was a set piece, a story object and the key light at once. **[PRINCIPLE]** When a practical is designed rather than merely bought, the lighting plan is partly written before the gaffer arrives, and the scene gains a light source the audience believes in.

### 6. Set dressing density

Density is a deliberate, specifiable quantity, and it is discussed in A6 because it is the single most under-specified variable in the craft.

### 7. Hero props

A hero prop is an object the story depends on and the camera will shoot close. It is distinguished from background props by three properties: it is designed rather than sourced, it exists in multiples (a hero, a stunt or rough version, and usually a spare), and it must survive being photographed at a metre or closer with a macro or long lens.

**[PRINCIPLE]** Every hero prop needs an owner, a multiples count, and a close-up plan. The most common commercial failure is discovering on the day that the one hero object has a fingerprint, a scratch, or a logo in the wrong place, and there is no second one.

## A4. LOCATION SCOUTING AS ITS OWN DISCIPLINE

Location work is not a subset of design. It is a separate profession with its own body of practice, and on many productions the Location Manager reports to the producer, not the designer.

### What makes a location shootable

Location professionals evaluate a space on criteria that have almost nothing to do with how it looks. The consistent checklist across published industry guidance covers:

**Power.** Is there real electrical service (the benchmark commonly cited is a 400-amp panel), or does the production need a generator, and if so where can the generator sit so it is neither in shot nor audible? **[SOURCED: industry location-scouting practice, B&H Explora, StudioBinder, The Location House guides]**

**Sound.** Proximity to airports, motorways, rail lines, schools, construction, and above all HVAC systems. The widely repeated professional rule is that sound is more often the deciding factor than picture, because visual problems can be worked around on a limited budget and audio problems usually cannot. **[SOURCED: same]** **[PRINCIPLE]** Ask specifically whether the building's air conditioning can be switched off, for how long, and who has the key. In hot climates that single answer can kill a location.

**Ceiling height.** Determines whether lighting can be rigged overhead and whether a boom can be flown without dipping into frame. **[SOURCED: same]**

**Window orientation and sun path.** Which way the windows face, and where the sun will actually be on the specific shooting date and hours. Professionals model this with sun-path tools rather than guessing. **[SOURCED: same, with PhotoPills and Sun Seeker cited as standard tools]** **[PRINCIPLE]** A room with big north-facing windows and a room with big west-facing windows are two different lighting jobs, not two versions of the same room.

**Control of the space.** Can the production close it, empty it, repaint it, remove a door, drill a fixing, block a corridor? Is it operating as a business during the shoot? Who can walk in? A location you can only borrow between 6pm and 10pm on a Tuesday is a different scheduling object from one you own for three days.

**Parking, load-in and staging.** Where do trucks park, how far is the carry from vehicle to set, is there a lift and what are its dimensions, is there somewhere to put crew, cast, catering, and a holding area for extras. Carry distance is a cost: every extra hundred metres between the truck and the set is time removed from shooting.

**Legal and practical access.** Permits, permissions, insurance, restoration obligations, neighbours, and the honest question of who else is affected.

### Looks right versus works

**[PRINCIPLE]** The central discipline of location work is separating two judgements that beginners collapse into one.

A location that **looks right** satisfies the design idea in a photograph taken by one person standing still holding a phone.

A location that **works** additionally survives: a crew of a given size inside it, the lighting package it needs, the sound it must record, the schedule it must fit, the number of setups it must yield, the blocking of the scene, and the load-in it demands.

The gap between those two is where scouting lives, and it is why the scout photograph should never be a single hero frame. A proper scout package for each location covers every wall, the ceiling, the floor, the power, the entrances, the view out of each window, and a panorama, plus a compass orientation and the times of day the space was visited.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The three most common ways a beautiful location fails: the sound floor is unusable, the room is too small for the lighting the look requires, or the production cannot actually control access for long enough to shoot the day.

## A5. THE REALISM DIAL IS NOT THE TONE DIAL

This deserves its own section because it is the most frequently confused pair of axes in art direction, and confusing them produces bad briefs and worse arguments.

**Axis 1: Realism / finish level.** Runs from scrappy and lived-in at one end to polished and aspirational at the other. This is a statement about the *material condition* of the world: how worn, how cluttered, how improvised, how expensive, how maintained.

**Axis 2: Tone.** Runs from, for example, warm and comic at one end to cold and severe at the other, or from hopeful to bleak, or from intimate to institutional. This is a statement about *how the film feels*.

They are independent. Any point on one axis can be combined with any point on the other. **[PRINCIPLE]**

The evidence for their independence is everywhere in the sourced record:

- *Barbie* sits at the extreme polished end of Axis 1. Greenwood's team explicitly removed patina, clutter, weather and electricity from the world. The tone it serves is comic and, by the end, melancholy. Polish did not make it glossy in tone. **[SOURCED: Sarah Greenwood]**
- *The Grand Budapest Hotel* is immaculately confected and symmetrical, and it is a story about fascism and loss. **[SOURCED: Adam Stockhausen's documented palette work]**
- Jack Fisk's Malick and Anderson interiors sit at the lived-in end, achieved partly by full 360-degree period-accurate construction, and they serve tones ranging from transcendent to menacing. **[SOURCED: Jack Fisk]**
- Ken Adam's War Room is a scrupulously clean, hard-edged, high-modern space, and the film is a black comedy about extinction. **[SOURCED: Ken Adam]**

**Why this matters operationally.** A brief that says "make it feel warm and human" is a *tone* note. It does not tell the art department whether to buy new furniture or worn furniture. A brief that says "scrappy, real, lived-in" is a *finish* note. It does not tell the art department whether the scene is funny or sad. Both notes are needed and they must be given separately, because they drive different purchases.

The corollary is a common and expensive mistake in commercial work: assuming that because a client wants to look premium, the environment must be pristine. A premium tone can be delivered by a beautifully worn, richly textured, well-used space. Conversely, a pristine environment can read as cold, corporate and unlikeable if the tone note is not separately handled by palette, warmth and human evidence.

**[PRINCIPLE]** Write them as two separate numbers in every brief. Finish level: 1 to 5. Tone: named in words. Never let one imply the other.

## A6. CLUTTER AND MESS AS DELIBERATE DESIGN

"Make it messy" is not a specification. It is an abdication.

### Why mess is harder in a clean modern room

This is a real and non-obvious asymmetry. **[PRINCIPLE]**

In a scrappy, aged, high-texture space, believable disorder is nearly free. The room already has visual noise: chipped paint, mismatched furniture, stains, cabling, uneven surfaces. Adding twenty objects reads instantly as "a place where work happens" because the objects are consistent with everything else in frame.

In a clean modern room, the same twenty objects read as **props placed by a crew**. The reason is consistency of evidence. A pristine white desk in a pristine white room implies a person who tidies. Putting a chaotic pile on that desk creates a contradiction the eye detects immediately, even if the viewer cannot articulate it. To make disorder believable in a clean space you must either:

1. Degrade the space itself (add wear, cabling, coffee rings, sun-faded patches, wall marks, mismatched added furniture), which is a paint and dressing cost, or
2. Make the disorder *specific and recent and explained* (an active project spread out, a deadline, a move, an incident), which is a design and continuity cost, or
3. Accept a lower disorder level and express overwhelm through other means (density of people, scale of the task visible on screens and boards, evidence of hours rather than evidence of chaos).

All three are decisions. None of them happens from the note "make it messy."

### The fixed minimum clutter checklist

The professional fix is to replace the adjective with a **countable, area-specific list**. Rather than "an overwhelmed workspace," the specification names a zone and enumerates a floor.

Example of the form (generic, not tied to any project):

> **Zone: primary work surface, one occupant, mid-project, high pressure.**
> Minimum dressing floor, all present before the first take:
> - 2 or more used drinking vessels, at least one with residue
> - 1 stack of paper, 15cm or more, not squared off
> - 3 or more loose pens/markers, at least one uncapped
> - 1 open notebook with visible handwriting
> - Visible cable disorder: 2 or more cables not routed
> - 1 personal object with no work function
> - 1 item of food evidence (wrapper, container, crumbs)
> - Wall or board surface: 6 or more affixed items, not aligned
> - Floor: 1 or more objects that should not be on a floor
> - Seat: 1 garment or bag left on or over it

That list is buyable. A runner can source it. A decorator can dress it in twenty minutes and photograph it for continuity. "Make it messy" is none of those things.

**[PRINCIPLE]** Three further rules govern believable disorder.

**Disorder must have a vector.** Real mess accumulates in a direction: toward the person, toward the door, toward the bin, away from the working area. Randomly scattered objects read as scattered by a crew. Objects piled where a human hand would put them read as lived.

**Disorder must survive continuity.** Every object in a mess is a continuity item. Mess is photographed before the first take and restored between takes. An undocumented mess will be different in the reverse, and the edit will show it. This is why the fixed list exists: it is also the reset list.

**Disorder must not eat the frame's subject.** The set decorator's density is negotiated against the DP's lens. High density behind a subject shot at a wide focal length and deep stop becomes visual noise competing with a face. The same density behind a long lens at a wide stop becomes texture. Density is therefore a joint decision with the DP, not a unilateral one.

## A7. PALETTE NEGOTIATION

The set palette cannot be locked alone. It is a three-way agreement with costume and camera, and in commercial work a four-way agreement including brand.

### Against wardrobe

**[PRINCIPLE]** The rule is separation of figure from ground. If a costume and the wall behind it share a hue and a value, the performer disappears into the set. If they share a hue but differ sharply in value, the performer reads as an accent. If they oppose in hue, the performer pops and the frame reads as designed.

The practical protocol is that the two departments exchange physical samples, not descriptions, and then photograph them together under the intended lighting before either locks. A shared digital swatch is not sufficient, because dye lots, sheen and camera response all shift the result. This is exactly the discipline Greenwood's team used when their pinks "started to look really dull and grey" once tested rather than imagined. **[SOURCED: Sarah Greenwood]**

Typical resolution when the two clash: **the set yields on hue, the costume yields on value.** Set repaints are cheaper than recasting a garment, but a garment's tone can usually be swapped from a rail. In practice the director arbitrates by asking which element carries story meaning. If the colour is characterising the person, costume wins and the set adjusts. If the colour is characterising the place, the set wins and costume adjusts.

### Against the DP's lighting temperature

Colour is a product of surface and illuminant. A wall painted a neutral warm grey under 3200K tungsten and the same wall under 5600K daylight are two different colours on camera. **[PRINCIPLE]**

Therefore the palette conversation with the DP has to cover four things, and covering only the first is the classic amateur error:

1. **Hue** of the surfaces.
2. **Value** of the surfaces, because value drives exposure and contrast ratio.
3. **Illuminant colour temperature**, both the ambient plan and each practical's temperature, because that is what the surfaces will be seen under.
4. **Sheen and reflectivity**, because that determines whether the surface throws light back onto faces and whether it shows the rig.

A published account of how designers and DPs actually work together confirms the shape of this: production designers need to know which lenses the DP plans to use, the lighting design, the expected camera movement and the photographic tone the DP wants, and the two work together specifically on the placement, power and colour temperature of every practical source. The designer continues watching dailies through the shoot and adjusts props and dressing in response to how the lighting is actually landing. **[SOURCED: Production Designers Collective and In Depth Cine practitioner accounts; Mark Vargo, "Key Collaborators: The Production Designer"]**

### Against brand colour reservations

This is the constraint feature designers do not have and commercial designers can never escape.

**[PRINCIPLE]** In branded work, one or more colours are *reserved*. A brand's primary colour is typically reserved for the brand: the product, the logo, the packaging, and a small number of sanctioned accents. Anything else in that hue competes with the brand asset and dilutes it. Some clients also reserve a secondary and prohibit competitor-associated colours outright.

That produces a hard rule set the art department must receive in writing before palette lock:

| Category | Meaning for the set |
|---|---|
| **Reserved (brand primary)** | May not appear on walls, large furniture, or large dressing. May appear only on the product itself and on approved brand surfaces. |
| **Permitted brand secondary** | May be used, usually at limited coverage, often specified as a percentage of frame. |
| **Neutral field** | The colours the set is actually built from. Chosen to make the reserved colour maximally legible. |
| **Prohibited** | Competitor-associated hues, and hues that clash with the product's own packaging. |

The design consequence is the same one Stockhausen described in reverse: to make the brand colour visible you must *withhold* it from the environment. A set that is full of the brand colour makes the product invisible. **[SOURCED: Adam Stockhausen's stated principle, applied]**

## A8. PRACTICAL FIXTURES BECOME THE LIGHTING PLAN

Expanding A3.5 because it is the highest-leverage collaboration the art department has.

The division of labour is precise: **the fixture is art department, the bulb and the power are lighting department.** **[SOURCED: Neil Oseman]** In practice that means the designer's choice of lamp silently determines a large part of the DP's options.

What the fixture choice actually decides:

- **Where light can come from in-frame.** A wall sconce at 1.6m and a table lamp at 0.8m produce entirely different facial modelling.
- **How much light is plausible.** A single candle and a bank of overhead fluorescents set completely different ceilings on how bright a scene can honestly be.
- **The colour of the world.** Warm tungsten practicals, cool LED strips and green-spiking older fluorescents each impose a cast that the colourist inherits.
- **Whether the DP can hide units.** A practical is also an alibi: it justifies a bigger unit just out of frame pointing in the same direction. A set with no practicals gives the DP no motivated sources, which forces either flat ambient light or lighting the audience cannot explain.
- **Screens.** Monitors, phones and televisions are practicals. Their brightness, content, refresh behaviour and colour are art department decisions with direct exposure and flicker consequences.

**[PRINCIPLE]** A designed environment should always answer the question *"where is the light in this room coming from, in the world of the story?"* before the gaffer asks it. Ken Adam answered it by designing a lighting ring as the room's central sculptural object. **[SOURCED: Ken Adam]** Most projects answer it with three well-chosen lamps. Both are the same act.

Practical fixture spec, minimum fields: fixture type, position (with height), quantity, bulb type and wattage requested, colour temperature requested, whether it must be dimmable, whether it appears in frame or only its light does, and whether the lighting department needs to replace the bulb (they very often will).

## A9. WHAT A SECOND LOCATION ACTUALLY COSTS

A second location is not a second address. It is a second production.

Adding one triggers, at minimum:

1. **Either a company move or a separate call time.** A company move relocates the entire cast, crew and equipment during a single shooting day and is one of the most time-consuming transitions in production, taking anywhere from thirty minutes to several hours depending on distance and complexity. Published scheduling guidance is blunt that grouping scenes by location to avoid mid-day relocations can save on the order of 20% of total daily shooting hours, and that a poorly planned move can derail a schedule, exhaust the crew, and cut into shooting time. **[SOURCED: film scheduling practice, Jungle Software, HowToFilmSchool, Studiovity, First Draft Filmworks guidance]** The alternative, a separate shoot day or a separate call, converts the move into an extra day of crew, kit and catering.
2. **Its own lighting package, or a shared one that must be struck and re-rigged.** Resource planning explicitly decides how long a lighting package stays on the truck and how tightly scenes are grouped, because every additional location means repeated lighting setups. **[SOURCED: same]**
3. **Its own dressing pass.** New dressing, new load-in, new set-up, new strike, new restoration. Dressing does not travel for free.
4. **Its own scout, permit, insurance, and fee.**
5. **Its own risk profile.** Weather, noise, access, neighbours, all separately.
6. **Its own continuity obligations** if any character or object crosses between the two.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The honest rule for anyone specifying a shoot: a second location is roughly a second half-day of production time even when it is ten minutes away, and it is frequently a second full day once dressing and lighting are counted. The art department must be the department that says this out loud during design, because by the time the schedule is drafted the decision is already sunk.

## A11. VOCABULARY GLOSSARY

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Art department** | The whole design organisation: designer, art directors, set designers, graphics, construction, set dec, props, greens. |
| **Build day** | A scheduled day of construction, distinct from a shoot day. Builds are counted in build days plus paint and dry time. |
| **Company move** | Relocating the entire unit from one location to another, usually within a shooting day. The most expensive routine event in a schedule. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Continuity (art dept sense)** | The obligation that every dressed object is in the same state and position across takes, angles and days. Enforced with reference stills. |
| **Dressing / set dressing** | Everything placed inside a space that is not structure and not handled by an actor. Owned by the set decorator. |
| **Greens** | Living or artificial plant material, and the greensman/greens department who source, place, water and maintain it. **[SOURCED: Greensman, standard industry definition]** |
| **Hero prop** | The story-critical object that will be shot close and must be perfect. Exists in multiples. |
| **Lead man** | The set decorator's head of crew, running the swing gang. |
| **Load-in / load-out** | Getting the department's material into and out of a space. A real scheduled cost, driven by carry distance, lifts and access. |
| **Practical** | Any light source visible in frame. Fixture is art department; bulb, wiring and power are lighting department. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Recce** | British and European term for a location visit or scout. Used interchangeably with "scout." |
| **Set dec** | Shorthand for the set decoration department and its work. |
| **Strike** | Removing a set or dressing after shooting, and restoring a location to its prior condition. |
| **Swing gang** | The crew who make changes to a set: building requested additions, striking furniture, and doing last-minute alterations before the camera rolls. **[SOURCED: standard industry definition]** |
| **Swing set** | The non-permanent portions of a set, the parts that can be moved or removed for a given shot. Also used to mean a set redressed to become a different location. **[SOURCED: Art Departmental glossary]** |
| **Tech scout** | The late-prep visit where department heads (director, DP, gaffer, key grip, 1st AD, designer, sound, locations) walk the actual location together and commit to positions, power, rigging and blocking. The last cheap moment to change anything physical. |
| **Wild wall** | A set wall built to be removable so the camera can shoot from where the wall would be. Only exists on builds, which is one of the main arguments for building. |

## A12. HOW COMMERCIAL AND ADVERTISING ART DIRECTION DIFFERS

The craft is the same. The constraints are not. Note first that the Art Directors Guild judges commercials as their own award category, alongside features, television, music videos and animation, which is the industry's own acknowledgement that this is a distinct professional practice rather than a smaller version of feature work. **[SOURCED: adg.org]**

**Turnaround.** Feature prep runs months. Commercial prep frequently runs days to two or three weeks. The consequence is not "less design," it is *different sequencing*: sourcing replaces building, decisions are made from references rather than developed through iteration, and the storyboard is treated as binding. Published commercial-production practice is explicit that storyboarding is not optional and that the board is the last point where changes are cheap. **[SOURCED: commercial production practice, Decade Pictures and Assemble production guides]**

**The product is a cast member.** Hero product staging is its own sub-discipline: surface condition, label alignment, fingerprints, condensation, pour and melt behaviour, and the fact that macro and long-lens close-ups expose flaws invisible to the eye. Commercial scheduling practice commonly groups all product close-ups into a single block for exactly this reason, and slots the anchor hero shot into a part of the day when the team is sharp but not exhausted. **[SOURCED: same]**

**Brand environment rules.** Beyond colour reservations (A7), commercial work inherits a rule layer features do not have: competitor products cannot appear, third-party logos must be cleared or removed, regulated categories have depiction rules, and in many markets the client's own brand guidelines constrain materials, typography on any in-shot graphic, and even the finish of surfaces near the product.

**Multiple deliverables from one build.** A single set is expected to yield a hero film, cutdowns, vertical crops and stills. That has direct design consequences: the set must be dressed to survive a 9:16 crop (where the top and bottom of frame become important and the sides vanish), and it must hold up to stills photography, which is far less forgiving of dressing than motion.

**Smaller crews, merged roles.** In most commercial production the designer, decorator and prop master are the same person or a team of two, and the location manager function is absorbed by the producer. The job descriptions do not disappear (see A2).

**Client presence.** The set is a venue for client attendance. Design must accommodate a video village, a client area, and the reality that approvals may happen live on the day. **[PRINCIPLE]** Build the approval path into the design: identify in advance which elements are locked and which can be adjusted on the day without collapsing the schedule, and say so before the client arrives.

## B1. THE QUESTIONS THIS ROLE ASKS ON RECEIPT OF A SCRIPT AND SHOT LIST

Ask in this order. Do not skip to palette.

**Tier 1: World and intent**
1. What is the world, in one sentence that is not a genre label?
2. What period is it, and how strictly? (Accuracy is a dial the director sets.)
3. What is the tone, named in words?
4. What is the finish level, 1 to 5, scrappy to pristine? (Separate answer from question 3. See A5.)
5. What is the audience meant to understand about the people who occupy these spaces, from the spaces alone?

**Tier 2: Enumeration**
6. List every distinct physical zone that appears on camera. Not every scene, every *zone*. Two scenes in the same room are one zone; one scene that crosses a threshold is two zones.
7. For each zone: how many setups, how many script pages, and does the camera ever see the ceiling, the floor, or out of a window?
8. How many humans occupy each zone at maximum? (This drives furniture count, desk count, and the dressing footprint.)
9. Which objects are handled by a performer? Which of those are story-critical?

**Tier 3: Physical reality**
10. Build, location, or hybrid, per zone?
11. If location: is it found, secured, or aspirational? What is the fallback?
12. What is the shooting date, and therefore where is the sun?
13. How many locations in total, and does any shooting day contain more than one? (See B7.)
14. What is the total art department budget, split between build, dressing, props and locations?

**Tier 4: Cross-department locks**
15. What is the DP's lens plan and lighting approach per zone? (Wide and deep, or long and shallow, changes dressing density.)
16. What is the wardrobe palette, in physical samples?
17. What is the colourist's grade intention? (A heavily desaturated grade changes which hues are worth paying for.)
18. What is the schedule, per zone, per day?
19. What are the brand colour reservations and prohibitions, in writing?
20. How many performers and background, and when is that number final?

**Tier 5: Risk**
21. What can be changed on the day without breaking the schedule, and what cannot?
22. What is the single most likely thing to go wrong per zone?

## B2. DECISION CHECKLIST IN DEPENDENCY ORDER

Each step depends on the one above it. Do not lock a lower step before an upper step is agreed.

1. **World statement.** One sentence. Agreed with the director.
2. **Tone and finish level.** Two separate values. Agreed with the director.
3. **Zone enumeration.** The complete list of distinct physical zones. Agreed with the 1st AD and the DP against the shot list.
4. **Build / location / hybrid per zone.** Agreed with the producer, because it is a money decision.
5. **Locations shortlisted and scouted** for every location zone, against the shootability checklist (A4), not against photographs.
6. **Anchor colour chosen.** One anchor, derived from research or from the brand's reserved colour, then tested on camera under the intended illuminant.
7. **Full palette derived from the anchor**, including the counterweight hue. Cross-checked against wardrobe samples and brand reservations.
8. **Materials, finishes, values and sheens** specified per surface per zone. Cross-checked with the DP for exposure and reflectivity.
9. **Practical fixtures specified** per zone: type, position, height, quantity, colour temperature, dimmability. Agreed with the DP and gaffer.
10. **Layout and furniture plan** per zone, drawn, checked against blocking and camera positions with the director and DP.
11. **Dressing density and the minimum clutter checklist** per zone, written as countable items. Checked against the DP's lens plan.
12. **Hero props identified**, with multiples counts and owners.
13. **Graphics, signage and screen content** specified, with clearance status.
14. **Build days, dressing days, load-in and strike** placed on the schedule with the 1st AD.
15. **Continuity reference protocol** agreed: who photographs each dressed zone before the first take.
16. **Contingency named** per zone.

## B5. PER-ZONE ENUMERATION DISCIPLINE

This is the central operating rule of the seat, and it is where under-specified plans are caught.

**Rule 1: Every distinct physical zone that appears on camera gets its own spec.** A zone is a bounded physical area the camera can be inside or pointed at. A corridor, a room, a doorway threshold, an exterior approach, a stairwell, and a corner of a larger room treated as a separate set are all zones.

**Rule 2: Each zone's spec is self-contained.** Never write "same as Zone 3." The phrase hides every difference that matters, and when Zone 3 changes, Zone 5 silently becomes wrong. Repeat the full spec for every zone even when it is nearly identical. Redundancy is cheaper than a wrong wall.

**Rule 3: Physical description is a separate field from everything else.** The most common under-specification failure is describing a place entirely by its contents and never by its shape. A spec must be able to answer *"what does this place look like"* on its own, before any question about props, lighting or risk is asked.

The mandatory field set per zone:

| Field | What it must contain | Failure if omitted |
|---|---|---|
| **Zone ID and name** | Unique, human-readable | Nobody can reference it in a schedule |
| **Physical description** | Shape, size in metres, ceiling height, floor material, wall material and finish, window count and orientation, door count and position, architectural period and condition | The room cannot be found or built. This is the field most often missing. |
| **Build / location / hybrid** | With the named location or stage, and status: aspirational, shortlisted, secured | The producer cannot cost it |
| **Palette** | Anchor hue, counterweight, wall value, floor value, permitted accents, prohibited colours | Painters guess |
| **Materials and finishes** | Per surface: material, sheen, condition | The DP gets a reflectivity surprise |
| **Furniture and layout** | Item list with counts and a plan sketch, plus camera-position notes | Blocking collapses on the day |
| **Practical fixtures** | Type, position, height, count, colour temperature, dimmable yes/no, in-frame yes/no | The lighting plan has no motivation |
| **Dressing density and minimum clutter checklist** | Countable, buyable list | "Generic dressing" arrives and satisfies nobody |
| **Hero props in this zone** | With multiples count and owner | One scratched object stops the day |
| **Graphics and screens** | Content, clearance status, brightness | Legal problem or flicker problem |
| **Occupancy** | Max humans in frame, seats required | Not enough chairs |
| **Access and logistics** | Load-in route, lift dimensions, carry distance, power, parking | Time lost before the camera turns over |
| **Sound notes** | Noise sources, HVAC control, hard-surface reverb | Unusable audio |
| **Risk and contingency** | Most likely failure, and the fallback | No plan B |
| **Continuity owner** | Named person who photographs and resets | Mismatched reverses |

**Rule 4: Lighting, dressing and risk are three separate questions and never merged.** A spec that answers "moody, cluttered, might be noisy" in one line has answered none of them at a usable level.

## B6. STANDARD TENSIONS AND TYPICAL RESOLUTIONS

| Tension | The clash | Typical director resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Set palette vs wardrobe palette** | The performer and the wall share a hue and value; the performer sinks into the background. Or both departments independently chose the same "hero" colour and the frame has no hierarchy. | Ask which element carries the story. If the colour characterises the person, wardrobe holds and the set shifts value or hue. If the colour characterises the place, the set holds and wardrobe pulls from a rail. Resolve with a photographed camera test, not a debate. |
| **Practical fixtures vs exposure** | The designer wants a warm, dim, atmospheric lamp; the DP needs enough light to hold a stop, or needs the practical dimmed so far it stops reading as a light source. Or the practical is in frame and blows out. | Almost always resolved technically rather than editorially: swap the bulb (lighting department's call), add a dimmer, add ND or a diffusion sock, or shift the fixture 20cm out of frame and keep the pool of light. The director rules only when the fixture is a story object, in which case the fixture stays and the exposure adapts. |
| **Dressing density vs the lens** | The designer dresses richly; the DP shoots wide and deep and the background competes with the face. | Reduce density behind the subject specifically, not everywhere. Keep density in the frame's periphery and in cutaways. Director arbitrates on whether the environment or the performance is the point of the shot. |
| **Wall value vs contrast ratio** | Bright white walls throw uncontrolled fill, flatten the image and cost setup time to negatively fill. | Drop the wall value one or two steps. This is almost always the cheapest fix in the whole production and the designer should offer it before being asked. |
| **Build ambition vs schedule** | The design requires build days the schedule does not contain. | Reduce scope to what the camera sees, or convert one build to a location, or convert two zones into one swing set. The 1st AD's number is real. |
| **Brand colour reservation vs design palette** | The designer's chosen anchor is the reserved brand hue, or a prohibited competitor hue. | Non-negotiable in commercial work. The set yields. Re-derive the palette from a permitted anchor and use the reserved hue only on the product. |
| **Location authenticity vs control** | The perfect real space cannot be controlled: cannot repaint, cannot kill the HVAC, cannot close it. | Weigh against a plainer space that can be fully controlled and dressed. Control usually wins, because a controllable plain room can be designed into the right room, while an uncontrollable perfect room cannot be shot. |
| **Client on-the-day changes vs locked design** | A live approval request that would break the schedule. | Pre-agree the locked list. Offer a menu of pre-approved on-the-day adjustments so the answer is never a flat no. |
| **Greens and organic material vs shoot duration** | Real plants and cut flowers degrade over a long day under lights. | Specify replacements, or specify high-quality artificial, or schedule the greens-critical shots early. This is a scheduling conversation, not a purchasing one. |

## B7. FAILURE MODES AND UNDER-SPECIFICATION TELLS

Treat each of these as a hard stop. If one is present, the plan is not finished.

**Tell 1: A zone described only by its props.** "The office has a cluttered desk, a whiteboard, and a coffee machine." This does not tell anyone the room's size, shape, ceiling height, window orientation, wall colour, floor material or architectural period. It cannot be scouted, built, or costed. **Fix: mandate the physical description field independently of the contents field.**

**Tell 2: "Generic dressing" or "dress it appropriately."** Nobody can buy this. It produces whatever the runner found. **Fix: the countable minimum clutter checklist (A6). If a note cannot be handed to a person with a budget and a van, it is not a specification.**

**Tell 3: "Same as Zone 3."** Hides differences and breaks silently when Zone 3 changes. **Fix: self-contained specs, always.**

**Tell 4: A palette named in adjectives.** "Warm, earthy, natural." Warm to whom, at what value, under what illuminant? **Fix: an anchor plus a counterweight plus wall and floor values, tested on camera.**

**Tell 5: No practicals listed for an interior night zone.** The lighting has no motivation and the gaffer will invent it, usually as flat ambient. **Fix: every interior zone gets a practical schedule or an explicit note that it is lit entirely from off-camera sources and why.**

**Tell 6: "Make it messy" with no vector, no count and no reset list.** Produces crew-scattered objects and continuity failure. **Fix: A6.**

**Tell 7: Tone note used as a finish note, or vice versa.** "Premium" is a tone note being asked to do a finish job. **Fix: two separate values, always. See A5.**

**Tell 8: A location described but never shootability-tested.** Photographs only, no power, no sound, no ceiling height, no compass bearing, no access notes. **Fix: A4 checklist per location.**

**Tell 9: An added location with no schedule consequence stated.** This is the most expensive silent failure in the entire seat. **Fix: A9. State the company move or extra call time explicitly, every time.**

**Tell 10: Hero props with no multiples count.** One object, no spare, shot in macro. **Fix: minimum of hero plus one spare, more if handled or consumed.**

**Tell 11: No continuity owner named.** Dressing drifts across takes and days. **Fix: name a person per zone.**

**Tell 12: Palette locked before wardrobe samples were seen.** Guarantees a clash discovered on the day. **Fix: dependency order in B2.**

**Tell 13: Ceiling and floor unaddressed when the shot list contains a low or high angle.** **Fix: cross-check every zone against the shot list for whether ceiling or floor is seen.**

**Tell 14: Screen content unspecified.** Monitors and phones are practicals, graphics, clearance items and exposure risks at once. **Fix: specify content, brightness and clearance for every screen.**

## B9. TRIGGER LIST: WHAT FORCES A RE-OPEN

When any of these upstream changes lands, the plan is reopened. The discipline is distinguishing what must be **genuinely re-derived** from what can be **merely relabeled**. Relabeling something that needed re-derivation is the most common way a plan silently becomes wrong.

| Trigger | Genuinely re-derive | Merely relabel | Scheduling implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Location swap (same zone, different place)** | Physical description in full. Ceiling height, window orientation and compass bearing, floor and wall materials, power, sound, access, load-in, parking. Practical fixture positions and heights. Layout and camera positions. Wall values, because the new walls are a different colour. Sun path. Risk. | Zone ID, zone name, palette *intent*, tone, finish level, hero prop list. | Re-scout and re-tech-scout. If the new location is a different address on the same day as another zone, a company move now exists that did not before. |
| **New second location added** | Everything for the new zone from scratch. Also re-derive the *existing* zones' schedule allocation, because the day now has less time in it. | Nothing about the existing zones' design. | This is the big one. Either a company move (30 minutes to several hours, plus repeated lighting setup and a second dressing pass) or a separate call time or day. State it explicitly in section 8 of the plan. Never let an added location enter the plan without a stated time cost. **[SOURCED: scheduling practice, A9]** |
| **Ensemble added / headcount up (more people, more desks, more zones to dress)** | Occupancy per zone. Furniture and seating counts. Dressing footprint, because more occupants means more personal-object clutter and more surfaces. Layout, because blocking changes. Possibly the zone count itself, if the new people occupy areas previously out of frame. Load-in volume. Budget. | World statement, tone, finish level, palette. | More furniture is more load-in and more dressing time. If new physical areas come into frame, treat each as a new zone with a full spec, not as an extension of an existing one. |
| **Palette pivot** | Wall values and hues per surface. Materials and finishes. Dressing item selection (fabric, upholstery, artwork, books, packaging). Practical fixture colour temperature, if the anchor changed warmth. Wardrobe cross-check, fully re-run. Camera test, fully re-run. | Layout, furniture counts, physical descriptions, access logistics, sound notes. | Repaint days. Possible re-purchase of dressing. If anything was already bought or painted, this is a hard cost, so pivot before purchase or accept the write-off. |
| **Framing rule change that widens what is seen** (wider lens, wider aspect, ceiling now visible, floor now visible, 9:16 crop added) | The extent of every build (walls that were not needed now are). Ceiling treatment. Floor treatment. Dressing coverage across the newly visible area. Practical positions, because more of the room's light sources are now in frame. Density, because a wider lens changes what competes with the subject. | Palette, tone, finish level, hero props. | Additional build and additional dressing pass. A widened frame is one of the few changes that can add build days late, and it must be flagged to the 1st AD immediately. |
| **DP changes the lens or lighting plan** | Dressing density. Wall sheen and value. Practical positions and dimming. Whether a wall must be wild. | Physical description, palette intent, occupancy. | Usually absorbed, unless a wild wall is newly required, which is a build change. |
| **Wardrobe palette change** | Wall and large-surface hue and value in every zone that character appears in. Re-run the camera test. | Everything structural. | Repaint risk if paint is already done. This is why wardrobe cross-check sits before paint in B2. |
| **Grade intention changes (e.g. heavy desaturation added)** | Whether subtle hue distinctions are still worth paying for. Value separation may need to increase to compensate for lost hue separation. | Layout, logistics, occupancy. | Minimal schedule impact if caught in prep. Severe if caught in the grade. |
| **Schedule compression (a day removed)** | Build scope. Which zones become swing sets. Which zones convert from build to location or from location to a corner of an existing set. Dressing density (the first thing to cut is depth of dressing in zones with few setups). | World statement, tone, palette. | The whole point. Every removal must be traded against something specific, not absorbed silently. |
| **Brand rules issued or changed** | Palette anchor if the anchor is now reserved or prohibited. All in-frame graphics. All screen content. Product staging. Competitor removal sweep across every zone. | Physical descriptions, layout, logistics. | A late brand rule change can invalidate purchased dressing. Get the rules in writing before section 4 of the plan is locked. |
| **Weather forecast change on an exterior or window-dependent zone** | Sun path assumptions. Practical and supplementary lighting. Greens condition. Cover-set plan. | Palette, layout. | May force a schedule reorder, which is the 1st AD's call, but the art department must have a dressed cover set ready if one is nominated. |

**[PRINCIPLE]** The test for re-derivation versus relabeling is a single question: *did the physical facts change?* If the shape, size, surfaces, light sources, occupancy or access of a space changed, the spec must be rewritten from the physical description down. If only the name, the reference image or the intent language changed, relabeling is honest. Anything in between should be treated as re-derivation, because the cost of an unnecessary rewrite is an hour and the cost of a missed one is a shoot day.

## B10. STANDING RULES FOR THE SEAT

1. Never lock palette before physical wardrobe samples have been seen and photographed under the intended illuminant.
2. Never write "same as" in a zone spec.
3. Never describe a zone by its contents without describing its shape.
4. Never accept "make it messy." Convert it to a countable checklist before agreeing.
5. Never add a location without stating the company move or the extra call time in the same sentence.
6. Never specify a hero prop without a multiples count.
7. Never leave an interior night zone without a practical schedule or an explicit note saying why it has none.
8. Always give tone and finish level as two separate values.
9. Always name a continuity owner per zone.
10. Always offer the cheap fix before the expensive one. Dropping a wall value one step solves more problems than any other single intervention available to this department.
