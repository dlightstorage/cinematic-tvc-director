## A1. WHAT THESE ROLES ACTUALLY ARE

There are two seats here, and confusing them is the first and most common error.

**The 1st AD owns time.** The role includes tracking daily progress against the production schedule, arranging logistics, preparing daily call sheets, checking cast and crew, maintaining order on set, and taking care of the health and safety of the crew. **[SOURCED: Directors Guild of America role definitions, as summarised in the standard industry description of the assistant director]** The 1st AD is directly responsible to the director and runs the floor.

**The line producer and the UPM own money.** In the American system, the unit production manager is the DGA-approved title for the top below-the-line staff position, responsible for the administration of a feature or television production. Non-DGA productions call the same job production manager or production supervisor. Sometimes the line producer and the UPM are the same person. **[SOURCED: DGA 2011 Basic Agreement, section 1-302 and following]** The line producer is the producer who generates and continuously reviews the budget line by line, and is directly responsible for the financial aspects of a production. **[SOURCED: StudioBinder, line producer definition]**

The two seats are institutionally paired. The 1st AD and the UPM are two of the highest below-the-line technical roles in filmmaking, as opposed to the creative or above-the-line roles, and in that strict institutional sense the role of the 1st AD is non-creative. **[SOURCED: DGA rate card classification]**

That word "non-creative" is a contract term, not a description of the work. It means the seat does not own authorship. It does not mean the seat does not make judgment calls. The judgment call the 1st AD makes twenty times a day, on how long a setup will take, is one of the hardest estimations in the industry and one that no software has replaced.

The DGA Basic Agreement enumerates the duties that the employer must assign to either the UPM or the 1st Assistant Director. Without limitation, these include:

- Prepare breakdown and preliminary shooting schedule
- Prepare or coordinate the budget
- Oversee preliminary search and survey of all locations and completion of business arrangements for the same
- Assist in the preparation of the production to ensure continuing efficiency
- Supervise completion of the Production Report for each day's work, showing work covered and the status of the production, and arrange for distribution of that report
- Coordinate arrangements for transportation and housing of cast, crew and staff
- Oversee the securing of releases and negotiate for locations and personnel
- Maintain liaison with local authorities regarding locations and the operation of the company
- Set up the production office and systems in coordination with the Production Office Coordinator
- Work with the various unions to submit and finalise contracts
- Handle above-the-line issues
- Work with the accounting team so the production is set up to be eligible for tax credits or rebates where applicable

**[SOURCED: DGA 2011 Basic Agreement, pp. 13 to 15]**

Read that list carefully. Two things jump out. First, "prepare breakdown and preliminary shooting schedule" and "prepare or coordinate the budget" sit in the same enumerated list, assigned to the same pair of people. **The schedule and the budget are one artifact viewed from two angles.** Second, the list contains almost nothing about the shoot day itself. The overwhelming majority of the job is prep.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The blunt summary of the relationship: the AD works to bring a production in on schedule, the line producer or UPM works to bring it in on budget, and because those two goals are usually the same goal expressed in different units, the relationship between the roles is simultaneously a tension and a symbiosis. **[SOURCED: Wrapbook, "Assistant Director: 21 Most Asked Questions"]**

### The AD department, role by role

| Role | Owns | Reports to | The question they answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1st AD** | The schedule, the floor, set safety, the tempo of the day | Director creatively, producer contractually | "Can we shoot that, in that order, in the hours we have?" |
| **2nd AD** | Call sheets, cast wrangling through hair, makeup and wardrobe, background, paperwork, the world off the set | 1st AD | "Is everyone where they need to be, and is it documented?" |
| **2nd 2nd AD / 3rd AD** | Background action, lockups, base camp to set movement, PAs | 2nd AD and 1st AD | "Is the frame clear and is the background moving on cue?" |
| **Key PA / Additional AD** | Overflow of any of the above, large crowd control | AD department | "Who is covering the gap?" |
| **Line Producer** | The budget, hiring, vendor deals, delivery schedule, the money answer to any creative question | Executive producer or client | "What does that cost and can we afford it?" |
| **UPM / Production Manager** | Below-the-line execution of the budget, logistics, reports, compliance | Producer / line producer | "How do we physically make that happen for that number?" |
| **Production Coordinator** | The office, travel, bookings, distribution of paperwork | UPM | "Is it booked, confirmed and circulated?" |
| **Production Accountant** | Cost reports, purchase orders, actuals, hot costs | Line producer | "What have we actually spent?" |

The sub-role distinctions vary by territory. In North America the split between a 2nd 2nd AD and a 3rd AD is common. British and Australian productions instead hire a second 2nd AD of equal experience to divide the same duties, and their 3rd ADs carry different responsibilities, so the terms are not synonymous across territories. **[SOURCED: UK National Careers Service and ScreenSkills role definitions]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** On a small crew, one person holds several of these hats. That is normal. What is not normal, and is the most common structural failure in commercial production, is a shoot with no one in the 1st AD seat at all, on the theory that the producer or the director can absorb it. The result is predictable: the day is scheduled optimistically by the person who most wants it to be true, and nobody is contractually the adult in the room when it starts slipping at 11am.

## A2. WHERE THE AUTHORITY BEGINS AND ENDS

The 1st AD's authority is real, narrow, and absolute inside its boundary.

**What the 1st AD owns**

1. **The order of the day.** What is shot when. The director owns what the shot is. The AD owns when it happens and how long it gets.
2. **The call.** Who is called, at what time, to what address. The call sheet is issued under the AD department's authority and approved by the 1st AD.
3. **The floor.** Silence, lockups, who is allowed to speak, when a take begins. The 1st AD calls the roll.
4. **Safety.** Discussed below, because it is a different kind of authority.

**Where it stops**

1. **It stops at the frame.** The AD does not decide the shot. If the director wants another take, the AD's move is to report the consequence, not to refuse.
2. **It stops at the money.** The AD can identify that an added location costs three hours. Only the line producer or the client can decide to pay for those three hours.
3. **It stops at craft quality.** The AD does not get to declare that the makeup is finished or the set is dressed. Those calls belong to the department heads. The AD gets to say how much time remains.
4. **It stops at the client, on commercials.** On features the director makes the call on whether it is time to move on. On commercials the client is king. The AD must check with the producer, who reports whether the client is happy to move on, and getting that wrong is one of the fastest ways to lose a shoot day's worth of goodwill. **[SOURCED: Wrapbook, "Assistant Director: 21 Most Asked Questions"]**

### The safety authority is different in kind

Every other authority in the AD seat is delegated and negotiable. Safety is not.

The health and safety of the crew is listed among the core duties of the assistant director alongside scheduling and call sheets. **[SOURCED: standard DGA and ScreenSkills role definitions]** In practice, across both the US and UK systems, the 1st AD is the person who runs the safety briefing at the top of the day, who names the hazards on the call sheet, who ensures the nearest hospital is listed and current for every location including after a company move, and who has the standing to stop a take.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The operating rule that working ADs describe consistently is this: anyone on a crew can raise a safety concern, but the 1st AD is the person whose job it is to act on it and whose refusal to be overruled is backed by the production. That authority does not require the director's agreement and does not require the client's agreement. It is the one place in the hierarchy where the below-the-line seat outranks the above-the-line seat.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The corollary matters as much as the rule. If the 1st AD is also the person under the most schedule pressure, and if the culture of the shoot has taught them that flagging a hazard makes them the obstacle, the safety authority stops functioning long before anyone gets hurt. A schedule that only works if nobody stops is not a schedule, it is a bet.

Three concrete safety-authority items that belong to this seat on every shoot, at any budget:

- **The safety briefing.** Held at the top of the day, covering the specific hazards of that location, that gear, and that action. Repeated after a company move, because the hazards changed.
- **The hospital line.** Nearest emergency facility, address, drive time, listed on the call sheet. It is a different hospital after a company move. If a second location is added and the call sheet still lists the first location's hospital, the safety document is false.
- **The fatigue arithmetic.** Turnaround, meal breaks and total hours are not administrative niceties. They are the primary fatigue controls on a set, and the AD is the one who schedules against them.

## A3. THE SCHEDULING LOGIC

The single most important fact about a shooting schedule is that it is not the story order.

Most productions are shot out of sequence, meaning they do not begin with the first scene and end with the last. For logistical reasons, scenes are grouped by talent or by location and arranged to accommodate the schedules of cast and crew. **[SOURCED: Gregory Goodell, *Independent Feature Film Production*, p. 87]**

### What groups together, and why

**1. By location.** This is the dominant grouping. The production identifies all scenes that involve the same location, cast, and crew and groups them together as much as possible so they can be shot at once. Completing all planned shots at a location is called **shooting out** that location. **[SOURCED: Goodell p. 87; Kellison, Morrow and Morrow, *Producing for TV and New Media*, p. 155; Rea and Irving, *Producing and Directing the Short Film and Video*, p. 67]**

**2. By cast.** Actors are normally paid a day rate. It makes more financial sense to shoot all three scenes involving a particular actor at a particular location on a single day, even though those scenes may occur in completely different parts of the script, than to pay that actor's day rate three times. **[SOURCED: Steven D. Katz, *Film Directing Shot by Shot*, p. 103]**

**3. By time of day and light state.** Interior versus exterior and day versus night is the primary axis of the stripboard's colour coding, because it is the axis you physically cannot cheat past a certain point. Exterior day scenes are constrained by available daylight. Climate extremes can severely limit the hours available for exterior work if cast and crew become incapacitated by heat or cold. **[SOURCED: Rea and Irving p. 69; Lorene M. Wales, *The Complete Guide to Film and Digital Production*, p. 48]**

**4. By lighting setup.** Within a location and a day, the sub-grouping is by lighting state. Turning a room around, moving the key from one side to the other, is a rebuild of the setup. Shooting all the coverage that shares one lighting state before turning around is standard practice and the reason a scene is often shot in the order "all of one side, then all of the other side."

**5. By build, rig and dressing state.** If a set has to be dressed one way for scene A and another way for scene B, the two states are separate scheduling units even if they are the same physical room. The reset time between them is a real cost.

**6. Against everything else.** Difficult stunts and effects are often scheduled early in the shoot so there is time to recover and make changes if problems arise. Intimate scenes are sometimes scheduled later so the lead actors have time to become comfortable with each other. Some directors and actors prefer to shoot heightened emotional scenes in chronological order, because it is awkward to shoot the end of an argument first. Scenes with long setup lead times, such as in-car dialogue where a vehicle must be rigged and towed, need their own slot. **[SOURCED: Eve Light Honthaner, *The Complete Film Production Handbook*, p. 85; Rea and Irving p. 69]**

**7. Cover sets.** Some producers arrange alternative interior cover sets near exterior locations so the production can relocate immediately to interior work if weather makes the exterior unusable. **[SOURCED: Rea and Irving p. 68; Wales p. 47; Joe Wallenstein, *Practical Moviemaking*, p. 174]**

### The cast rules that bend the schedule

Scheduling around cast is not just courtesy, it is contract arithmetic.

- Union agreements can require payment for **hold days** between non-consecutive shooting days at remote locations. **[SOURCED: Goodell p. 87; Wurmfeld and LaLoggia, *IFP/Los Angeles Independent Filmmaker's Manual*, p. 52]**
- A minimum **turnaround** of 12 hours between an actor's wrap and their next call means the same actors cannot be scheduled for a dawn day call after a night shoot that runs past midnight. **[SOURCED: Honthaner p. 85; Rea and Irving p. 68]**
- If an actor accumulates more than ten hold days, the production may need a **drop-pickup**, dropping the actor from contract and releasing them, then picking them up later. **[SOURCED: Wales p. 47]**
- If an actor has fewer than ten days between shoots, they may become eligible for a **weekly rate rather than a daily rate**, even if they do not work every day of the week. **[SOURCED: Rea and Irving p. 68]**
- **Child performers** are subject to legal limits on hours per day, and separately tend to have less stamina before performance quality degrades. **[SOURCED: Rea and Irving p. 69; Honthaner p. 85; Wales p. 48]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** Every one of those rules turns a creative preference into a number. That is the whole job. "I'd like her in the last scene too" is not a creative note when she was already released. It is either a hold day, a weekly conversion, or a pickup.

### How much can be shot in a day

Three measures exist, and the industry uses all three because none of them is sufficient alone. **[SOURCED: Yamdu production knowledge base, "The Shooting Day Workload"]**

| Measure | What it is | Why it lies |
|---|---|---|
| **Pages** | Scene length in eighths of a script page. A half-page scene is 4/8. Rule of thumb, one script page is about 50 seconds of screen time. | Identical page lengths do not imply identical effort. A one-page dialogue scene is far faster than a one-page block of action description and camera instruction. |
| **Estimated time** | How long the scene is expected to run in the finished film. Prepared by the script supervisor before scheduling. | An action scene and a dialogue scene of the same estimated running time are nowhere near the same day. |
| **Setups** | The number of distinct camera positions and lighting states. | The only measure with a genuine relationship to time, but it still ignores set changes, resets, moves and everything else that eats the day. |

**[PRINCIPLE]** The professional practice is to schedule on setups, sanity-check against pages, and use estimated time as a tiebreak. If a creative document gives you a shot list with no setup count, the first thing to do is derive one, because it is the only number that predicts hours.

**[PRINCIPLE]** A working commercial rule of thumb, which every experienced AD holds and none will guarantee: a lit interior setup with a lighting change is roughly 30 to 60 minutes from the previous cut to the next roll, a re-angle within an existing lighting state is 10 to 25 minutes, and a fully new location within the same building with power, blackout and dressing is effectively a small company move. Adjust for crew size and gear. Never quote it as a fact to a client. Use it to test whether a proposed day is arithmetic or fantasy.

## A4. THE ARTIFACTS AND WHAT EACH ACTUALLY COMMUNICATES

Four documents carry the entire logistical plan. They are not redundant. Each answers a different question, for a different reader, at a different distance.

### The stripboard

A chart of colour-coded strips, one per scene, each carrying the scene number, the time of day, the page count in eighths, the set as described in the script, the actual location, the characters in the scene, and production notes. The strips can be rearranged and laid out to represent the order the production intends to shoot in. **[SOURCED: Goodell p. 85]**

Historically the strips were physical, assembled by hand on multi-panel vinyl or wooden boards about 15 to 18 inches tall. Those boards are now obsolete and aspiring filmmakers are no longer routinely trained in them. Contemporary practice uses digital stripboards, most commonly Movie Magic Scheduling from Entertainment Partners, with alternatives including Celtx, Scenechronize and custom spreadsheets. **[SOURCED: Honthaner p. 82]**

The colour convention encodes interior versus exterior and day versus night. The conventions differ slightly between the generic standard, StudioBinder, Scenechronize and Movie Magic, which is a real practical hazard when documents cross between tools. **[SOURCED: Ralph Singleton, *Film Scheduling*, 2nd ed., ch. 4; software vendor documentation]** A commonly used generic set: white for day interior, yellow for day exterior, blue for night interior, green for night exterior, black for day separators.

**What the stripboard communicates:** the shape of the whole shoot at a glance, and specifically whether it clusters properly. A stripboard where the colours are scattered rather than banded is a schedule that has not been optimised.

### The one-liner

A compact summary of the shoot, one line per scene, carrying scene number, location, interior or exterior, day or night, page count, cast list and a short description. **[SOURCED: standard industry definition, as catalogued in production glossaries]**

**What the one-liner communicates:** the running order in a form a human can read in a meeting without a laptop. It is the document you send to a department head who needs to know when their scene is, not how the whole schedule was reasoned.

### The shooting schedule

The full version. Every day, every scene, in order, with cast numbers, location, times, and the specific requirements each scene places on each department. It is the document departments prep against.

**What it communicates:** the obligations. If a scene requires eight background performers and a rain effect, the shooting schedule is where that becomes an instruction to two departments rather than a line in a script.

### The day out of days

A grid showing every cast member and every other constrained resource against every shoot day, with a status per cell: start, work, hold, travel, drop, pickup, finish. **[SOURCED: standard DOOD structure, as described in production documentation and glossaries]** The DOOD summarises the days on which all resources within a production, such as actors, sets, extras and props, will be needed, which makes it the primary instrument for cost control of expensive or contractually constrained resources. It is how you check whether a schedule remains within the budgetary framework, or whether items have shifted and have to be adjusted. **[SOURCED: Yamdu production knowledge base, "Day Out of Days"]**

**What the DOOD communicates:** cost per person. It is the schedule translated into contracts. When a director adds a cast member to one more scene, the DOOD is the document that tells you whether that costs nothing or costs a whole additional engagement.

### The call sheet

The document issued to cast and crew telling them where and when to report, usually no later than 12 hours before the start of the next work day. **[SOURCED: standard practice; Merriam-Webster and industry references]** It is supervised and crafted by the assistant director using the director's shot list, the production schedule and other logistical considerations.

Standard contents: general call time and individual call times, the scenes and pages shooting, the location address and parking arrangements, cast transportation, contact information, safety notes, weather, sunrise and sunset times, nearest hospital, meal times and quantities, dietary limitations, and a section on the front dedicated to reminding department heads of that day's specific needs beyond the unit's usual kit, such as a crane rental, a special effects build, or a prop or set that must be readied. **[SOURCED: standard call sheet structure, per Pat P. Miller, *Script Supervising and Film Continuity*, and Rea and Irving]**

**What the call sheet communicates:** the contract for tomorrow. Everything on it is a promise. A call sheet with a missing hospital after a company move, or a call time that breaches turnaround, is not a document error. It is a broken promise with a legal and safety dimension.

### The daily production report and the wrap report

The DGA duty list requires the UPM or 1st AD to supervise completion of the Production Report for each day's work, showing work covered and the status of the production, and to arrange its distribution. **[SOURCED: DGA 2011 Basic Agreement pp. 13 to 15]** It records who was present, what was shot, hours, meal breaks, unexpected expenses and delays, and serves both operational and legal purposes.

**Hot costs** are the fast day-after estimate of what a shoot day actually cost, including overtime, meal penalties and extra hires, produced before the full accounting catches up. The **wrap report** or wrap book assembled at the end of principal photography summarises actual versus budgeted cost, schedule slip and departmental notes. **[SOURCED: standard production accounting practice, as catalogued in production glossaries]**

## A5. BUDGETING: ABOVE THE LINE AND BELOW THE LINE

The distinction is literal and historical. In the early studio era the budget top sheet had an actual line drawn across it separating two groups of costs. **[SOURCED: standard budget history, per Backstage and Film Victoria crew role documentation]**

**Above the line** covers the individuals who guide and influence the creative direction and voice of the narrative, and their associated costs: rights to the underlying material, the screenplay, the screenwriter, the producer, the director, the principal cast, and cost items attached to them such as their assistants. These costs are usually set, negotiated, spent or promised before principal photography begins. **[SOURCED: standard ATL definition, per Film Victoria crew roles guide and Backstage]**

**Below the line** covers everything physical: the crew, the equipment, the locations, the build, the stock, the transport, the post services.

The division of labour follows the line. A producer typically oversees all cost-related decisions including above-the-line expenditure, especially during pre-production, while the UPM is responsible for the more detailed planning and execution of below-the-line costs. The director selects key department heads. For below-the-line matters, the UPM negotiates the deals for locations and equipment and hires the remaining crew, typically on the recommendation of the keys. **[SOURCED: DGA-derived UPM role description]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** The practical consequence for anyone reading a creative document: an above-the-line change is a negotiation, a below-the-line change is arithmetic. Adding a named performer is a negotiation. Adding a location is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is not optional.

### How a creative decision becomes a line item

This is the conversion every line producer performs constantly, and the reason the seat has to read creative documents rather than wait for a brief.

| Creative decision | Immediate line items it creates |
|---|---|
| "We see the same character in a second environment" | Location fee, permit, possible company move, transport, extra dressing, possible additional wardrobe change and therefore a costume standby and continuity stills, revised call sheet, second hospital |
| "She changes outfit between shots" | Wardrobe unit count, fitting hours, standby dresser time, on-set change space, continuity photography, schedule gap between setups |
| "Shot at golden hour" | A hard, unmovable, roughly 30 to 50 minute window that the entire day must be reverse-engineered around, plus a contingency plan if the sun does not cooperate |
| "A crowd fills the background" | Background performer count and rate, casting time, wrangling headcount in the AD department, catering count, holding area, wardrobe and makeup throughput, release forms |
| "Practical rain" | SFX crew, water supply and disposal, drying time between takes, wardrobe doubles, extended reset, safety brief, likely a whole scheduling block on its own |
| "Shot on a specialty lens set" | Rental package change, possible camera body change, possible additional assistant, insurance value, prep day at the rental house |
| "A built environment rather than a found one" | Build days, stage or space rental split between build and shoot rates, construction labour, materials, strike, storage |
| "One continuous take" | Rehearsal hours that must be scheduled as production hours, blocking time, possibly a full day for one shot, and an explicit fallback |

**[PRINCIPLE]** Note what all the right-hand cells share. Almost none of them are the item that was named. The named item, the location or the outfit or the lens, is usually the cheapest thing in the row. The cost is in what the named item drags behind it.

### What actually drives cost on a shoot day

Ranked roughly by how often they blow a budget, in the experience the industry's standard forms encode:

1. **Crew hours.** Overtime is the single largest uncontrolled variable on most shoot days. It is calculated per head, so it scales with crew size, which means a large crew running two hours late costs multiples of what a small crew running two hours late costs. The standard commercial bid form carries a payroll overtime calculator in every personnel section for exactly this reason. **[SOURCED: AICP Bid Form structure, sections A, B and G]**
2. **Company moves.** See A6.
3. **Cast day rates and the DOOD consequences of any addition.**
4. **Location fees, permits, security and the associated services.** Location shooting frequently carries unexpected costs, which is why the standard bid form breaks it into its own section and why experienced producers treat location estimates as the least reliable in the document. **[SOURCED: AICP Bid Form, Section D]**
5. **Gear packages.** Not the daily rate, but the package boundary. Adding one format or one specialty item can move you into a different package, a different truck, or a different number of technicians.
6. **Build days and stage time.** Standard practice separates studio rental for build days from studio rental for shoot days, precisely so the production can see how it is spending across time. **[SOURCED: AICP Bid Form, Section F]**
7. **Prep hours in art, wardrobe and makeup.** Chronically underestimated because they happen before anyone is watching. See A7.
8. **Meal penalties and turnaround breaches.** Avoidable, entirely schedule-driven, and pure loss.

## A6. THE COMPANY MOVE

A company move is the whole unit, cast, crew, trucks and equipment, packing up at one location and travelling to another within a shoot day. It is one of the costliest things a schedule can ask for, because for the length of the move nobody is shooting and everybody is being paid, and the rig has to come down and go back up. Good scheduling minimises moves by grouping scenes by location on the stripboard so the day shoots out one place before travelling. When a move is unavoidable it goes on the call sheet with travel times, a new map, new parking and a fresh nearest hospital. Producers watch moves closely because each one is a direct hit to the day's page count. **[SOURCED: standard scheduling definition, as documented in production glossaries; the same principle appears in Rea and Irving p. 67, noting that each company move may take up significant amounts of time]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** The anatomy of a move, in hours, on a modest commercial crew:

| Phase | What happens | Typical cost in time |
|---|---|---|
| Call the wrap on location 1 | Last shot, safety, begin strike | 0 |
| De-rig | Lights down, stands collapsed, cable coiled, camera broken down, art department strikes and restores the space | 30 to 90 minutes |
| Load | Into trucks and vehicles, gear checked against the list | 20 to 40 minutes |
| Travel | Convoy, not a car. Slowest vehicle sets the pace | Actual drive time plus 50 percent |
| Unload and stage | Position trucks, get gear to the working area, which may involve stairs, lifts or a long carry | 20 to 45 minutes |
| Re-rig | Lighting rebuilt, camera built, art dressed, sound reset | 45 to 120 minutes |
| Safety brief for the new location | Non-negotiable, and it is a genuinely different brief | 10 minutes |
| First shot | | |

The honest number for a real second location in the same city, with a normal crew, is **two and a half to four hours of paid, non-shooting time**, before anything goes wrong. On a ten hour day that is a quarter to nearly half the day gone.

**[PRINCIPLE]** This is why adding a location is a scheduling decision at least as much as a creative one. The creative conversation is "should this moment happen somewhere else." The scheduling conversation is "which shots are we deleting to pay for it." Both conversations have to happen, and the second one does not happen by itself. It has to be forced into the room by the person holding the schedule.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The most dangerous version of this is the location that is described creatively but never labelled as a move. A crew bible that says "the workshop" and "the yard" may be describing one property or two properties forty minutes apart. A shot list that names "the corridor" and "the roof" may be one building with roof access, or a building plus a permit plus a lift plus a separate risk assessment. Nothing in the language distinguishes them. Only asking distinguishes them.

### The sub-move nobody budgets

A move within a single property is not free either. Different floor, different power, different blackout, different noise environment, different access for the gear. If the second space needs its own lighting plan built from zero, it is functionally a company move with a shorter drive. Budget an hour to two hours and put it on the schedule as a named event, not as a gap.

## A7. PREP-TIME BUDGETING: THE HOURS BEFORE THE HOURS

The most consistently under-budgeted part of any shoot is the work that happens before the first shot. It is invisible to the client, invisible to the creative document, and it is the thing that determines whether the day starts on time.

The standard commercial bid form treats prep as structurally separate from shoot: Section A is prep crew, Section B is shoot crew, and Section C is prep and wrap expenses. The art department gets its own labour section because it is frequently the most fluid department on the crew and it is simply easier to account for an overall number of days per art crew member than to split them into prep, shoot and wrap. **[SOURCED: AICP Bid Form, sections A, B, C and G, as documented by Wrapbook, "How to Fill Out the AICP Bid Form"]**

Read that as an admission. The industry's own standard form concedes that art department time does not fit neatly into a shoot day model, because it does not.

### Makeup and hair throughput

**[PRINCIPLE]** Makeup is a queue problem, and queues do not scale linearly. The governing arithmetic is chairs times hours.

- A chair is a physical position with an artist in it. Two artists and one mirror is one chair for throughput purposes.
- A straightforward clean beauty look runs roughly 30 to 45 minutes per person. A more constructed look, period, character, effects or extensive hair, runs 60 to 120 minutes and up.
- Background performers are usually batched at a much lower per-head time, but batching requires space and a second artist.
- Nobody can be in the chair and on set simultaneously, which means the makeup schedule and the shooting order have to agree with each other.

The consequence: **if you have two chairs and a 45 minute look, four cast members take 90 minutes, six take 135 minutes, and eight take 180 minutes.** Adding two people to a cast list does not add two units of anything. It adds a block of time at the very front of the day, which either pushes the crew call earlier, pushes the first shot later, or requires a third artist. All three are budget events.

**[SOURCED]** That prep times drive the shooting schedule is explicit in standard pre-production meeting practice, which lists "discussing prep times, for example if special effects make-up is needed that will affect a shooting schedule" as a formal agenda item. **[SOURCED: Yamdu, "Preproduction Meeting (PPM)"]**

### Wardrobe fittings

**[PRINCIPLE]** The arithmetic here is looks, not people.

- A fitting is per performer per look, not per performer. One performer with three costume changes is three fittings' worth of work, or one long fitting, plus three continuity records.
- Standard commercial practice is roughly 45 to 90 minutes per performer for a single look including alterations assessment, longer for period or constructed costume.
- Alterations are a separate labour line with a lead time measured in days, not hours.
- On the day, every costume change is a scheduled gap in the shooting order, typically 10 to 30 minutes depending on complexity and whether there is a private change space at the location.

The consequence: **a cast expansion from four to six performers, each with two looks, moves the wardrobe prep requirement from eight fitting units to twelve**, plus four more continuity records, plus additional standby time on the day. If the previously agreed prep window was built for eight, it is now wrong, and it is wrong by a margin that no amount of goodwill on the day recovers.

### Set dressing hours

**[PRINCIPLE]** Dressing scales with zones and surfaces, not with locations.

- A "zone" is any area the camera will see that has to be composed: a desk, a wall, a corner, a threshold, a table top.
- A modest zone takes an art department pair roughly 45 to 90 minutes to dress from a prepared kit. A zone that needs sourcing, building or repainting is a day or more of prep before anyone dresses anything.
- A location that will be seen from multiple angles has more zones than a location seen from one angle. A 360 degree usable space is a multiple, not an increment.
- Strike and restore is real. On a practical location it is often 50 to 80 percent of the dressing time, and it happens at the end of the day when everyone is tired and the clock is already lost.

The consequence: **adding one wall of a room to the shot list can add a zone, and a zone is roughly an hour of pre-call art department labour** that has to be bought either by starting the art department earlier, which is a pre-call day, or by adding a body, which is a rate.

### The pre-light and the pre-rig

**[PRINCIPLE]** If the lighting plan for a space is more than a couple of sources, someone has to build it before the crew call, or the crew spends the first two hours of the day watching it get built. A pre-light day, or a pre-light morning with a skeleton crew, is a line item. The standard bid form's separation of prep crew from shoot crew exists to hold exactly this. **[SOURCED: AICP Bid Form, Section A, which explicitly contemplates a key grip pre-rigging a stage during pre-production]**

## A8. READING A CREATIVE DOCUMENT FOR HIDDEN COST

This is the skill that distinguishes a scheduler from a typist. Creative documents almost never announce their cost. The cost is embedded in ordinary descriptive language.

Here is the catalogue of tells, with what each one silently adds.

| Phrase pattern in a creative document | What it may silently add |
|---|---|
| Two environments named with different nouns, no relationship stated | A company move, a second permit, a second hospital, two to four hours |
| "Later, at the same place" | A time-of-day change, which is a full relight, or a scheduling constraint tying two blocks to specific hours |
| "Golden hour", "just before dawn", "as the sun sets" | A fixed, unmovable, roughly 30 to 50 minute window, and a mandatory contingency plan |
| A new character name appearing once, midway through | A full cast engagement: casting, fitting, makeup chair, release, catering, DOOD entry |
| "A group of", "a crowd", "onlookers", "a team" | Background performers, and therefore casting, wrangling, wardrobe throughput, catering count, and an AD department headcount increase |
| "He is now wearing" | A costume change: a unit, a fitting, a continuity record, a standby dresser, and a schedule gap |
| "We move through the door into" | Possibly a single continuous space, possibly two lighting states, possibly two locations. Never assume |
| "Shot on [specific camera or lens]" appearing in only some entries | Package inconsistency. Either the package is bigger than budgeted or the document contradicts itself |
| "Rain", "smoke", "fire", "water", "wind" | SFX crew, safety brief, reset time between takes, wardrobe doubles, permits, and frequently a dedicated block |
| "Practical light source", "the lamp is on" | Art department owns the fixture, lighting owns the bulb and the power, and someone has to establish which and dim it |
| A build, a set piece, a constructed element | Build days, materials, a space rented at build rate before shoot rate, and a strike |
| Any animal | A handler, welfare provision, a wrangler day, insurance, and a scene that will take longer than any human scene of equal length |
| Any minor | Legal hour limits, a chaperone, a tutor in some jurisdictions, and reduced usable hours |
| "One continuous shot", "no cuts" | Rehearsal hours as production hours, and a fallback plan that must be scheduled |
| "Drone", "aerial", "vehicle-mounted", "underwater" | A specialist unit, a permit, an operator, a licence and usually a separate block |
| A technical rule stated absolutely, such as a fixed stop or a fixed lens for the whole piece | Check it against the light available at each scheduled time. A rule that cannot be achieved in the light available is a schedule problem, not a camera problem |

**[PRINCIPLE]** The most expensive line in any creative document is the one that reads as a small clarification. "Also in the second half, we see her at home." Eleven words. A location, a permit, a move, a dressing day, a continuity check and a hospital.

## A9. COMMERCIAL PRODUCTION SPECIFICS

Commercial and advertising production runs on a different economic structure from features, and the differences change the job materially.

### The bid

The Association of Independent Commercial Producers was founded in 1972 as a not-for-profit trade association dedicated to creating industry standards in advertising media production. Its most important resource is the AICP Bid Form, introduced in 1975 to standardise bidding on commercials, in response to widespread confusion caused by agencies and advertisers using a range of different forms. It became the de facto standard on which commercial production has been based since, and it has been revised repeatedly, with an Excel version released for public use in 2019 and updated in January 2023. AICP also publishes Bidding Guidelines, Suggested Best Practices for Bidding, a Bidding Reference Guide and Sample Language for Bids. **[SOURCED: AICP, Bidding Resources; Wrapbook, "How to Fill Out the AICP Bid Form"]**

The form's structure is worth knowing even outside the US, because it encodes how the industry thinks about cost:

| Section | Covers |
|---|---|
| Cover | Job info, production info, spot titles and lengths, and the global percentages: fringes, production fees, insurance, handling, post markup, post tax |
| Bid Notes | Free text for any special circumstance |
| A | Prep crew |
| B | Shoot crew |
| C | Prep and wrap expenses |
| D | Location expenses |
| E | Props, wardrobe, animals, and also theatrical makeup and greens |
| F | Studio costs, split between build-day and shoot-day rental |
| G | Art department labour |
| H | Art department expenses, including art department meals |
| I | Equipment rental, by category rather than individual item |
| J | Media and stock |
| K | Miscellaneous production costs |
| L | Director's fees |
| M | Talent, including background |
| N | Talent expenses |
| O | Other |
| P1, P2 ... | Custom named categories, each a category and not a single line item |
| Q | Editorial |
| R | Social versions |
| S | Audio |
| T | Finishing |
| V | Miscellaneous editorial |
| W | Editorial labour and creative fees |
| X | Visual effects, design, animation, interactive |

**[SOURCED: AICP Bid Form section structure]**

Three structural features matter more than the section list.

**Production fees are a markup, not a line.** Every line item carries a percentage markup above actual estimated value when presented to an agency. These fees account for the production company's overhead and create its profit margin. Fringes, insurance, handling and post markup work the same way. Because these are dependent variables with a degree of subjectivity, an agency may attempt to negotiate the percentages down as a way of shrinking the estimate without decreasing the physical capabilities of the production. **[SOURCED: Wrapbook on the AICP Bid Form]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** That last sentence is the single most important commercial-production fact in this document. A client who negotiates the markup rather than the scope is reducing the production company's margin, not the shoot. A client who negotiates the scope is reducing the shoot. Knowing which one is happening is the line producer's job, and saying so plainly is the line producer's obligation.

**Every section carries Actual and Variance columns.** These exist so the budget can be actualised during wrap: enter what you actually spent, and the variance calculates against the estimate. **[SOURCED: AICP Bid Form structure]** A bid is not a document you write once. It is a document you are held to.

**The section numbering is conventional but not sacred.** Line descriptions can be changed to suit each job. Some numbers have been broadly stable, with 112 usually meals and 119 usually fuel, but they shift between budgets. The professional discipline is to keep the line descriptions and rates consistent for a repeat client, because a consistent budget layout makes an award easier to obtain. **[SOURCED: Wrapbook, quoting working commercial producers]**

**[SOURCED]** The stated professional rule of thumb for bidding: think like a cost controller. When you submit a bid, interrogate not only the total but why every line item is as high as it is, then assess whether you genuinely need the specific equipment or whether the creative can be modified. **[SOURCED: Wrapbook, "How to Fill Out the AICP Bid Form"]**

### The PPM

The pre-production meeting is the commercial world's defining ritual and the term originates there. It marks the completion of the preparation phase and is the last joint meeting before shooting. Participants are the representatives of the client, the agency, the production, and the director. Its purpose is to clarify and obtain the final green light on all production areas before principal photography, including time frame and budget milestones, deliverables and cut-downs, storyboard and shot list, locations and production design, cast and styling. All areas are summarised, often as a production book or presentation that anticipates the overall impression of the finished advertising. Even where there is no formal production book, it is strongly recommended to produce at least a report, follow-up memo or email in written form, because it prevents a great deal of trouble in the later approval process. **[SOURCED: Yamdu, "Preproduction Meeting (PPM)"]**

Crucially: the PPM is also the last chance to reach agreement on anything not yet decided, to identify problems and to make final changes, which is why the production has to plan and set it up carefully. Changes at this point can significantly disrupt production processes and trigger unpredictable costs. **[SOURCED: Yamdu]**

There is usually more than one. Christie Poulos, writing as Client Director at Genero, describes the first PPM as the kick-off for the production, a later one once more elements are locked and more detailed questions need answering, and a final one that should be at least two days before the shoot to give all stakeholders time to handle any actions. Her agenda covers idea and treatment recap, deliverables, key dates and payment milestones, whether clients will attend the shoot so they can be accounted for in the planning of the shoot day, crew and special skills, talent casting and the decision-making process to confirm talent, wardrobe, hair, makeup and colours to avoid, props and product handling, locations and any requirements that could impact schedule or cost, travel and shipping, post-production and graphics alignment, and legals and insurance. Her closing advice is that accurate detailed notes should be uploaded as an official binding record. **[SOURCED: Christie Poulos, Genero, "Why the Pre-Production Meeting can make or break your shoot"]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** For the schedule and budget seat, the PPM has one function above all others: **it is the moment the scope stops moving.** Everything agreed there is the baseline against which every later change is priced. A production that does not hold a PPM, or holds one and does not write it down, has no baseline, and therefore has no way to charge for a change, and therefore absorbs every change as margin.

### Shoot day economics and the presence of agency and client

**[PRINCIPLE]** The commercial shoot day has an audience. Agency creatives, brand marketers and occasionally legal sit at video village. This is not a nuisance to be managed around, it is a structural feature that changes the schedule:

- Every setup has an approval step that does not exist on a feature. The AD's rhythm becomes shoot, check, wait for the nod, move on.
- The person who says "moving on" is the producer relaying the client, not the director. **[SOURCED: Wrapbook]**
- Client attendance is itself a logistics item: seating, monitors, shade, catering, hospitality, and a named person whose job is to look after them so the AD does not have to.
- Client presence is an agenda item at the PPM specifically so it can be accounted for in shoot day planning. **[SOURCED: Genero PPM agenda]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** Budget the approval loop explicitly. Ten to fifteen minutes per setup for client viewing and discussion, on a day with twelve setups, is two to three hours. If that time is not in the plan, it comes out of the shooting time, and the shots that get cut are the ones at the end of the list, which are usually the ones the client cares about least on paper and most in the edit.

### Overtime and meal penalties

**[PRINCIPLE]** Both are schedule failures expressed as money.

- **Overtime** is per person per hour, multiplied across the whole crew, usually at a premium rate that escalates. It is the fastest way for a day to go from on-budget to badly over.
- **Meal penalties** accrue when the break does not happen inside the contracted interval, and they typically compound in increments until the break is called. They are pure loss with no production value whatsoever.
- **Turnaround breach** costs money and, more seriously, is a fatigue event. Union agreements in the US and UK set a minimum, commonly ten to eleven hours for crew and twelve for cast, and breaking it triggers penalty payments and, more importantly, fatigue risk. **[SOURCED: IATSE and BECTU turnaround thresholds as documented in industry scheduling references; Honthaner p. 85 and Rea and Irving p. 68 on the 12-hour cast turnaround]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** Meal penalty is the cleanest diagnostic of AD competence available. It is entirely within the seat's control, it is entirely predictable, and it costs money for nothing. A production that regularly incurs it does not have a schedule problem, it has a decision problem.

## A10. HOW COMMERCIAL SCHEDULING DIFFERS FROM FEATURE SCHEDULING

| | **Feature** | **Commercial** |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | Weeks to months of principal photography | Typically one to three shoot days |
| Unit of planning | The shoot day within a multi-week board | The hour within a single day |
| Source document | A screenplay, broken down into scenes and eighths | A board, a shot list, a treatment. Often no script pages at all |
| The measuring unit | Pages and setups | Setups and finished seconds. Page counts are frequently meaningless |
| Who says "moving on" | The director | The producer, relaying the client **[SOURCED: Wrapbook]** |
| Approval loop | In the edit, weeks later | At video village, immediately, every setup |
| Contingency | Built into the board across weeks. A lost half day is recoverable | There is no tomorrow. A lost hour is a deleted shot |
| Cost structure | Studio or financier budget, actualised over months | A bid with markups, fixed at award, actualised at wrap **[SOURCED: AICP Bid Form]** |
| Overrun consequence | Schedule days added, budget amended | Overtime on a fixed-fee job, absorbed by the production company's margin |
| Company moves | Planned across a board, sometimes unavoidable | Usually catastrophic. A move on a one-day shoot can be a third of the day |
| Prep visibility | Weeks of documented prep, department by department | Compressed into days, and the client only sees the PPM |
| Cast contracting | Day players, weekly conversions, hold days, drop-pickup | Usage and buyout terms often matter more than day rates |
| The scope baseline | Locked script, revisions coloured and dated | The PPM record, and only if someone wrote it down |

**[PRINCIPLE]** The single biggest cognitive shift moving from features to commercials: **in features, the schedule absorbs error across days. In commercials, the schedule has no shock absorber.** Every scheduling decision on a one-day commercial is zero-sum. Every hour granted to one thing is taken from another thing, and the thing it is taken from is always the last item on the list.

## A11. NAMED PRACTITIONERS, INSTITUTIONS AND DOCUMENTATION

**The Directors Guild of America** represents Directors, Assistant Directors and Unit Production Managers under the Basic Agreement, which enumerates the duties an employer must assign to the UPM or 1st AD, and publishes rate cards that place the 1st AD and UPM as the top below-the-line technical classifications. **[SOURCED: DGA Basic Agreement and rate card documentation, dga.org]** The Guild's contracts department is the primary institutional reference for what these jobs formally are, as opposed to what they informally become.

**The Association of Independent Commercial Producers**, founded 1972, created the AICP Bid Form in 1975 and maintains it alongside published Bidding Guidelines, Suggested Best Practices and a Bidding Reference Guide. Its form is the de facto commercial budgeting standard worldwide. **[SOURCED: aicp.com, Bidding Resources]** In 2018 the separate post-production bid form previously maintained by the Association of Independent Creative Editors was folded into it, so a single flexible document now serves both production and post bids. **[SOURCED: Wrapbook on the AICP Bid Form]**

**Abby Singer** was a production manager whose habit of announcing the second-to-last shot of the day gave the industry the term "the Abby Singer." The final shot of the day is the "martini." **[SOURCED: standard crew terminology, as catalogued in production glossaries]** That a production manager's scheduling tic became permanent industry vocabulary is a reasonable measure of how central this seat is to the culture of a set.

**Tamra Davis**, director, describes the 1st AD as her right arm, the person who manages cast and crew schedules so the director can concentrate on the creative work. **[SOURCED: Tamra Davis, interviewed for StudioBinder's Stages of Filmmaking series]**

**Pete Dress**, 2nd AD on *No Country for Old Men* and *King Richard*, describes the second's role as central to keeping everyone informed and ready for filming each day. **[SOURCED: Pete Dress, interviewed for StudioBinder's Stages of Filmmaking series]**

**Christie Poulos**, Client Director at Genero, has published the most usable practitioner account of running commercial PPMs, including the rule that the final PPM should sit at least two days before the shoot so stakeholders can act on their actions. **[SOURCED: Genero Insights]**

**The standard textbooks** that encode this discipline and are still cited in current practice: Eve Light Honthaner, *The Complete Film Production Handbook*; Ralph Singleton, *Film Scheduling*; Gregory Goodell, *Independent Feature Film Production*; Rea and Irving, *Producing and Directing the Short Film and Video*; Steven D. Katz, *Film Directing Shot by Shot*; Pat P. Miller, *Script Supervising and Film Continuity*; Lorene M. Wales, *The Complete Guide to Film and Digital Production*; Wurmfeld and LaLoggia, *IFP/Los Angeles Independent Filmmaker's Manual*.

**On who holds the job.** A 2019 USC Annenberg study of top-grossing American films found that women held 9 percent of 1st AD positions, 33.6 percent of 2nd AD positions, and 31.9 percent of 2nd 2nd AD positions. **[SOURCED: Smith, Pieper, Choueiti and Choir, "Inclusion in the Director's Chair?", USC Annenberg, January 2019]** The gap between the first number and the other two is the clearest available evidence that the promotion from 2nd to 1st is not functioning as a neutral meritocracy, and it is worth knowing when building a crew.

**On job security.** The 1st AD is paid well to keep the production on track, and is usually the first person to be replaced if the production strays too far from its original schedule or budget. **[SOURCED: Barbara Freedman Doyle, *Make Your Movie: What You Need to Know About the Business and Politics of Filmmaking*, p. 213]**

## A12. VOCABULARY GLOSSARY

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Stripboard / production board** | A chart of colour-coded strips, one per scene, carrying scene number, time of day, page count, set, location, cast and notes, rearranged to form the shooting order |
| **One-liner** | A compact one-line-per-scene summary of the shoot, readable in a meeting |
| **Shooting schedule** | The full day-by-day breakdown with every scene's requirements on every department |
| **DOOD (Day Out of Days)** | A grid of every cast member and constrained resource against every shoot day, with a status per cell (start, work, hold, travel, drop, pickup, finish). The primary cost-control instrument for contracted resources |
| **Call sheet** | The document issued the night before telling everyone where and when to report, plus scenes, safety, weather, hospital and department-specific needs |
| **Eighth of a page** | The scheduling unit of scene length. A script page is divided into eight; a half page is 4/8 |
| **Setup** | A distinct camera position and lighting state. The only workload measure with a real relationship to elapsed time |
| **Company move** | Relocating the entire unit from one location to another within a shoot day. The most expensive hour on any schedule |
| **Shooting out** | Completing all planned shots at a location so the unit never has to return |
| **Cover set** | An alternative interior location kept ready so the unit can relocate if weather kills an exterior |
| **Turnaround** | The mandatory rest period between wrap and the next call. Commonly 10 to 11 hours for crew, 12 for cast under union agreements. Breaching it triggers penalties and is a fatigue event |
| **Meal penalty** | Money owed when a meal break is not called within the contracted interval. Compounds until the break happens. Pure loss |
| **Hold day** | A day an actor is contracted and paid but not working, between non-consecutive shooting days |
| **Drop-pickup** | Releasing an actor from contract and re-engaging them later, used when hold days exceed the contractual threshold |
| **Abby Singer** | The second-to-last shot of the day, named for the production manager who habitually announced it |
| **Martini** | The final shot of the day |
| **Hot costs** | The fast day-after estimate of what a shoot day actually cost, including overtime and penalties, produced before full accounting |
| **Daily Production Report (DPR)** | The end-of-day record of work covered, hours, attendance, delays and unexpected costs. A DGA-mandated duty of the UPM or 1st AD |
| **Wrap report / wrap book** | The end-of-production summary of actual versus budgeted cost, schedule slip and departmental notes |
| **AICP bid** | The Association of Independent Commercial Producers' standardised commercial bid form, sections A through X, with actual and variance columns for wrap actualisation |
| **Production fee** | The percentage markup applied across bid line items to cover production company overhead and margin |
| **PPM** | Pre-production meeting. The last joint meeting between client, agency, production and director before the shoot, where scope is finally agreed |
| **ATL (above the line)** | Costs attached to the creative authorship of the piece: rights, script, producer, director, principal cast. Usually negotiated before principal photography |
| **BTL (below the line)** | Costs of physical production: crew, gear, locations, build, stock, transport, post services |
| **Top sheet** | The one-page summary at the front of a budget, totalling every category. The historical origin of the ATL/BTL distinction |
| **Base camp** | The area holding production, cast, hair and makeup, away from the shooting set |
| **Calling the roll** | The 1st AD's fixed sequence of cues that takes the set from ready to rolling |
| **Lockup** | Physically controlling an access point so nothing interrupts a take |
| **Pre-light / pre-rig** | Building the lighting or grip setup before the main crew call, staffed and budgeted separately |

# PART B: AGENT OPERATING MANUAL

**How to occupy the 1st AD and line producer seat inside a multi-department pipeline.**

---

## B1. THE QUESTIONS THIS ROLE ASKS ON RECEIPT OF A CREATIVE DOCUMENT

Ask these before producing anything. Never assume an answer. An unanswered question is a finding, and it goes in the report as one.

### On the shape of the job

1. How many shoot days is this, and who decided that number, and against what?
2. What is the total scheduled crew day: call to wrap, in hours?
3. Is this a fixed-fee job or a cost-plus job? If fixed, whose margin absorbs an overrun?
4. Has a PPM happened? Is there a written record? If not, what is the scope baseline?
5. What is the deliverable count, including cut-downs and social versions? Each one may carry its own capture requirement.

### On locations

6. List every distinct place named anywhere in the creative document, the shot list and the crew bibles. For each: **is this the same physical space as another entry, a different room in the same property, or a separate address?**
7. For every separate address: is there a permit, a fee, a load-in restriction, a power supply, a parking plan and a nearest hospital?
8. For every same-property second space: does it need its own lighting build? Its own blackout? Its own dressing? If yes to any, it is a sub-move and it gets an hour on the schedule.
9. Is any location time-restricted? A venue that is only available before 10am is a hard constraint on the whole day, not a note.

### On cast

10. Exact headcount of principal performers. Exact headcount of background.
11. For each performer: number of distinct looks. Number of costume changes on the day.
12. For each performer: makeup and hair complexity, and therefore chair time.
13. How many makeup chairs and how many artists are budgeted? Compute throughput, then compute the crew call that throughput implies.
14. Any minors, any animals, any performer with specific access requirements? Each has a legal or welfare constraint that outranks the schedule.
15. Has any performer been added since the prep estimate was made? If yes, the prep estimate is void until re-totalled.

### On camera, lens and gear

16. Is the camera and lens specification consistent across every entry in the shot list and every crew bible? Name every inconsistency.
17. Does any single entry require a piece of kit that is not in the base package? If yes, name the package change, the rate and whether it needs an extra technician.
18. Any specialist unit: drone, underwater, vehicle mount, high-speed, motion control? Each is a separate block with its own crew, permit and setup time.
19. Does the gear list imply more than one truck, and does the location have space for it?

### On technical rules

20. List every stated Locked Rule: fixed stop, fixed lens, fixed frame rate, fixed lighting approach, one-take mandate, natural light only.
21. For each rule, check it against the specific time of day each scene is scheduled. **Can this rule physically be achieved in the light available at that hour, in that space, at that stop?** If not, the rule and the schedule are in contradiction and one must change.

### On the day itself

22. What is the setup count for the day? If the shot list does not give one, derive it.
23. What is the implied minutes-per-setup, given the scheduled hours minus meal minus moves minus approval loop?
24. Is there client or agency attendance? How many people, and who is looking after them?
25. Where does the meal break fall, and does that placement work with the shooting order or interrupt a lighting state mid-scene?
26. What is the turnaround from this day's wrap to the next day's call, per person, including anyone who came in for pre-light?

## B2. THE PRODUCTION-LOGISTICS AUDIT PROTOCOL

This is the core procedure. Run it in order. Do not skip steps because an earlier document looked fine, and do not stop at the first finding.

**The protocol's governing instruction: read the creative document hunting specifically for scheduling and cost implications that were never flagged as such.** The document will not tell you where it is expensive. It was not written to. Assume every descriptive sentence may contain a logistics event and check it as one.

---

### STEP 1. Inventory the document for unflagged logistics events

Read the creative document, treatment, shot list and every crew bible end to end, and extract into a flat list:

- every place named
- every person named or implied
- every costume state named or implied
- every time of day stated or implied
- every technical specification stated
- every physical effect, animal, vehicle, minor or specialist element
- every stated rule or constraint

Do not evaluate yet. Extract first. The purpose is to convert prose into a countable list, because prose hides quantity and lists do not.

**Output of step 1:** a raw inventory with a source reference for each item (which document, which section).

---

### STEP 2. Location resolution and move detection

For every place in the inventory, resolve it into exactly one of four categories:

| Category | Definition | Schedule consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Same space** | Genuinely the same physical area, same lighting build, same dressing | None |
| **Same property, new setup** | Different room or angle in the same building, requiring its own light and dressing | Sub-move. Add 1 to 2 hours. Own line on the schedule |
| **Separate address** | A different location requiring travel | Full company move. Add 2.5 to 4 hours. Own permit, own parking, own hospital, own safety brief |
| **Unresolved** | The document does not say, and nobody has confirmed | **This is a finding.** Report it as an open item with both cost outcomes stated |

**Do not resolve an unresolved location by guessing.** Report both outcomes. "If the yard is on the same property as the workshop, this costs 1 to 2 hours. If it is a separate address, it costs 2.5 to 4 hours and a second permit. Confirmation required before the schedule can lock."

**Output of step 2:** every place categorised, every move counted, every hospital requirement identified, and every unresolved location flagged with a two-sided cost.

---

### STEP 3. Cast and prep-budget revalidation

For every performer in the inventory:

1. Count looks, not people.
2. Assign a makeup and hair time band: light (30 to 45 min), standard (45 to 75 min), constructed (90 min or more).
3. Compute total chair-hours required.
4. Divide by budgeted chairs to get elapsed prep time.
5. Compute the crew call and cast call that elapsed prep time implies, working backwards from the required first shot.
6. Count fittings required (performers times looks) and compare against the fittings budgeted.
7. Count on-day costume changes and place each one in the shooting order as a named gap.

**Then apply the staleness test (B3).** If the cast list has changed at all since the prep window was agreed, the prep window is void and must be recomputed, not adjusted.

**Output of step 3:** a prep-hours total, an implied call time, a fittings count, a chair count requirement, and an explicit statement of whether the previously agreed prep window still holds.

---

### STEP 4. Gear, lens and package consistency check

1. Build a table of every camera, lens, format and specialty item named anywhere across all documents.
2. Flag every inconsistency, including inconsistencies of omission where one entry specifies and another does not.
3. Determine whether the union of all named items fits inside one rental package or forces a package change.
4. For each package change, state: the rate delta, whether it needs an additional technician, whether it needs an additional prep day at the rental house, and whether it changes the truck requirement.
5. Check whether any item requires a specialist operator who is not on the crew list.

**Output of step 4:** a consistency table, a package decision, and any crew additions the gear implies.

---

### STEP 5. Locked Rule achievability check

For each stated technical rule, test it against the schedule as scheduled, not as imagined.

- A fixed wide aperture in a bright exterior at midday requires ND. Is ND in the package?
- A natural-light-only rule for an interior at a scheduled hour of 4pm in winter requires you to check actual light levels at that hour. Is the scene scheduled inside the available light?
- A golden-hour rule gives you 30 to 50 minutes. How many setups are assigned to that window? If more than two, the rule and the schedule contradict.
- A single-take rule requires rehearsal hours. Are they on the schedule as production hours, or is the rehearsal assumed to be free?
- A fixed frame rate that implies a light level requires you to check whether the fixture package can deliver it in that space.

**Any rule that cannot be achieved in the hours scheduled is reported as a contradiction, with both resolutions offered: change the rule, or change the schedule.**

**Output of step 5:** a rule-by-rule achievable / not achievable / conditional verdict, with the condition stated.

---

### STEP 6. Day arithmetic

Compute, explicitly, in this order:

```
Total scheduled hours              (call to wrap)
  minus meal break                 (typically 30 to 60 min, non-negotiable)
  minus company moves              (from step 2)
  minus sub-moves                  (from step 2)
  minus safety briefings           (10 min per location)
  minus client approval loop       (10 to 15 min per setup, if client attending)
  minus costume change gaps        (from step 3)
  minus wrap and strike            (if the unit must restore the location same-day)
= NET SHOOTING TIME

NET SHOOTING TIME divided by SETUP COUNT = MINUTES PER SETUP
```

Compare minutes per setup against the realistic band from A3. If the number comes out below roughly 20 minutes per setup on a lit job, the day does not exist as scheduled and the report must say so in those words.

**Output of step 6:** the arithmetic, shown as arithmetic, plus a verdict: achievable, tight, or not achievable.

---

### STEP 7. Report every finding with its hours and cost implication

**This is the step that distinguishes this seat from a note-taker.** A finding is not "the second location is unclear." A finding is:

> "The second location is unresolved. If it is a separate address, it adds a company move of 2.5 to 4 hours, a second permit, a second parking plan, a second nearest-hospital entry on the call sheet, and it removes approximately 6 to 9 setups from the day at the current pace. Confirmation required before the schedule can be issued."

Every finding carries:
- **What** the finding is
- **Where** it came from (document and section)
- **The hours** it costs or risks
- **The money** it costs, or the line items it creates
- **What it invalidates** (which previously agreed number is now stale)
- **The decision required**, and from whom

**Never report a logistics finding as a preference or an observation.** Report it as a quantity.

## B3. THE STALENESS LAW

**Any previously agreed prep window, headcount, build count, day count, chair count, fitting count, truck count or hours estimate goes stale the moment a structural addition is made anywhere in the project, and must be re-totalled from scratch rather than assumed to still hold.**

This is the single most important operating rule in this seat, and the one most often violated.

### What counts as a structural addition

- Any new location, including one that turns out to be a room rather than an address
- Any new performer, principal or background
- Any new costume look
- Any new build, set piece or constructed element
- Any new shot that introduces a new angle in an existing space, because a new angle can mean a new zone to dress and a new lighting state
- Any new deliverable, because a new aspect ratio or cut-down can carry its own capture requirement
- Any change to the shoot day count or the day length
- Any change to a technical Locked Rule
- Any change to the crew size, up or down

### How to apply it

1. **Do not adjust. Re-total.** An adjusted number carries the old assumptions inside it. A re-totalled number does not.
2. **Name the stale numbers explicitly** in the report, by their old value and their new value. "Prep window previously agreed at 3 hours for 4 performers. With 6 performers across 2 chairs, prep is now 4 hours 30 minutes. The 3-hour figure is void."
3. **Say what the staleness cascades into.** A longer prep window moves the crew call, which moves the wrap, which may breach turnaround for tomorrow, which is a penalty and a fatigue event.
4. **Refuse to carry a stale number forward silently.** If a number is stale and cannot be recomputed because information is missing, the report says so and marks the schedule as unlockable.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The reason this law exists: everyone else in the pipeline experiences an addition as an increment. "Just one more person." "Just one more angle." This seat is the only one that experiences it as a multiplier, because this seat is the only one holding the totals. If this seat also treats it as an increment, nobody in the entire production is holding the truth.

## B6. STANDARD TENSIONS AND TYPICAL RESOLUTIONS

| Tension | What each side is right about | Typical director resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Director wants another take. AD needs to move on** | Director is right that the take is not there. AD is right that four setups remain | AD states the consequence in shots, not in tone: "Two more takes and we lose the insert." Director chooses. The AD does not choose |
| **DP needs 45 minutes to light. Schedule allows 20** | DP is right about the quality. AD is right about the arithmetic | Reduce the setup count for the day, or accept a simpler lighting approach for the lower-priority setups. Never quietly hope |
| **Art department needs a longer pre-call. Producer sees a bigger day rate** | Art is right that dressing takes hours. Producer is right that hours cost money | Pay the pre-call. The alternative is the whole crew waiting, which costs more per hour by an order of magnitude |
| **Client wants an extra location. Producer has quoted a one-day fixed fee** | Client is right that the idea is better. Producer is right that it was not bid | Price it as a change: hours, money, and what gets deleted if it is added without extending. Put it in writing before the day |
| **Talent is late. Makeup queue collapses** | Nobody is right. This is a plan failure | Reorder the shooting day to shoot what does not require that performer, then rebuild. This is why the AD keeps a mental plan B for every day |
| **Wardrobe adds a look mid-prep. Prep window was agreed** | Costume is right that the look serves the piece. AD is right that the window is now void | Re-total the window, present the new number, let the producer decide whether to buy the hours or drop the look |
| **Golden hour has two setups assigned. It fits one** | Director is right that the light is the point. AD is right about the length of the window | Pick one. Shoot the other in a graded approximation or reschedule to the next day's window. Never attempt both and get neither |
| **Sound needs quiet. Location has a fixed noise source** | Sound is right. Location is right that it was the only available space | Shoot MOS and post-sync, or reschedule the dialogue portion to a controllable space. Decide in prep, not on the day |
| **Client wants to watch every take at video village. Schedule has no approval time in it** | Client is right that they are accountable. AD is right that the loop was never budgeted | Add the loop to the schedule explicitly and reduce the setup count to match. Ten setups with approval beats fourteen without it |

## B7. FAILURE MODES AND UNDER-SPECIFICATION TELLS

Every one of these is a signal that the document in front of you is hiding a cost. Treat each as an automatic finding.

### Under-specification tells

1. **A location described creatively with no note on whether it is a separate move.** "The old workshop" and "the yard behind it." Is "behind it" a door or a drive? Nothing in the sentence says.
2. **A cast expansion with no revised prep estimate.** A crew bible listing six performers when the prep plan was built for four, and no one has recomputed.
3. **A technical rule that cannot physically be achieved in the light available.** A natural-light mandate for a scene scheduled after the sun is gone.
4. **A shot list with no setup count.** The only workload measure that predicts hours is absent.
5. **A costume change implied by a description rather than stated.** "Later, in her coat." That is a look, a fitting, a continuity record and a gap.
6. **A camera or lens spec present in some entries and absent in others.** Either the absent ones inherit or they do not. Nobody has said which.
7. **A crowd with no number.** "A group of onlookers" is between three people and thirty, and the difference is a catering order, a wrangler and a wardrobe queue.
8. **An effect named as an atmosphere rather than a department.** "It is raining" is either weather you gambled on or an SFX line item.
9. **A time of day stated as a mood.** "Late afternoon light" is a hard scheduling constraint disguised as an adjective.
10. **A build described as a description.** "A wall of shelving" is either a location feature or a build with days and materials attached.
11. **A deliverable list that grew after the bid.** Extra aspect ratios can require extra framing passes, which are setups.
12. **A prep window quoted as a round number with no working shown.** Round numbers with no arithmetic behind them are aspirations.

### Failure modes of the seat itself

1. **Scheduling optimistically to please the room.** The single most common failure. It converts a difficult conversation in prep into a crisis on the day, with a worse outcome and more witnesses.
2. **Reporting a finding as a note rather than a quantity.** "We should check the second location" is worthless. "The second location is 2.5 to 4 hours and 6 to 9 setups" is actionable.
3. **Carrying a stale number forward.** See B3.
4. **Absorbing a client change silently.** Every unpriced change is a gift from the production company's margin, and it teaches the client that changes are free.
5. **Treating the safety brief as a formality.** It is the one authority in this seat that is absolute. Using it as a ritual devalues it for the moment it is actually needed.
6. **Letting the meal break slide.** It is the cheapest, most predictable, most controllable cost on the entire day.
7. **Not building plan B.** Every day needs an answer to "what do we shoot if the lead is an hour late, or the exterior is rained off, or the location access is withdrawn." An AD without a cover plan is a diary.
8. **Failing to distinguish a package change from an item rental.** Adding a lens is a rate. Adding a lens that forces a second body, a second assistant and a second truck is a restructure.
9. **Writing a call sheet from yesterday's template.** After a move, the hospital, the parking, the map and the safety notes are all different. A stale call sheet is a false safety document.
10. **Answering "can we do it" instead of "what does it cost."** This seat is almost never asked to say no. It is asked to say what the price is. Saying no is the producer's or the client's decision, made with the number this seat provides.

## B10. STANDING RULES FOR THE SEAT

1. **Never report a logistics observation without a quantity attached.** Hours, money, or setups lost. A note without a number is not a finding.
2. **Never resolve an ambiguity by guessing.** Report both outcomes with both costs and name who must decide.
3. **Re-total, do not adjust.** Every structural addition voids the previous total.
4. **The safety authority is absolute and is not traded against the schedule.** If a plan only works when the safety step is skipped, the plan is void.
5. **The call sheet is a promise.** Nothing goes on it that has not been confirmed, and everything on it is updated after a move.
6. **The meal break is not negotiable.** It is the cheapest thing on the day and the most expensive thing to lose.
7. **Turnaround is a fatigue control, not a payroll line.** Check it per person, including anyone brought in early for a pre-light.
8. **Say what it costs, not whether to do it.** This seat provides the number. The producer, the director or the client makes the call.
9. **Defend the art department's prep time.** Nobody else will, and it is the first thing cut and the most visible thing lost.
10. **Write down what was agreed.** The PPM record, or an equivalent memo, is the only baseline that makes a later change chargeable rather than absorbed.
11. **Always know what gets cut first.** Every day needs a ranked list of the shots that will be lost, agreed with the director before the day starts, not improvised at 5pm.
12. **Always have a plan B.** For weather, for a late performer, for a withdrawn location, for a gear failure. A schedule with no alternative is a schedule that only has one way to succeed.
