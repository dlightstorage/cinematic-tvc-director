### The HMU Department Craft Bible

*A masterclass reference and an agent operating manual for the hair and makeup department across film, television, and commercial production.*

# PART A: CRAFT REFERENCE

## A1. What the Role Owns, and Where Its Authority Ends

The Makeup and Hair Designer (in feature credit language, the Hair and Makeup Designer, or on American union productions the Makeup Department Head and Hair Department Head as two parallel positions) owns **the readable surface of every human being who appears in frame**.

That surface breaks into five ownable things:

1. **Skin.** Tone correction, texture management, blemish and shine control, sun damage, illness, exhaustion, bruising, dirt, sweat, tears, blood.
2. **Feature emphasis.** Eyes, brows, lips, cheekbones, jaw. What the camera is pulled toward on a face.
3. **Hair.** Cut, colour, styling, period accuracy, wigs, hairpieces, facial hair, wear-and-tear across a story day.
4. **Prosthetics and character transformation.** Appliances, bald caps, aging, scarring, creature and species work, body makeup, tattoos.
5. **Continuity of all of the above** across a shooting schedule that almost never runs in story order.

The role's authority is real but bounded. Three fences matter.

**Fence one: the actor's body and consent.** The designer proposes, the performer lives inside it for twelve hours. Nadia Stacey, who won the Academy Award for *Poor Things* and a BAFTA for *The Favourite*, has described her working relationship with Emma Stone as built on trust across three films, to the point where she could propose spray-painting text across Stone's face for *Cruella* and get an immediate yes `[SOURCED]`. That yes is the product of relationship, not of authority. A designer who forgets this loses the performance long before they lose the look.

**Fence two: the frame.** The designer does not decide what the camera sees. The Director of Photography and the director do. HMU can only specify to the resolution the frame will actually deliver, and must be told what that is.

**Fence three: the schedule.** The 1st Assistant Director owns the clock. A three-hour prosthetic build is a design decision only if the AD has agreed to a call time that contains it. Frances Hannon's team on *Wicked* averaged roughly two hours fifteen minutes to two hours forty-five minutes per day on Cynthia Erivo's full green makeup, having driven it down from longer `[SOURCED]`. That number is a schedule negotiation as much as it is a craft achievement.

Where the role's authority genuinely ends: **it does not own the final image.** Colour grading, VFX cleanup, and beauty retouching can undo or amplify anything HMU does. The designer's leverage over that is influence and pre-agreement, not veto `[PRINCIPLE]`.

## A2. The Decision Set

Every face in a production resolves into one of a small number of registers. Naming the register is the first and most consequential decision, because it determines product, time, crew size, and camera tolerance.

| Register | What it is | Typical time in chair | Camera risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **No-makeup makeup** | Correction only. Skin evened, shine controlled, brows groomed, nothing that reads as applied. | 15 to 30 min | Reads as "done" if base does not match tone exactly |
| **Naturalistic / character-appropriate** | The makeup the character would plausibly wear in their own life. | 20 to 45 min | Anachronism, over-perfection |
| **Beauty / polished** | Deliberately camera-ready. Defined eye, sculpted skin, controlled lip. | 45 to 90 min | Product texture, powder catch, dated technique |
| **Character makeup** | Aging, illness, injury, grime, weather, occupation. Paint and stipple, no appliances. | 30 min to 2 hr | Muddiness, symmetry, "painted on" edges |
| **Prosthetic transformation** | Sculpted appliances bonded to skin, then painted and blended. | 2 to 6 hr, plus removal | Edges, seams, translucency, motion |
| **Creature / full conversion** | Full head, body, dentures, lenses, hair punching. | 4 hr and up | Everything above, plus performance restriction |

**Beauty vs character is not a hierarchy.** Ve Neill, a three-time Academy Award winner for *Beetlejuice*, *Mrs. Doubtfire*, and *Ed Wood*, has worked as department head across both registers on productions with background casts numbering upward of fifty per day, and has described the department's core planning act as a breakdown that times out how many people work per day and how many minutes each makeup takes `[SOURCED]`. The register determines the arithmetic. The arithmetic determines whether the day is possible.

**Prosthetics have their own decision tree.** Kazu Hiro (formerly Kazuhiro Tsuji), who won Academy Awards for *Darkest Hour* and *Bombshell*, works from lifecast and 3D scan, and has integrated Artec 3D scanning and printing into his lifecasting workflow `[SOURCED]`. For Charlize Theron as Megyn Kelly he built a nose plug and nose tape, a chin piece (the real subject's chin being longer), jawline pieces for angularity, and eyelid adjustment (Theron being deep-socketed, the subject heavy-hooded), plus a contact lens to darken eye colour, in a roughly three-hour daily application `[SOURCED]`. The lesson is not the pieces. The lesson is that a transformation is an **enumerated list of specific anatomical deltas**, not a vibe.

Greg Cannom, Oscar winner for *Bram Stoker's Dracula*, *Mrs. Doubtfire*, *The Curious Case of Benjamin Button*, and *Vice*, put the hardest constraint plainly: "With monsters, you design whatever you want. With age makeup, everybody knows what they look like, so it's got to be really good." `[SOURCED]` Realism is the strict register. Fantasy is the forgiving one.

**Hair design is a parallel, equally deep discipline** and should never be treated as an accessory to makeup. Jenny Shircore, who won the Academy Award for *Elizabeth*, has described period work as beginning with primary research: for Tudor and Elizabethan work she researched smallpox presentation and had to decide which stage of the disease was most relevant to the story, and she had to find modern equivalents for period cosmetics that were historically mixed with mercury and other toxic substances `[SOURCED]`. On heavy days for Margot Robbie in *Mary Queen of Scots* the build was around three hours, comprising a skin condition, a thinning wig over a bald cap, and the heavy white base `[SOURCED]`. Hair, skin condition, and base were one design, not three.

**Continuity across shooting days** is the discipline that separates a department from a crew. Scenes shoot out of order. The department is responsible for ensuring everyone on camera carries the correct look for where the character sits chronologically, and for touching up on set so each shot matches `[SOURCED]`. In practice this means continuity stills for every character in every state, logged by scene number, and a named artist responsible for each principal.

**Touch-up discipline on set** is a rhythm, not an event. The department watches the monitor, moves in on the "last looks" call before a take, works fast, and gets out of the DP's light. The good version is invisible to the schedule. The bad version becomes the reason a company falls behind `[PRINCIPLE]`.

## A3. How Camera Resolution and Sensor Sensitivity Changed the Craft

This is the single largest technical shift in the department's modern history, and it happened twice: once at the standard-definition to high-definition transition, and again with 4K, 6K, 8K acquisition on large-format sensors.

**What higher resolution actually exposes.** When broadcast and cinema moved from SD to HD, on-air talent and performers were openly concerned that the new resolution would exaggerate blemishes and fine lines, and the department's most visible response was to promote airbrushing as the primary solution for the high-definition era `[SOURCED]`. HD-specific product lines emerged built around ultra-fine, light-scattering pigments such as quartz and silica microparticles, designed to diffuse light away from pores and fine lines rather than to sit on top of them, with markedly lighter texture than conventional camera makeup `[SOURCED]`.

**Why heavy product reads as product.** At sufficient resolution the camera resolves the *particle*, not just the *colour*. Traditional heavy foundation is an opaque layer with a visible edge, a visible texture, and a light response that is fundamentally different from skin. Skin is translucent, subsurface-scattering, and irregular. An opaque layer is none of those things. Once the sensor can resolve pore-level detail, a coverage-first approach stops reading as "flawless skin" and starts reading as "a layer of something on a face" `[PRINCIPLE], consistent with the sourced HD-product rationale above`.

**The practical response, in order of importance:**

1. **Skin prep replaced coverage.** Cleansing, exfoliation, hydration, and the right primer do more at 4K than any amount of foundation. The base becomes correction, not concealment.
2. **Thin, buildable, sheer layers.** Multiple micro-layers instead of one heavy one.
3. **Airbrush for even, low-volume deposition.** Airbrush and ultra-HD formulations both exist specifically because ultra-sharp lenses and high-resolution acquisition demanded finishes that survive that scrutiny `[SOURCED]`.
4. **Powder discipline.** Powder is the most common HD failure. Too much and it flattens, catches in lines, and reads chalky under a hard key.
5. **Colour matching became forensic.** A base half a step off in undertone is invisible at SD and obvious at 4K `[PRINCIPLE]`.

**On the prosthetic side the same pressure changed materials.** High-resolution acquisition is more sensitive to colour and to material behaviour, and the demand for appliances that look and move like real skin pushed the industry toward silicone, whose translucency, flexibility, and durability suit close-up work at high definition better than foam latex, while foam latex retains its place for full-head and body work where weight and comfort dominate `[SOURCED]`. Mark Coulier, Oscar winner for *The Iron Lady* and *The Grand Budapest Hotel*, works in exactly this idiom: for *Suspiria* he built a tubular silicone neck plus separate cheeks, chin, top lip, nose, forehead, ears, back of head, hand prosthetics, fingernails, wig, and body padding `[SOURCED]`. Multi-piece silicone exists because a single rigid piece cannot survive both HD scrutiny and facial motion.

**Large sensors add a second, less discussed problem: shallow depth of field.** On a large-format sensor at a wide aperture, the plane of focus can be thin enough that the tip of a nose and the ear are not equally sharp. Makeup that was designed to be read as a whole face is now read as a razor-thin slice of face `[PRINCIPLE]`. Practically: edges of appliances and blend lines that fall outside the focus plane are forgiven, and anything on the focus plane is not.

**Sensor sensitivity changed the lighting, which changed the makeup.** Modern sensors expose comfortably at light levels that would have been unusable on film stock, which is why contemporary productions light with practicals, screens, and small soft sources. Lower, softer, more motivated light is far less forgiving of makeup designed for hot, bright, frontal keys, because there is no longer a wash of light flattening the surface `[PRINCIPLE]`.

## A4. The "Looks Like Nobody Did Anything" Discipline

This is the most misunderstood part of the department's work, and it is where most under-briefed productions fail.

**"No makeup" and "no makeup department" are completely different instructions.**

A face with genuinely nothing on it, under a modern sensor and a hard practical source, does not read as authentic. It reads as shiny, blotchy, and unevenly toned, with visible flush around the nose, a bright T-zone, and dark under-eye. It reads as **an accident**, not as a choice. The audience does not think "how real." The audience thinks "how did nobody notice."

What the department actually does to achieve an un-made-up read `[PRINCIPLE], corroborated by working-artist accounts` `[SOURCED]`:

- **Prep and hydrate** so the skin's own surface behaves.
- **Correct, do not cover.** Spot-conceal only what breaks the read. Leave freckles, moles, texture, and natural colour variation. These are the things that sell "real."
- **Match the base to the exact undertone.** Working artists identify base-to-skin mismatch as the single dead giveaway of a supposedly no-makeup look `[SOURCED]`.
- **Control shine, not sheen.** Skin should still look alive. A completely matte face is as artificial as a heavily made-up one.
- **Groom the brows and lashes.** Brow shape is disproportionately responsible for whether a face reads as "put together" or "just woke up."
- **Manage the lip.** A bare lip on camera often reads paler and flatter than in life. A tint that returns the lip to its own natural colour is invisible and necessary.
- **Warm slightly.** Practitioners describe using warmer tones to keep actors from washing out under bright sources while still reading natural `[SOURCED]`.

The industry framing is blunt: the "no makeup" look is a professional face with no visible flaws, bringing out natural features while preventing wash-out `[SOURCED]`. It is a **result**, achieved by work, not the absence of work.

The design instruction that follows from this: **never write "no makeup" on a character sheet.** Write "no-makeup makeup, correction-only, texture retained." The first sentence gets you a shiny actor and a lost hour. The second gets you what you actually wanted.

## A5. Skin Under Different Lighting

Makeup does not have a fixed appearance. It has a response curve to whatever light hits it. Designing without the lighting plan is designing blind `[PRINCIPLE]`.

**Warm key vs cool key.**

| Key light | What it does to skin | What makeup must compensate |
|---|---|---|
| Warm (tungsten, firelight, sodium practicals, low sun) | Pushes skin toward orange/red. Flattens redness *distinction*, so blemishes and flush blend in but overall face can read sunburnt or jaundiced. | Cool the base marginally. Reduce warm blush and bronzer. Watch yellow-heavy foundations turning sallow. |
| Cool (daylight-balanced LED, north-facing window, overcast, moonlight-effect) | Emphasises redness, blue-green undertones under the eye, and any grey in the skin. Makes people read tired and ill. | Warm-correct the base. Colour-correct under-eye. Add controlled warmth at cheek and jaw so the face is not corpse-like. |
| Mixed (practical tungsten plus daylight window) | Different halves of the face read as different skin tones. | Split-difference base, and flag it to the DP early. This is a lighting problem before it is a makeup problem. |

**Hard vs soft sources.**

- **Hard light** (bare source, direct sun, small practical) throws sharp shadows, so it reveals texture, edges, prosthetic seams, powder buildup in fine lines, and any unblended boundary. Everything must be blended further and applied thinner.
- **Soft light** (large diffusion, bounce, overcast, big window) wraps and fills, so it hides texture and edges but also flattens dimension. Under soft light the makeup often has to *add back* structure with subtle contour and highlight that would look overdone under hard light.

**Screen light and practical light.** Contemporary productions frequently key faces off monitors, phones, televisions, and neon. These sources are highly saturated, often at extreme colour temperatures, and close to the face. A face lit by a blue screen at close range needs a base with enough warmth in it to survive the cast, and blush and lip choices that do not turn brown or grey under the specific hue `[PRINCIPLE]`. The only reliable way to know is a camera test with the actual practical.

**Sweat and shine under hot practicals.**
This is the department's most frequent on-set battle. The management stack, in order:

1. Cool the actor between takes (fans, cold packs at the neck, ice water). Prevention beats correction.
2. Blot before powdering. Powder over sweat cakes instantly.
3. Micro-powder, targeted at T-zone, upper lip, and hairline only.
4. Setting spray at build, not mid-take.
5. Accept and control. In a scene that is *supposed* to be hot, the answer is a designed, even sheen, sometimes deliberately applied, rather than an eliminated one. Bertolazzi has described an organic approach to rain-drenched, grimy sequences and finding creative possibilities in everyday materials `[SOURCED]`.

The corollary: **wet and sweaty looks must be designed, not improvised**, because "real sweat" on camera is patchy, dries between takes, and is a continuity disaster.

## A6. Documentary-Realist vs Polished-Commercial Registers

These are two coherent, internally consistent worlds. Problems arise almost entirely from mixing them by accident.

**Documentary-realist register.** Skin retains texture, pores, uneven tone, and marks. Hair is styled the way the character would style it, including badly. Nothing is symmetrical. Nothing looks fresh. Makeup wears down across a story day and the department tracks that wear. The audience should be unable to name a single choice.

**Polished-commercial register.** Skin reads as an ideal surface. Hair holds. Lip and eye are defined and consistent. Symmetry is pursued. Nothing wears down unless the story asks it to. The audience is meant to register the person as *presented*.

**The deliberate exception, and how to scope it.**
The single most common under-specification failure in a brief is a blanket rule ("nobody looks done up," "everyone is unpolished, real people") that silently contradicts a named exception ("the presenter is camera-ready," "the influencer character is fully made up," "the product demonstrator looks flawless").

The correct way to write the rule is to **scope it and name the exceptions in the same sentence**:

> "Documentary-realist register applies to all characters except [named character(s)], whose polish is diegetic: they are made up *within the world of the story* because their job or self-presentation requires it. Their polish must read as their own choice, not as the production's."

That last clause is the craft distinction. A polished character inside an unpolished ensemble should look like **a person who does their own makeup well**, not like a person a film crew made up. Practically that means:

- Their finish is slightly imperfect in believable ways (a lip that is not perfectly lined, a base that stops at the jaw, a lash that is real rather than false).
- Their polish is consistent with their access and budget as a character.
- The contrast with the ensemble is deliberate and legible in the same shot, so the audience reads it as characterisation rather than as inconsistency.

If the exception cannot survive being in the same frame as the unpolished characters, the exception is wrong, or the blanket rule is `[PRINCIPLE]`.

## A7. Partial-Frame vs Face-Forward

A large share of commercial and product work never shows a face. Getting this scoping right is where a department saves or wastes money.

**When the camera only sees a hand, forearm, shoulder, or back of head:**

- **Hands are their own discipline.** Nails cleaned, shaped, and either bare-buffed or in an agreed colour. Cuticles managed. Knuckle redness corrected. Veins can be reduced with base or raised deliberately. Hydration matters because dry skin on a hand at macro is unusable.
- **Skin evenness on the forearm.** Tan lines, watch marks, bruises, tattoos, scars, and healing marks all need a verdict: keep, cover, or reposition.
- **Hair on the arm** needs a decision. At macro on a large sensor, arm hair is extremely visible and often unflattering under a hard source.
- **Continuity is still mandatory.** A hand in a shot on day one and the same hand on day four must match.
- **No face means no face makeup.** This is a legitimate, cost-saving verdict, and it should be written down as a verdict, not omitted.

**Everything that changes the moment a face enters frame:**

| What changes | Why |
|---|---|
| Time in chair | Minutes become tens of minutes, minimum |
| Skin prep | Now mandatory, including the night before for a hero close-up |
| Base | Full undertone match required, not spot correction |
| Shine management | Becomes a continuous on-set task |
| Hair | Full styling and continuity, not just "tidy" |
| Continuity stills | Required per scene state |
| Crew count | At least one dedicated artist per principal, plus on-set touch-up capacity |
| Camera test | Now genuinely required, not optional |
| Grooming | Facial hair, brows, nostril and ear hair, teeth all in scope |

The trigger is absolute: **any change to framing that puts a previously unseen face in shot forces a full re-derivation of that character's specification, not an edit to the existing note** `[PRINCIPLE]`.

## A9. Vocabulary Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Continuity stills** | Reference photographs of each character in each state, logged against scene numbers, used to rebuild a look on a later shooting day. |
| **Touch-up** | On-set maintenance between takes: shine, hair, lip, sweat, displaced product. |
| **Last looks** | The AD's call, immediately before a take, that gives HMU and wardrobe a final few seconds at the actor. |
| **Airbrush** | Compressed-air application of thinned product in fine, even, low-volume layers. Adopted broadly as the HD-era answer to coverage without texture. |
| **Stipple** | Application by dabbing rather than stroking, with sponge, brush, or textured tool, to build irregular, skin-like texture (broken capillaries, five o'clock shadow, grime, bruising). |
| **Prosthetic appliance** | A pre-sculpted piece (silicone, foam latex, gelatine, encapsulated silicone) bonded to the skin, then blended and painted. |
| **Lifecast** | A full negative mould of a performer's face or body, from which appliances are sculpted. Increasingly supplemented or replaced by 3D scanning. |
| **Bald cap** | A thin cap creating a shaved or hairless head, usually the foundation layer for a wig or prosthetic build. |
| **Lace front** | A wig or facial-hair piece with a fine lace edge that allows an invisible hairline at close range. |
| **Punching / knotting** | Hand-inserting individual hairs into lace or silicone for realistic hairlines, brows, and beards. |
| **Blend / edge** | The transition between appliance and skin. The most scrutinised millimetre in the department. |
| **HMU trailer** | The mobile makeup and hair unit on location. Also the department's de facto planning room and the reason call times exist. |
| **First team** | The principal cast, in position for rehearsal and shooting. |
| **Second team** | Stand-ins who occupy the marks while lighting is set, sparing first team hours under hot sources. HMU's ally, because it protects the makeup. |
| **Beauty pass** | A separately captured take or lighting setup optimised for how a face or product reads, as distinct from the coverage pass built for action or blocking. In post it also refers to a dedicated retouch stage. |
| **Breakdown** | The department's scene-by-scene, character-by-character analysis of required looks, times, and crew. |
| **Camera test** | A pre-shoot capture of the actual cast in the actual makeup under the actual lighting, through the actual lens and LUT. |
| **Set life** | How long a makeup survives under working conditions before it needs rebuilding rather than touching up. |
| **Removal** | The scheduled, non-negotiable end-of-day process. Prosthetic removal can take a substantial fraction of application time and must be in the call sheet. |

## A10. How Commercial and Beauty Work Differs from Feature Work

The craft is the same. The economics, the approval structure, and the tolerance for imperfection are not.

**1. Product-adjacent skin standards.** When the product being sold is applied to or near the skin (cosmetics, skincare, razors, food, jewellery, fragrance), the skin *is* the product demonstration. In beauty photography specifically, skin is the focus of the image and can occupy up to ninety percent of it, which makes it the most time-consuming and highest-skill retouching category `[SOURCED]`. That standard travels upstream into what the makeup artist must deliver on the day.

**2. Restraint is a commercial skill.** Working commercial artists describe most clients as wanting either modern polished skin or a no-makeup-makeup finish, with makeup expected to complement rather than compete with the product `[SOURCED]`. The instinct to demonstrate skill is the enemy of a beauty campaign.

**3. Real-time client approval.** On a commercial set, hair and makeup wait for the photographer's or director's approval on test frames and then make fast adjustments, reviewing captures immediately to identify what needs to change `[SOURCED]`. In feature work the equivalent conversation happens in prep and camera test; in commercial work it happens continuously, in front of a client, on a monitor.

**4. Practical vs post is a negotiated line, and it should be negotiated in writing.** Professional artists deliberately design to reduce reliance on heavy retouching, because getting it right on set is faster and better `[SOURCED]`. But even with a strong artist on set, the client's final vision typically requires additional colour and makeup manipulation in post `[SOURCED]`. Best practice named in the field: book a shared reference call, document measurable skin goals, and define retouch rounds, approvers, and deadlines in writing before the shoot `[SOURCED]`.

The working split `[PRINCIPLE]`:

| Do practically | Leave to post |
|---|---|
| Base match, undertone, evenness | Stray-hair cleanup on a moving frame |
| Texture and finish (matte, satin, dewy) | Sensor dust, rig removal |
| Shine and sweat control | Minor continuity mismatches between takes |
| Eye, lip, brow shape and colour | Global colour temperature and skin-tone grade |
| Hairline, flyaways, parting | Blemish removal where the actor genuinely developed one overnight |
| Anything that has to move naturally in a moving shot | Anything that would require removing real skin texture |

The line to hold: **do not remove texture practically that post could remove digitally, because texture cannot be added back convincingly** `[PRINCIPLE]`. Over-applied product is a permanent decision. Retouching is a reversible one.

**5. Ethics and standards.** Retouching in commercial photography is a contested area with published guidelines and active industry debate about how far manipulation should go `[SOURCED]`. Some markets and some categories carry regulatory or platform-level restrictions on how much a beauty result may be enhanced. The designer should know the rules of the market the work will run in before designing to them.

# PART B: AGENT OPERATING MANUAL

*This half is written so that an AI subagent can occupy the role of Makeup & Hair Designer inside a production process, and produce a defensible department plan.*

## B1. The Questions This Role Asks on Intake

Handed a script, a shot list, and a cast list, the designer asks these in order. Refusing to proceed without answers is correct behaviour, not obstruction.

**About the world**
1. What is the period, and is the period strict or stylised?
2. What is the register: documentary-realist, naturalistic, polished-commercial, heightened, or fantastical?
3. Are there multiple worlds or factions in the same story? Does each need a distinct HMU logic?
4. Is there a named exception to the dominant register? Who, and is their polish diegetic (a choice their character makes) or authorial?

**About the frame**
5. What is the acquisition format and resolution, and what sensor size?
6. What is the tightest shot in the piece, and on whom?
7. Which characters are ever seen face-forward, and which are only ever partial-frame?
8. What are the lens choices and typical apertures? How thin is the focus plane on close-ups?
9. Are there macro or extreme close-up frames on skin, hands, hair, eyes, or lips?

**About light**
10. What is the key light colour temperature per scene, warm or cool?
11. Hard or soft? Motivated by practicals, screens, windows, or units?
12. Are there hot practicals close to the face, or long hours under exposure that will produce sweat?
13. Is there a LUT or show look, and can I see the cast through it before I lock anything?

**About people**
14. Who is actually cast? Real skin tones, real skin conditions, real hair, real allergies, real facial hair, real tattoos.
15. Any performer-specific constraints: sensitivity to adhesives, contact-lens tolerance, claustrophobia inside appliances, prior haircut commitments, contractual restrictions on cutting or colouring hair.
16. Any minors, which changes both product choice and hours.
17. How many background performers, in what states, on which days?

**About story-time**
18. What is the story-day map? How many distinct chronological states does each character pass through?
19. Which scenes involve degradation: injury, weather, water, blood, dirt, exhaustion, crying, aging?
20. Are any of these states irreversible within a shooting day (a haircut, a shave, a real dye)?

**About the schedule and the money**
21. What are the call times, and how many hours before crew call can HMU start?
22. How many chairs, how many artists, how many days?
23. Is there a camera and makeup test day, and if not, why not?
24. What is the removal time allowance at the end of each day?

**About the back end**
25. What is the colourist's intention for skin tone: warm and rich, cool and desaturated, filmic and lifted, high contrast?
26. What will be fixed in post, and what must be right in camera? Who signs that off?
27. Who approves the look, and at what stage does approval lock?

## B2. Decision Checklist in Dependency Order

Work top to bottom. Do not skip. Each step depends on the one above it.

1. **Read the script and build the story-day map.** Every character, every scene, every chronological state.
2. **Confirm the register**, including scoped exceptions, in writing.
3. **Confirm the frame**: format, resolution, tightest shot per character, who is ever face-forward.
4. **Confirm the light** per scene: colour temperature, hardness, practicals, heat.
5. **Get the actual cast.** Nothing before this point is a specification, only an intention.
6. **Classify every named character** into a register from the table in A2. Include partial-frame characters with an explicit verdict.
7. **Identify transformations** that need prosthetics, wigs, or facial hair, and separate them from paint-only work.
8. **Cost each look in minutes**, per character, per state.
9. **Multiply by cast and days.** Produce the chair-hours requirement.
10. **Negotiate chair-hours against the call sheet** with the 1st AD. If the arithmetic does not fit, redesign now, not on the day.
11. **Reconcile with wardrobe palette and colourist intent.** Adjust base warmth, lip, and hair colour so nothing fights the costume or the grade.
12. **Run the camera test**: real cast, real makeup, real lighting, real lens, real LUT.
13. **Lock and document.** Continuity stills, product lists, application notes, per-character sheets.
14. **Assign artists to principals** and name an on-set continuity owner.
15. **Plan removal and recovery**, including skin care for performers in daily appliances.
16. **Establish the change-trigger protocol** (see B7) with the AD and the DP so that upstream changes reach the department before the day, not on it.

## B3. What This Role Needs from Other Departments Before It Can Lock Anything

| From | What is needed | Why it blocks |
|---|---|---|
| **Director** | Register, tone, the emotional read of each character, and explicit rulings on exceptions | Without the register the department is guessing at the whole design |
| **Director of Photography** | Format, resolution, sensor size, lens set, typical stop, lighting plan per scene (colour temperature, hardness, practicals), show LUT, and the tightest framing per character | Determines product weight, finish, blend tolerance, and whether a face is even seen |
| **Casting** | The final, actual cast. Real skin tones, hair, conditions, allergies, tattoos, facial hair, and any contractual restrictions | A design built on a casting brief rather than a cast person is fiction |
| **Costume / Wardrobe** | Full palette, fabric sheen, necklines, headwear, collars, and the change schedule per character | Base warmth, lip, and hair colour must sit against the costume, not fight it. Necklines determine how far body makeup extends. Headwear determines wig construction |
| **Production Design / Art** | Set and location palette and the colour of practical sources | A set that throws green or amber onto every face changes every base |
| **Colourist / Post** | Skin-tone intention, the show LUT, and the agreed practical/post split | Prevents building a look that the grade will undo, or leaving something to post that post cannot fix |
| **1st Assistant Director** | Call times, day-out-of-days, number of chairs, hours available before crew call, background counts per day, and removal windows | Converts design into feasibility. This is the hardest constraint in the department |
| **VFX** | Any digital extension, de-aging, face replacement, or cleanup planned, plus tracking-marker requirements | Determines whether a prosthetic is the final image or a base layer |
| **Stunts / SFX** | Water, fire, wind, rain, blood rigs, harness points | Determines set life, waterproofing, and rebuild frequency |
| **Script Supervisor** | The continuity log and scene order | HMU continuity is only as good as the shared record of story order |

## B5. Per-Character Enumeration Discipline

**The rule: every named character in the script gets a written specification. No character is omitted. Light-touch characters get a written verdict, not silence.**

An omission is indistinguishable from an oversight. A verdict is a decision. The difference matters because downstream departments read the plan as a complete list.

Minimum fields per character:

```
CHARACTER: [name or role label]
SEEN: [face-forward / partial-frame only / background]
TIGHTEST FRAME: [e.g. MCU, or CU on hands only]
REGISTER: [no-makeup makeup / naturalistic / polished / character / prosthetic]
STORY STATES: [state 1, state 2, state 3, with scene numbers]
SKIN: [prep, base approach, correction level, texture retained or reduced, finish]
FEATURES: [brow, eye, lip, cheek, or "none" with reason]
HAIR: [cut, colour, styling, wig or own hair, per state]
FACIAL / BODY HAIR: [verdict]
BODY MAKEUP: [coverage boundary, or "none"]
PROSTHETICS: [pieces, or "none"]
LIGHTING NOTES: [key temperature and hardness in their scenes, and the compensation]
CHAIR TIME: [minutes, per state]
ARTIST ASSIGNED: [name]
CONTINUITY OWNER: [name]
RISKS: [sweat, water, adhesive sensitivity, long day under hot practicals]
```

A valid light-touch entry looks like this, and it is a specification, not an omission:

> **SEEN:** Partial-frame only (hands and forearm, scenes 12 and 14). **REGISTER:** Grooming pass only. **SKIN:** Hand and forearm hydration, cuticle tidy, nails buffed bare, knuckle redness knocked back with sheer base to the wrist. **FEATURES:** None required, face not in frame. **HAIR:** Not in frame. **CHAIR TIME:** 15 minutes. **RISK:** If framing changes to include the face, this entry is void and must be re-derived from scratch.

That last sentence is the load-bearing part. Write it every time.

## B6. Standard Tensions and Typical Director Resolutions

| Tension | HMU position | Other department's position | Typical resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chair time vs shooting hours** | The build takes what it takes | The 1st AD needs first shot at a fixed time | Director rules. Usual outcome: simplify the build, stagger call times, add artists, or use second team to protect the makeup during lighting |
| **HMU vs DP on hard light** | Hard key exposes every edge and blend | The DP wants the hard source for the scene | Director keeps the light. HMU redesigns thinner and blends further, or the DP agrees a specific angle limit for the tightest shots |
| **HMU vs Colourist on skin** | The base is neutral and correct in camera | The grade wants a heavy warm or cool push | Agree the LUT in prep and design under it. Never design under a neutral monitor and hope |
| **HMU vs Wardrobe on palette** | The lip and hair colour serve the face | The costume palette is already approved | Costume usually wins on palette, HMU adjusts. Escalate only if the costume makes the actor read ill |
| **HMU vs VFX on prosthetics** | Practical delivers texture and performance | VFX would rather have a clean plate and build digitally | Depends on budget and shot count. Common compromise: practical build as the base, digital cleanup of edges. Cannom's late-career work on aging bridged exactly this line `[SOURCED]` |
| **HMU vs Director on realism** | Real skin has texture, and removing it makes the film look like an advert | The director wants the lead to look their best | Resolve by scoping: keep texture in wide and medium, allow a slightly cleaner build for designated hero close-ups |
| **HMU vs Client on a commercial** | Design to reduce retouching, keep skin real | The client wants an idealised, on-brand result | Resolve in prep with documented, measurable skin goals and a written practical/post split, plus defined retouch rounds and approvers `[SOURCED]` |
| **HMU vs Production on crew count** | One artist cannot maintain eight principals on set | The line producer sees a headcount | Present the chair-hour arithmetic. It is the only argument that works |

## B7. Failure Modes and Under-Specification Tells

Scan any HMU plan for these. Each is a defect, not a stylistic choice.

**Hard contradictions (must be fixed before lock):**

1. **"No makeup pass" on a character whose face appears in the shot list.** Either the shot list is wrong or the note is. This is the single most common contradiction in an under-specified plan.
2. **A blanket register rule that contradicts a named exception.** "Nobody looks done up" plus a character described elsewhere as camera-ready. Rewrite the rule with the exception scoped inside it (see A6).
3. **A named character with no entry at all.** Silence is not a verdict.
4. **A prosthetic build with no removal time in the schedule.**
5. **A hair change that is irreversible (cut, real colour) scheduled before scenes that need the prior state.** Check against the day-out-of-days, not the script order.
6. **Body makeup specified without a coverage boundary**, against a costume with a neckline that will move.

**Soft tells (signal the plan was written from a brief rather than from a cast and a frame):**

7. Registers described in adjectives ("natural," "clean," "edgy") with no product, finish, or time attached.
8. No chair-time numbers anywhere. A plan without minutes has not been costed and cannot be scheduled.
9. No mention of format, resolution, or lens. The plan was written blind to the frame.
10. No mention of key-light temperature or hardness in any scene. The plan was written blind to the light.
11. Identical specifications across visibly different cast members. Real skin tones do not share one base.
12. "Fix in post" appearing more than once without a named approver and a defined round count.
13. No camera test. On any project where a face is in close-up, this is a red flag on its own.
14. Continuity stills not mentioned. The plan has no mechanism for surviving day two.
15. Background performers referenced as a mass with no state, count, or time per person.
16. Sweat, water, blood, or weather in the script with no set-life or rebuild plan.

## B9. Trigger List: What Forces a Re-Open

The distinction that matters is between **re-derivation** (the underlying decision must be made again from inputs) and **relabelling** (the decision stands, the wording updates). Confusing the two is how plans silently rot.

| Upstream change | Must be genuinely RE-DERIVED | Merely RELABELLED |
|---|---|---|
| **Framing rule change that puts previously unseen faces on camera** | Full specification for every newly visible character: skin, features, hair, chair time, artist assignment, continuity stills, camera test inclusion. The old partial-frame entry is void, not editable | Nothing. This is a full re-open for the affected characters |
| **Resolution or format change (e.g. 2K to 8K, S35 to large format)** | Product weight, powder policy, blend and edge tolerance, prosthetic material choice, base-match precision, hand and skin detail work at macro | Register names, story-day map |
| **New character from a different world or faction** | Their entire register and its relationship to the dominant rule. Whether their presence breaks the existing contrast logic. Chair time and crew impact | Nothing about existing characters, unless the new character shares frames with them |
| **Casting change on any role** | Base tone and undertone, hair (own vs wig), facial hair, prosthetic fit (a lifecast is person-specific and does not transfer), allergy and sensitivity profile, chair time | The character's register and story states, if the role is unchanged |
| **Costume palette pivot** | Base warmth, blush and lip colour, hair colour where it sits against fabric, body-makeup coverage boundary against new necklines | Prosthetic design, skin condition work, story-day map |
| **Lighting plan change (temperature, hardness, or new practicals near the face)** | The compensation row for every affected scene. Finish and shine policy. Blend and edge scrutiny under a new hardness. Sweat plan if heat changed | Character registers |
| **LUT or colourist intention change** | Base neutrality vs correction, lip and blush saturation, how much warmth to carry in camera | Chair times, hair design |
| **Schedule compression or chair reduction** | The design itself. Simplify builds, restage transformations, or reassign. Do not simply promise the same look in less time | Nothing. Time is a design input, not a delivery variable |
| **Story-order or day-out-of-days change** | Continuity map. Irreversible-change ordering (cuts, real colour). Which state is built on which day | Per-character look design |
| **New macro or extreme close-up added** | The specification for whatever is in that frame, at a resolution that was not previously required. Hands, nails, lashes, hairline, lip texture | Everything not in that frame |
| **VFX scope change (de-aging, face replacement, digital cleanup added or removed)** | Whether the practical build is the final image or a base layer. Marker requirements. Edge tolerance | Register, story states |
| **Reshoot or pickup scheduled** | Nothing new in design, but the continuity stills and product log become the primary deliverable, and any cast physical change since the original block must be matched | The plan itself |

**Standing protocol for the agent occupying this role:** on receiving any change above, do not patch the affected line. Mark the character's specification `VOID, RE-DERIVING`, re-run the B2 checklist from the first step that the change touches, and re-issue with a change-log entry naming what was re-derived and what was carried forward. An HMU plan that has been patched more than it has been re-derived will contradict itself, and the contradiction will surface on the day, in front of the cast, with the clock running.
