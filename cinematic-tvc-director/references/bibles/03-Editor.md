# PART A: CRAFT REFERENCE

## A1. What the Editor Actually Is

The editor is the last writer. Every other department produces material. The editor is the only person on the crew whose entire job is deciding what the audience will and will not see, in what order, and for exactly how long.

Paul Hirsch, who shared the 1978 editing Oscar for the original *Star Wars* with Marcia Lucas and Richard Chew, argues in his memoir *A Long Time Ago in a Cutting Room Far, Far Away* that editing is a creative art, not a technical trade, and he makes the case that an editor can materially change the creative direction of a film. **[SOURCED]**

Thelma Schoonmaker, who has cut Martin Scorsese's films for decades, describes her role as shaping the movie and the performances: giving the film rhythm and pace, and sometimes adjusting structure so the thing flows at all. **[SOURCED]**

That is the honest description of the job. Not "assembling footage." Shaping performance, rhythm, structure, and meaning out of raw material that was shot under compromise.

## A2. What the Editor Owns, and Where the Authority Stops

### Owns outright
| Owned | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cut points | The exact frame on which one shot ends and the next begins |
| Shot order | Including reordering against the storyboard or script when the material demands it |
| Shot duration | How long each image is on screen |
| Rhythm and pace | The felt tempo of the piece |
| Performance selection | Which take, and which portion of which take |
| Structure proposals | Moving, merging, or cutting whole scenes and beats |
| Temp sound and temp music | The provisional audio bed that makes the offline readable |
| Runtime | Hitting the exact required duration, to the frame |

### Does not own
| Not owned | Owner |
|---|---|
| Whether a shot exists at all | Director and DP (coverage plan) |
| Final mix, final sound design | Sound department, after picture lock |
| Final grade | Colourist and DP |
| Final VFX | VFX supervisor |
| Approval of the cut | Director, then producer or client |
| The right to change the script's meaning without a conversation | Director |

The formal boundary is well defined in the feature world. Under the Directors Guild of America Basic Agreement, the process runs assembly, then editor's cut, then the director's cut, and the producer must give the director time to complete that cut: ten weeks, or one day of editing per two days of scheduled photography, whichever is longer. The director's right to prepare that cut is absolute and nobody may cut behind them during that period. Who holds final cut is a separate matter and must be designated in the director's deal memorandum. **[SOURCED]**

The practical translation for any crew, including a two-person commercial crew: **the editor proposes, the director disposes, and the person with final cut is named in writing before work starts.** If nobody has written down who holds final cut, that is an unresolved production problem, not an editorial one.

## A3. Walter Murch's Rule of Six, In Full

Walter Murch set this out in *In the Blink of an Eye*. It is the single most useful framework in the craft because it is ranked and weighted, which means it tells you what to sacrifice when you cannot have everything. **[SOURCED]**

| Rank | Criterion | Weight | What it actually means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Emotion** | 51% | Does the cut make the audience feel what they are supposed to feel at this moment? Not "is it smooth," but "is it true." |
| 2 | **Story** | 23% | Does the cut advance the narrative, or clarify meaning? Does the audience now know or suspect something they did not know one frame ago? |
| 3 | **Rhythm** | 10% | Is the cut at the right moment tempo-wise? Murch treats this as musical: the cut is a beat, and the sequence is a bar. |
| 4 | **Eye-trace** | 7% | Where is the viewer's eye on the outgoing frame, and where does the incoming frame want it? Manage the eye, or the cut feels like a jolt. |
| 5 | **Two-dimensional plane of screen** | 5% | Screen grammar. Screen direction, the 180 degree line, the flat geometry of where things sit in frame. |
| 6 | **Three-dimensional space of action** | 4% | Literal physical continuity. Is the hand in the same position, is the glass at the same level. |

Three things about this list matter more than the list itself:

1. **Emotion outweighs everything else combined, and that is deliberate.** Murch's weights are not a joke. Emotion at 51% means a cut that lands emotionally and breaks physical continuity is a *better cut* than one that is perfectly continuous and dead. **[SOURCED]**
2. **The top three account for 84%.** Emotion, story, and rhythm are the job. Items 4 to 6 are craft hygiene.
3. **Sacrifice from the bottom up.** When a cut cannot satisfy all six, you give away 3D continuity first, then screen geometry, then eye-trace. You never give away emotion.

Schoonmaker and Scorsese demonstrate this in practice: they deliberately keep certain "bumps" in their films because the roughness adds grit and reality, in contrast to the slicker mainstream house style. Schoonmaker has said that sometimes the goal is to slap the audience in the face. **[SOURCED]** That is Murch's hierarchy enacted: emotion bought at the cost of smoothness.

## A4. Where To Cut: The Blink Theory

Murch, cutting *The Conversation*, noticed that Gene Hackman blinked almost exactly where Murch had independently decided to cut. He built a theory from it: a blink is emotional punctuation, and it marks the end of one thought and the start of the next. He credits John Huston with the underlying observation, quoting Huston: look at that lamp across the room, now look back at me, look back at the lamp, now back at me. You blinked. Those are cuts. Your mind cut the scene. **[SOURCED]**

The operational value is this: **a cut is not a change of image, it is the end of a thought.** If you cannot say what thought is ending and what thought is beginning, you do not have a cut, you have a splice.

Corollary for performance-driven work: Anne V. Coates described herself as very much an actor's editor who cut for performance rather than for action. **[SOURCED]** The cut point is usually inside the performance, not at the tidy edge of the shot.

## A5. The Decision Set

### A5.1 Cut points
The cut point is a frame number, not a region. The professional habit is to be able to defend the frame: why this frame and not the one before it. Coates, asked how the famous *Lawrence of Arabia* match cut was perfected, said David Lean told her to make it perfect, and she did it by taking two frames off the outgoing shot. **[SOURCED]** Two frames. That is the resolution the job operates at.

### A5.2 Continuity cutting vs discontinuity cutting
- **Continuity cutting** hides the cut. Match on action, respect screen direction, respect the 180 degree line, keep eyelines consistent. The intent is that the audience never notices the join.
- **Discontinuity cutting** exposes the cut and uses it as an expressive device. Jump cuts, deliberate mismatches, temporal ellipsis, smash cuts.

Neither is superior. The rule is that discontinuity must be **systematic**. One jump cut in an otherwise continuous piece reads as a mistake. A pattern of jump cuts reads as a voice. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A5.3 Match cuts
A match cut joins two shots that share a graphic, motional, or conceptual rhyme so that the join itself carries meaning. The Coates match from a blown-out match flame to the desert sunrise is the canonical example, and notably it was scripted as a dissolve and became a cut because of technical constraints of the day. Lean and Coates both recognised immediately that the harder join was the better one. **[SOURCED]**

The lesson generalises: **a cut is almost always stronger than the dissolve that was scripted in its place.**

### A5.4 Montage construction
Soviet montage theory is still the working vocabulary. Lev Kuleshov demonstrated that a neutral face intercut with different subjects reads as different emotions, which establishes that meaning is manufactured by juxtaposition, not contained in the shot. Sergei Eisenstein categorised five montage types: metric (cut on a fixed frame count regardless of content), rhythmic (cut driven by the content and movement within frame), tonal (shots that share an emotional register and accumulate a theme), overtonal or associational (combining the previous three), and intellectual (colliding shots to produce an idea that is in neither shot). **[SOURCED]**

Practical montage rules:
- A montage needs an **argument**, not just a set of pretty images. If you cannot state the sentence the montage is making, it will feel like a screensaver. **[PRINCIPLE]**
- A montage needs **escalation**. Shot 6 must be doing more than shot 2, in scale, intensity, or specificity. **[PRINCIPLE]**
- Metric cutting (fixed frame counts) is the fastest way to make a montage feel mechanical, and also the fastest way to make it feel musical. Use it on purpose, never by default. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A5.5 J-cuts and L-cuts (split edits)
- **J-cut:** the audio of the incoming shot arrives before its picture. The audience hears the next scene before they see it. Effect: anticipation, momentum, pull-through.
- **L-cut:** the audio of the outgoing shot continues under the incoming picture. Effect: lingering, reflection, letting a line land on a listening face.

Both exist because straight cuts on picture and sound together are audibly abrupt, and because split edits let the viewer maintain visual continuity while the story moves. In practice, split edits are the default in dialogue and the default under voiceover, not a special technique. **[SOURCED, general post-production practice]**

Rule of thumb: **in a dialogue scene, if every audio cut is aligned with every picture cut, the scene has not been edited yet, it has been assembled.** **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A5.6 Transitions, and when a transition is a cop-out
A transition (dissolve, fade, wipe, whip, morph, light leak) is justified when it carries information a cut cannot carry: a change of time, a change of place, a change of consciousness, or a deliberate softening at a structural boundary.

A transition is a cop-out when it is used to:
- Hide the fact that two shots do not cut together. Fix the shots or fix the order.
- Pad runtime. A 20-frame dissolve is not content.
- Substitute for a missing beat. If the story needs a shot you do not have, a whip pan does not create it.
- Decorate. If the transition is the most interesting thing in the sequence, the sequence is weak.

Test: **remove the transition and replace it with a straight cut. If the piece is not measurably worse, the transition was decoration.** **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A6. Pacing As A Measurable Thing

Pace is not a vibe. It is measurable, and professionals measure it.

### A6.1 Average Shot Length (ASL)
ASL is total runtime divided by number of shots. Cinemetrics-style analysis of studio features shows a long-term acceleration: roughly 8 to 11 seconds ASL before 1960, moving toward roughly 4 to 6 seconds in recent decades, driven in part by music video and digital media grammar. **[SOURCED]**

Useful working bands **[PRINCIPLE, calibrated against the sourced ranges above]**:

| ASL | Reads as | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 8 sec and above | Contemplative, observational, confident | Prestige drama, luxury brand film, testimony |
| 4 to 8 sec | Classical, invisible, comfortable | Mainstream narrative, corporate film, most 60s |
| 2 to 4 sec | Energetic, propulsive | Most 30 second spots, sports, retail |
| Under 2 sec | Aggressive, kinetic, potentially exhausting | Trailer, montage sections, social hook stacks |

### A6.2 Cuts per line
For dialogue and voiceover work, ASL is too coarse. The finer metric is **cuts per line**: how many picture changes occur across one spoken line or one VO sentence.

- 0 to 1 cut per line: the line is being trusted. The performance carries it.
- 2 to 3 cuts per line: the line is being illustrated. Standard for VO-over-visuals advertising.
- 4 or more cuts per line: the line is being outrun by the picture. The audience will retain the images and lose the words.

This is the single most useful number to state in an editor's plan, because it is directly checkable against the VO's actual word count and read speed. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A6.3 Shots per second, and the shot frequency point
"Fast pace" means **shot frequency**, not shot count. This distinction is constantly botched by people writing briefs.

A 60 second film with 60 shots and a 90 second film with 60 shots do not have the same pace. The first has an ASL of 1 second and is frantic. The second has an ASL of 1.5 seconds and is merely quick. **Shot count is a budget number. Shot frequency is a craft number.** Adding runtime while holding shot count constant *slows the film down*. Removing runtime while holding shot count constant *speeds it up*, whether anyone intended that or not. **[PRINCIPLE]**

This is why any runtime change is an editorial re-derivation, not a trim. See Part B, trigger list.

### A6.4 Rhythm is not only picture
Joe Walker, editor of *Sicario*, *Arrival*, *Blade Runner 2049* and *Dune*, trained as a musician and has said his ambition is to bring musicality into editing. He also makes the point that rhythm is not purely auditory and not purely the editor's: it is a combination of many departments, including the rhythm of a shot's composition and the rhythm of the speed of the movement inside that shot. **[SOURCED]**

Practical consequence: a slow camera move inside a 2 second shot fights the cut. Pace lives in the material as well as in the joins.

## A7. Pacing and Tone Are Independent Axes

This is the distinction that separates editors from operators, and it is worth stating flatly.

**PACING is cut speed.** How frequently the image changes.
**TONE is register.** Whether the piece is warm, cold, comic, elegiac, ironic, sincere, aggressive, tender.

They are orthogonal. Every combination exists and is legitimate:

| | Slow pace | Fast pace |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm tone** | Family drama, heritage brand film | Upbeat lifestyle spot, wedding film |
| **Cold tone** | Psychological thriller, minimalist tech film | Action sequence, hard-sell retail |

The failure this prevents: a director says "it feels too cold, warm it up" and an inexperienced editor slows the cutting down. Slowing the cut does not add warmth. It adds *weight*. A cold piece cut slowly becomes a **colder** piece, because the audience now has more time to sit inside the coldness. **Reversing one axis does not reverse the other.** **[PRINCIPLE]**

Tone is changed by: shot selection (which performance, which expression, which angle), music, sound texture, the choice of what to hold on, and what you cut *away from* and *toward*. Pace is changed by cut frequency. Treat the notes separately, and when receiving a note, always ask which axis the note is really about.

Sally Menke's practice is instructive here. She made her first pass without music, saying she just made the scene work emotionally and dramatically, and only then would Tarantino lay the track over it and they would tweak to the beats. **[SOURCED]** That is a deliberate separation: get the tone right on the naked cut, then tune the pace against the music. If you build the cut on top of a temp track from frame one, you cannot tell which of the two axes is doing the work.

Menke's other signature was **contrast between the axes**: long-take, low-cut conversational scenes sitting directly against fast-cut action, so that each makes the other more extreme. **[SOURCED]** Pace contrast is a tonal instrument when it is structured.

## A8. Cutting To A VO or Script, Versus Cutting Picture-First

There are two fundamentally different assembly orders, and choosing the wrong one wastes days.

### A8.1 Picture-first
You build the sequence visually, on its own logic, then place audio against it. Appropriate when: the piece is performance-driven, dialogue-driven with sync sound, or when the visual event is the story.

### A8.2 Script-first (the radio cut)
In documentary and VO-driven work, the standard method is the **paper edit**, turned into a **radio cut**: an assembly driven strictly by sound, with no regard for visuals. The test is explicit: *does the story work if you close your eyes and only listen?* Once the radio cut holds, it gets fleshed out with B-roll, graphics, natural sound, and music. **[SOURCED, standard documentary post practice]**

### A8.3 Why full-runtime VO forces script-first
If the voiceover runs wall to wall across the entire runtime, the VO is no longer a layer, it is **the spine**. Its consequences are structural:

1. The VO's duration, once read, *is* the film's duration. You cannot shorten the film without cutting words.
2. Every picture decision must land inside a word window that already exists. The pictures serve the sentence they sit under.
3. Any picture reorder is also a script reorder, because the audience will bind whatever image is on screen to whatever word is being spoken. Kuleshov again: the juxtaposition creates the meaning, and the viewer will assume the image is illustrating the word.
4. Therefore the assembly order must be: lock the read, lay the read, mark the sentence boundaries, then cut picture into the resulting grid.

Attempting picture-first on a full-runtime VO piece produces a beautiful sequence that the VO does not fit, and the fix is always to re-cut the picture. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A8.4 Breathing room
A read is not just words, it is words plus air. The editor needs the VO delivered with the pauses intact and, ideally, with alternate reads at different tempos. A read with no air cannot be paced. A read with too much air can always be tightened. **Always request the slower read as well as the faster one.** **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A9. How A Held Shot Works

A held shot is a shot deliberately allowed to run past the point at which its information has been delivered.

**Why minimum durations exist.** Every shot has a floor: the time required for the audience to (a) recognise where they are, (b) find the subject in frame, and (c) read the intended information. Below that floor the shot is not perceived, it is only registered as motion. Working floors **[PRINCIPLE]**:

| Shot type | Practical minimum |
|---|---|
| Close-up of a known face or object | 8 to 12 frames |
| New close-up, new object | 16 to 20 frames |
| Medium shot with action | 20 to 30 frames |
| Wide establishing shot, new location | 1.5 to 3 seconds |
| Shot containing readable text or a product name | 2 seconds minimum, longer for long words |
| End card with a call to action | 1.5 to 2.5 seconds absolute minimum |

**What a held shot buys.** Once the information floor is passed, additional duration stops being informational and becomes emotional. Holding buys:
- **Dread and suspense.** Lee Smith, cutting *Dunkirk*, has described creating tension precisely through how long shots were held, tuning exposed-soldier moments endlessly to sustain a sense of dread, while deliberately providing intermittent relief because you have to engage the audience to the point where they can barely stand it and then let them breathe. **[SOURCED]**
- **Interiority.** Joe Walker describes a technique he has used across *Arrival*, *Blade Runner 2049* and *Dune*: cut from somebody looking very intently, then cut to something else, and it reads as though they are thinking about it. **[SOURCED]** The hold on the look is what makes the following shot read as thought rather than as geography.
- **Weight and consequence.** Schoonmaker has noted that Scorsese encourages her to hold shots longer than feels comfortable, building tension before the cut. **[SOURCED]**
- **Trust.** A held shot tells the audience the filmmaker is not nervous. Frequent cutting reads, subconsciously, as insecurity about the material.

**The cost.** A hold that carries no emotional charge is dead air, and the audience's attention leaves the frame. Hold on *faces, decisions, and consequences*. Do not hold on scenery unless the scenery is the subject.

## A10. Sound and Picture Sync As An Editing Concern

Sound is not a downstream department that receives a finished cut. It is a co-author of pace.

Thomas Grove Carter, editor at Trim Editing in London, is explicit about this: he does a lot of sound design in the offline because it goes hand in hand with the picture edit, and because sometimes the picture simply does not work without the sound. He also notes the economics of it, that he may work on a project for a month while the mixer gets a day, so the offline has to hand over as much creative direction as possible. **[SOURCED]**

### A10.1 Frame-exact sync points
A **sync point** is a named moment where a specific sound event must land on a specific picture frame. In a professional handover, every sync point is expressed as a timecode with a frame number, never as prose.

Bad: "the whoosh hits as we cut to the product."
Good: "SFX_WHOOSH transient peak lands at 00:00:14:07, one frame before the cut at 00:00:14:08."

The reason is mechanical: sound and picture are conformed in different applications, and the only thing that survives translation between them is a number. **[PRINCIPLE, grounded in standard turnover practice]**

### A10.2 Turnover, conform, change lists
After picture lock, the picture edit is handed to sound as an **AAF** (preferred, carries clip gain, pan and multi-channel metadata) or **OMF** (older, still used as a fallback), accompanied by a reference QuickTime at matching timecode and an **EDL** as a recovery document. If picture changes after lock, editorial issues a **change list** describing precisely what moved, so that sound can conform rather than rebuild. **[SOURCED, standard post interchange practice]**

After picture lock the sound team runs a **spotting session** with the director, walking the film and agreeing every point where music, effects, or design starts, evolves, and stops. The picture editor is the person in that room who knows every cut intimately and can flag the intent behind each one. **[SOURCED]**

### A10.3 Temp music and temp love
Temp music lets the editor shape the cut before a score exists and lets the director screen something that feels finished. Its known hazard is **temp love**: the director, client, or editor becomes attached to a track that was never licensable, and every subsequent score is judged against a ghost. **[SOURCED, standard scoring practice]**

Mitigations: temp with music that is stylistically close to what is actually achievable, state in writing that the temp is temp, and where possible cut a version without music to prove the picture stands alone (Menke's method). **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A13. How Commercial and Short-Form Editing Differs From Feature Editing

Everything above still applies. What changes is the constraint density.

### A13.1 The runtime is a hard specification, not a target
A 30 is 30 seconds, to the frame, because a broadcast slot is a purchased unit. Features negotiate runtime. Commercials do not. The consequence is that **structure and pace are locked to each other from the first frame of assembly**. If a beat runs long, another beat must lose exactly that duration. This is a zero-sum arithmetic problem, and it is why commercial editors think in frame budgets per beat before they think in cuts. **[PRINCIPLE]**

Working frame budget for a 30 second spot **[PRINCIPLE]**:

| Section | Typical budget |
|---|---|
| Hook / setup | 3 to 5 sec |
| Development / demonstration | 15 to 18 sec |
| Reveal beat | 3 to 5 sec |
| Resolution / payoff | 2 to 4 sec |
| End card | 2 to 3 sec |

### A13.2 The reveal beat
Short-form almost always has one moment where the film's actual subject lands: the product appears, the twist declares itself, the meaning of the preceding images resolves. This is the **reveal beat**, and it is the single most important edit decision in the piece.

Rules:
- The reveal must be **prepared**. Everything before it exists to make it land.
- The reveal usually wants **a held shot immediately before it**, not immediately after. You buy attention with a hold, then spend it.
- The reveal must not coincide with the busiest audio moment. Give it its own acoustic space.
- If the reveal is in the last 3 seconds, the piece has no payoff, it has an ending. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A13.3 The end card
The end card is not decoration and it is not the editor's afterthought. It has legal, brand, and legibility requirements, and it has a duration floor set by reading speed, not by taste. Two habitual errors: cutting to the end card on the music's final hit (which makes the brand feel like a full stop rather than a destination), and giving the end card less time than the longest word on it needs to be read. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A13.4 Platform cutdowns as a system, not an export
One campaign now routinely requires a hero cut, 15 second and 6 second cutdowns, square and vertical versions, captioned versions, and sometimes alternate calls to action per audience. The professional position is that this is **a system of assets, not one master with variations**, and each version must be built for the platform's native viewing behaviour rather than exported at a different length. **[SOURCED, current commercial post practice]**

Specific consequences:
- **Vertical is a re-cut, not a re-crop.** The framing changes what the eye can find, which changes minimum shot durations upward.
- **Sound-off viewing** means every essential story beat must survive muted. If the film only works with audio, the social cut does not work.
- **The 6 second cut has room for one idea.** Choose it deliberately: usually the reveal beat plus the end card, nothing else.
- **The hook is a separate craft problem.** In a broadcast 30 you have the viewer. In a feed you have between one and three seconds to earn them, and that changes which shot goes first regardless of narrative logic.

The industry practice at good editorial houses is to hand cutdowns and versioning to assistants as genuine creative work rather than mechanical labour, precisely because versioning is where the craft judgement about what the film is actually about gets tested. **[SOURCED, Trim Editing practice]**

### A13.5 Where the editor sits in the commercial chain
Commercial editors are typically pencilled while the director is still pitching, and start immediately after the shoot. Being on set is usually avoided on purpose so that the rushes can be viewed coldly, without the crew's emotional investment in how hard a particular shot was to get. **[SOURCED]** The editor's independence from the shoot is a feature, not a scheduling accident.

# PART B: AGENT OPERATING MANUAL

This half is written so that an AI subagent can occupy the editor's chair inside a multi-department crew. Read Part A as knowledge. Execute Part B as procedure.

## B1. Intake: The Exact Questions To Ask

On being handed a script, a VO, and a shot list, ask these in this order. Do not begin cutting or planning until each has an answer or an explicit "unknown, proceed on assumption X."

### Runtime and format
1. What is the exact required runtime, in seconds and frames? Is it a hard broadcast duration or a soft target?
2. What frame rate and what timecode start?
3. What deliverables and aspect ratios are required, and are cutdowns in scope? Which durations?
4. Is there a sound-off requirement (feed placement), and if so, are captions or on-screen text part of the picture edit or a later layer?

### Voice and script
5. Is there voiceover, and does it run wall to wall or intermittently?
6. Is the VO recorded, or is the script still text? If text, what is the estimated read duration at the intended tempo?
7. Was the read delivered with usable pauses, and do alternate tempo reads exist?
8. Which lines are legally or client-mandated fixed wording and cannot be trimmed?
9. Is there dialogue with sync sound, and is it scripted or improvised?

### Picture material
10. What is the full shot list, and which shots are confirmed captured versus intended?
11. What coverage exists per beat: how many angles, how many sizes, and is there a cutaway or safety for each risky join?
12. Which shots are single-take with no alternate, and therefore load-bearing?
13. Which shots are plates awaiting VFX, and what is the handoff frame range?
14. Are there any shots with on-screen text, product, or brand marks that carry a legibility duration floor?

### Intent
15. What is the target ASL or the intended pace band, stated as a number?
16. What is the tone, stated separately from the pace, in register words not tempo words?
17. Where is the reveal beat, and what is being revealed?
18. What is the one idea that must survive into the 6 second cut?
19. What is the reference, and is the reference being cited for pace, for tone, or for structure? (Insist that whoever gives a reference names which.)

### Sound
20. Is the music licensed, composed, or temp? If temp, is a real track known?
21. Are there named sync points, and are they frame-numbered?
22. Who is mixing, and what turnover format do they need?

### Authority
23. Who approves the cut, and who holds final say if they disagree?
24. How many review rounds are budgeted, and what constitutes lock?

## B2. Decision Checklist In Dependency Order

Each step depends on all steps above it. Do not skip forward. If an upstream item is unresolved, mark it and note what downstream work is now provisional.

1. **Confirm runtime and frame rate.** Everything else is arithmetic against this.
2. **Determine assembly order.** If VO runs full runtime, script-first. Otherwise picture-first. State the choice explicitly.
3. **If script-first: build the radio cut.** Prove the piece works with eyes closed. Get the read approved before any picture is cut.
4. **Mark the sentence grid.** Every VO sentence boundary gets a timecode. This is the frame budget skeleton.
5. **Allocate the frame budget per beat.** Section durations must sum to exactly the required runtime.
6. **Log the selects.** Best portion of every take, with in and out points.
7. **Identify load-bearing shots.** Single-coverage moments where a specific frame must be used because no alternative exists.
8. **Place the reveal beat.** Fix its timecode before cutting around it.
9. **Assemble.** All beats present, in order, no polish.
10. **Apply the pace target.** Measure the actual ASL and cuts-per-line. Compare to the stated target. Reconcile the difference deliberately, not by feel.
11. **Refine cut points.** Apply the Rule of Six per join. Where a join fails, sacrifice from the bottom of the list up.
12. **Add split edits.** Convert straight audio cuts to J and L cuts through dialogue and under VO.
13. **Set the holds.** Identify every intended emotional hold and defend its duration in frames.
14. **Verify duration floors.** Every text, product, and end card frame meets its legibility minimum.
15. **Temp sound and temp music.** Build enough that the cut reads. Flag every temp element as temp in writing.
16. **Frame-number every sync point.** Produce the sync list.
17. **Sound-off pass.** Mute the timeline and watch it. If the story breaks, fix the picture, not the mix.
18. **Runtime verification.** Exact to the frame, including any required slate, black, or end card hold.
19. **Review, note, revise.** Classify each incoming note as pace, tone, structure, or content before acting on it.
20. **Picture lock.** Issue turnover to sound, VFX, and grade with EDL, AAF, reference file at matching timecode, and a VFX shot list with frame ranges.
21. **Derive cutdowns.** Build each version from intent, not by trimming the master.
22. **Change list discipline.** Any post-lock picture change is documented as a change list and circulated to every downstream department.

## B3. What The Editor Needs From Other Departments Before Anything Can Lock

| From | Needs | Why nothing locks without it |
|---|---|---|
| **Director** | Named final-cut authority, tone brief in register words, pace target as a number, the reveal beat identified | Without a stated pace target and a named approver, every note is arbitrary and the cut cannot converge |
| **DP / Camera** | Full coverage plan, confirmed captured shot list, sizes and angles per beat, screen direction notes, any deliberate line crossings, frame rate and any overcranking, camera-move durations | Coverage determines which cuts are available at all. A beat with one angle cannot be paced, only trimmed |
| **Sound** | Design intent per section, named sync points with frame numbers, whether music is licensed or temp, the mixer's required turnover format, the stem structure | A sync point without a frame number is unlockable. The picture cannot be committed against an ambiguous audio target |
| **VO / Casting** | Final approved read, delivered with pauses intact, alternate tempo reads, isolated takes per line, pronunciation-locked mandatory wording | The read's duration and its breathing room define the film's structure when VO runs full runtime |
| **VFX** | Handoff frame ranges per shot, plate availability dates, whether a shot's duration can change after handoff, temp comps for offline | A shot that becomes a VFX handoff stops being trimmable. Its duration is now a contract |
| **Music / Composer** | Tempo, structure map, hit points, whether the cut leads the music or the music leads the cut | Determines whether pace is negotiable or fixed to a bar grid |
| **Producer** | Exact deliverable list, exact runtimes, review round count, lock date, legal wording requirements | Frame budgets cannot be allocated against an unknown deliverable list |
| **Art / Wardrobe** | Continuity notes and any known mismatches | Tells the editor in advance which joins will need a cutaway |

**Hard rule for the agent: if the VO is not final and it runs full runtime, nothing downstream of step 4 in B2 can lock. Say so explicitly rather than proceeding quietly.**

## B4. What The Editor Owes Other Departments

| To | Owes |
|---|---|
| **Director** | An honest first cut that represents the material rather than flattering it; options, not just a preference; a clear statement of what the material cannot do |
| **Sound** | Picture lock that is actually locked; AAF plus OMF fallback plus EDL; reference file at matching timecode; separated dialogue, music and effects tracks; a frame-numbered sync list; presence at the spotting session; a written statement of which elements are temp |
| **VFX** | Shot list with exact frame ranges and handles; early flagging of any shot whose duration might change; temp comps returned promptly; no silent trimming of a delivered VFX shot |
| **Colour** | Conformed EDL, reference file, and a note on any speed changes, reframes, or stabilisation applied in the offline |
| **Music** | A cut that states its own rhythm clearly enough to score against; hit points identified; honesty about temp attachment |
| **Producer** | Accurate runtime at every review; a realistic count of what is left; early warning when a note requires re-derivation rather than a tweak |
| **Camera / DP** | Feedback on coverage gaps while there is still time to shoot, not after wrap |

## B5. Standard Tensions and Typical Director Resolutions

### Editor vs Sound: the frame-sync tension
**The tension.** The editor cuts on a picture logic. Sound wants events to land on specific frames for impact, and a sound event landing one frame late reads as sloppy while one frame early reads as anticipation. When picture moves after lock, every designed sound event is thrown out of position, and the sound team has to conform rather than create.

**Typical resolution.** The director enforces real lock. Where a late picture change is unavoidable, editorial issues a formal change list and the director accepts that the affected section's sound work is now re-derived, not adjusted. Where a specific sound event is structurally load-bearing (a hit, a drop, a reveal sting), the director rules that **picture conforms to sound** at that point and the editor gives up the frame. Elsewhere sound conforms to picture. The rule is decided per event, in advance, and written down.

### Editor vs VO casting: the breathing-room tension
**The tension.** Casting selects a voice for its texture, authority, or warmth. The editor needs a read with usable air: pauses long enough to cut into, and consistent tempo. A voice that is perfect in timbre but reads flat-out with no gaps leaves the editor no cut points, and a voice that is beautifully paced but too slow eats the runtime and forces beats out of the film.

**Typical resolution.** The director rules on which is dominant for this piece: if the voice is the brand asset, the picture yields and beats get cut. If the story is the asset, the read is re-recorded to the editor's tempo notes. The standard preventive is that the editor supplies **timing direction to the record session** before it happens, including target read duration and required pause lengths, and that the session captures at least one slower and one faster full read. When neither is possible, the director chooses between recasting and cutting a beat, and states the choice.

### Editor vs Director: the darling tension
**The tension.** The director has an emotional relationship with shots that were expensive or difficult. The editor's value depends on not having that relationship. Thomas Grove Carter has said explicitly that he prefers to stay off set so he can look at rushes coldly, because if you were there you carry the baggage of how hard the shot was to get. **[SOURCED]**

**Typical resolution.** The editor cuts the version without the shot and screens both. The comparison, not the argument, settles it.

### Editor vs DP: the coverage tension
**The tension.** The DP shot for the frame. The editor needs the join. A beautiful oner with no cutaway cannot be shortened, and a beat with only one angle cannot be paced.

**Typical resolution.** Raised during prep, not post. The editor reviews the coverage plan and names, per beat, the minimum coverage required to make the intended pace achievable. Post-wrap, the director decides between accepting the pace the coverage permits and authorising a pickup.

### Editor vs VFX: the frozen-shot tension
**The tension.** Once a shot goes to VFX with a frame range, its duration is effectively fixed, because retiming it wastes the vendor's work. The editor loses the ability to trim exactly the shots that are often the most trimmable.

**Typical resolution.** The director enforces that VFX handoffs happen only after the surrounding section is locked, and that handoff frame ranges carry generous handles (commonly 8 to 12 frames each side) so that small adjustments remain possible without a re-render.

### Editor vs Producer: the round-count tension
**The tension.** Notes arrive that are not tweaks but re-derivations, while the schedule assumes tweaks.

**Typical resolution.** The editor classifies every note before acting: **tweak** (frame-level, within the current structure), **re-cut** (section rebuild, structure retained), or **re-derive** (pace, tone, or runtime pivot that invalidates downstream work). The producer prices and schedules against that classification, and the director signs off on which class a note belongs to when it is disputed.

## B6. Failure Modes and Under-Specification Tells

These are the signals that a brief, a treatment, or a crew document is not yet executable. When an editor agent encounters one, it must flag it rather than silently invent a value.

| Tell | What is missing | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| A shot appears in the list with no assigned duration | Frame budget | Assign a provisional duration, state it explicitly as an assumption, and flag that the total will not sum to runtime until it is confirmed |
| A transition is described but not timed ("dissolve into the next scene") | Duration in frames | Ask for the frame count. Default to a straight cut and note that a transition was requested but unspecified |
| A sync point is named but not frame-numbered ("the music hits on the reveal") | Timecode | Refuse to lock. Convert to a frame number and circulate for confirmation |
| A pace note uses tone words ("make it feel warmer, cut it tighter") | Axis separation | Split the note into its pace component and its tone component and confirm each separately |
| A reference is supplied with no dimension named | What is being referenced | Ask whether the reference is for pace, tone, structure, or grade |
| Runtime is stated as "about 30 seconds" | Hard specification | Establish whether this is a broadcast slot. If yes, it is exactly 30 seconds and everything is arithmetic |
| VO is described but not recorded, and runs full runtime | The film's spine | State that structure is provisional until the read exists. Estimate read duration from word count and flag the estimate |
| A beat has one shot and no coverage | Editorial options | Flag that this beat can be trimmed but not paced, and that any note on its rhythm cannot be actioned |
| Music is named but licensing is unconfirmed | Temp love risk | Mark as temp in writing at every review |
| "We'll fix it in the edit" appears anywhere upstream | A missing decision | Name the specific decision being deferred and who must make it |
| Cutdowns listed with no per-version intent | Version strategy | Ask, per version, what the single retained idea is |
| No named approver | Authority | Escalate. Do not begin fine cutting without it |
| The same shot appears twice in the intended structure | Dupe, possibly unintentional | Confirm whether the repetition is deliberate. Deliberate repetition is a structural device (see Dody Dorn's use of identical film to signal reverse chronology) and accidental repetition is an error |

**Additional failure modes internal to the edit:**
- **Cutting to hide, not to say.** Increasing cut frequency to disguise weak material. The pace goes up and the film gets worse.
- **Pace as a substitute for structure.** If the piece is boring, cutting it faster makes it a faster boring piece.
- **Transition as glue.** Reaching for a dissolve every time two shots do not join. Fix the shots or fix the order.
- **Building on temp from frame one.** You lose the ability to tell whether the cut works or the track works.
- **Ignoring the sound-off case** on anything destined for a feed.
- **Locking before VFX handles are agreed.**
- **Trimming the end card** to buy frames elsewhere. Always trim from the middle.

## B8. Trigger List: What Forces A Re-Open, And What Must Be Genuinely Re-Derived

A **re-open** means the editor's plan returns to an earlier step in B2 and the affected downstream work is invalidated, not adjusted. The agent must state which step it is returning to.

| Upstream change | Re-open to B2 step | What must be genuinely re-derived |
|---|---|---|
| **Runtime change** (any amount) | Step 5 | The entire frame budget. Every beat duration. ASL changes even if no shot is added or removed, because pace is frequency, not count. All cutdowns. All sync points after the change. The end card allocation must be protected first, not last |
| **Pacing pivot** (target ASL changes) | Step 10 | Every cut point in the affected sections. The hold list, because holds that read as tension at one ASL read as dead air at another. The split-edit map. Almost always the temp music, because the track's tempo was chosen against the old pace |
| **Tone pivot** (register changes) | Step 6 | Shot selection, not cut points. Which take, which expression, which angle. The music. What the film cuts away from and toward. **Do not change the ASL in response to a tone note unless the note also names a pace change** |
| **VO recast or re-record** | Step 3 | The radio cut. The sentence grid. Every picture placement bound to a word. Breathing room per line. If the new read differs in duration by more than about 5%, the frame budget re-derives and beats will be lost or gained |
| **VO script wording change** | Step 4 | The sentence grid from the changed line onward. Everything downstream shifts in time, which means every sync point after it is invalid |
| **Shot added** | Step 5 | The frame budget for its section. Every neighbouring shot's duration, because the added shot's frames are taken from somewhere. The ASL for that section |
| **Shot removed** | Step 7 | Whether the removed shot was load-bearing. If it carried a story beat, the beat must be rebuilt from remaining coverage or the story changes. If no coverage remains, escalate: this is a reshoot question, not an editing question |
| **A cut becomes a VFX handoff** | Step 20 partially, then step 12 of the plan | That shot's duration is now frozen. Re-derive the surrounding beat's frame budget against a fixed value. Confirm handles. Any subsequent trim in that section must come from adjacent shots only. Re-issue the VFX list |
| **Music track changes** | Step 10 | The rhythm map. Every cut placed against a beat of the old track. Hit points. Whether the piece now leads or follows the music |
| **Sync point moves** | Step 16 | The frame number, circulated to sound. Whether picture yields or sound yields at that point, decided by the director |
| **New deliverable or platform added** | Step 21 | That version's intent, built from scratch. Do not derive it by trimming an existing version |
| **Aspect ratio added (vertical or square)** | Step 21 | Reframing per shot, and upward revision of minimum shot durations, because a reframed shot takes longer to read |
| **Coverage discovered to be missing** | Step 7 | Which beats are now unpaceable. Escalate to director with the specific list of notes that can no longer be actioned |
| **Approver changes** | Step 1 | Confirm the tone and pace targets with the new approver before continuing. Previous approvals of structure are not automatically inherited |
| **Any picture change after lock** | Formal change list | Sound conform, VFX conform, grade conform. Every downstream department is notified with exact frame deltas. Nothing is changed silently |

**Standing rule for the agent:** when any trigger fires, the correct first output is not a new cut. It is a **restated Editor's Plan** showing the new frame budget, the re-derived ASL, and the explicit list of what has been invalidated. Cutting before re-deriving produces a piece that is internally inconsistent in exactly the way audiences feel but cannot name.
