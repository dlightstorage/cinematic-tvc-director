## A1. WHAT THE ROLE ACTUALLY IS

Every other department on a production owns a material. Camera owns the image. Art owns the space. Costume owns the body. Sound owns the air. Edit owns time. The director owns none of these and is nonetheless responsible for all of them, because the director owns the only thing that is not a material: **the relationship between the departments' decisions**.

The Directors Guild of America says this in almost exactly those terms. Under Article 7 of the DGA Basic Agreement, "The Director contributes to all creative elements relating to the making of a motion picture and participates in molding and integrating them into one dramatic and aesthetic whole." **[SOURCED: DGA Basic Agreement of 2020, Article 7, paragraph 7-101, summarised at dga.org]** The operative words are *integrating* and *whole*. The guild does not define the director by what the director makes. It defines the director by what the director reconciles.

The advertising industry's definition is structurally identical. Cannes Lions judges Direction (category A01 under Film Craft) on "the vision and achievement of the direction, including the translation of the creative brief through a director's vision and how well that vision has been achieved," and states explicitly that this "may include but is not limited to the way that casting, set design, sound design and cinematography have been used to bring that vision to life." **[SOURCED: Cannes Lions, Film Craft Lions category definitions, canneslions.com]** Note what the world's most-watched craft award is actually scoring: not the director's shots, but the director's *use of other departments*.

Kim Gehrig, the Somesuch director appointed 2023 Film Craft Jury President at Cannes Lions, put the same idea in the first person on her appointment: "As a director, I am only as good as my collaborators. Every aspect of film craft, from writing and casting to shooting, editing, and post-production, is the result of the combined efforts of a talented team of people. It's the alchemy of these people and their crafts that elevates a piece of film beyond what we could even dream of." **[SOURCED: Kim Gehrig, Cannes Lions announcement, 23 February 2023]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** So the honest job description is: the director is the person who decides what the film is *about* at the level of feeling, converts that into a small number of binding rules, distributes those rules to departments who will each interpret them slightly differently, and then resolves the collisions that interpretation produces. Most of the actual work is the last part.

## A2. WHERE THE AUTHORITY BEGINS AND ENDS

### In a feature (DGA-style authority)

The DGA codifies a very wide band of director authority. From assignment until the end of post, the director must be informed of any proposal and has the right to participate in all decisions concerning cast selection, other creative personnel, script, locations, set designs and construction, shooting schedule and post-production scheduling. No creative decision about preparation, production or post-production should be made without consulting the director. **[SOURCED: DGA Basic Agreement of 2020, 7-202 and 7-1501]**

Further hard rights: only one director may be assigned to a picture at a time (7-208); the director has the right to select the First Assistant Director (7-204); the producer must consult on Unit Production Manager and Second Unit Director selection (7-203, 7-205); the producer may not increase the difficulty of a stunt or add one without the director's consent (7-303); the director must see dailies at a reasonable time each day (7-401); looping and narration must be directed by the director (7-1101); and the director's right to prepare a Director's Cut is described by the guild as **absolute**, with no one permitted to cut behind the director during that period (7-504, 7-508). For theatrical features the minimum cutting time is ten weeks, or one day of editing for every two days of scheduled photography, whichever is longer (7-505(b)). **[SOURCED: DGA, all as cited]**

Where it stops, even at the top of the industry, is instructive. **Final cutting authority is not automatically the director's.** The DGA requires only that the person holding final cut be *named in the deal memorandum* (7-206). The guild guarantees the director the right to make a cut, not the right to have that cut released. The distinction between "I get to make my version" and "my version is the version" is the single most important boundary in the entire craft, and directors who confuse the two are the ones who get replaced.

That the DGA needs a standing Creative Rights Committee at all is the tell. Christopher Nolan sits as a co-chair of it alongside Jonathan Mostow. **[SOURCED: DGA Creative Rights Handbook 2023-2026 foreword, dga.org]** Directorial authority is not a natural property of the job. It is a negotiated position that erodes when unexercised, which is exactly what that handbook says to its own members.

### In a commercial (the triangle)

Commercial directing runs on a different constitution, and mistaking one for the other is the fastest way to lose a job.

| Party | Owns | Cannot be overruled on |
|---|---|---|
| **Client / brand** | The brand, the message, the claim, legal and regulatory compliance, the money | Whether the product is represented correctly and lawfully |
| **Agency** (Creative Director, copywriter, art director, agency producer) | The idea, the approved board or script, the strategy it serves | Whether the executed film is still the idea they sold the client |
| **Production company** (Executive Producer, line producer) | Feasibility, budget, crew, schedule, risk | Whether the plan can physically and legally be shot for the money |
| **Director** | Interpretation of the board, tone, casting recommendation, visual language, performance, pacing, and the resolution of every cross-department conflict | Nothing, unilaterally |

**[PRINCIPLE]** The commercial director's authority is *delegated and revocable*. It is granted by the agency and client on the strength of a treatment, exercised inside the boundaries that treatment established, and returned at delivery. Final cut in advertising almost always sits with the client, and practically with the agency. A commercial director who behaves as though DGA feature rights apply will be replaced before the pre-production meeting.

What the commercial director does hold, and holds absolutely, is **coherence authority**. Nobody else in the triangle is positioned to know that the wardrobe decision just invalidated the lighting plan. The agency sees the idea. The client sees the brand. The producer sees the money. Only the director is looking at the intersection of all department plans at once. That is the seat's real leverage, and it is technical rather than political.

### The four hard edges, in any format

1. **It stops at legality and safety.** Stunt, minor, animal, pyro, drone, location and regulatory limits are not creative questions. The 1st AD and the producer hold the veto.
2. **It stops at the money.** A director proposes; only the budget can approve. Under DGA rules a producer may not reduce budget or schedule "in bad faith or capriciously" (7-201), which is a rule about process, not a guarantee of resources.
3. **It stops at the brief.** In a commercial the board is the contract. A director who "improves" the idea into a different idea has not been bold, has breached the agreement.
4. **It stops at the craft heads' expertise.** The director says what the scene needs to feel like and what the frame must contain. The DP decides how to light it. The designer decides how to build it. A director who specifies the method rather than the requirement gets a compliant crew and a dead film.

## A3. THE DECISION SET

Every decision that belongs to the director resolves into one of these. If a project has not answered all of them, no department can safely start.

| # | Decision | What it actually fixes | Who is blocked until it locks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Interpretation of the brief or script** | What this story is about beneath its plot. The one sentence. | Everyone |
| 2 | **Tone** | The film's attitude toward its own subject. Warm, cold, wry, reverent, anxious, celebratory. | Casting, wardrobe, art, music, grade, performance |
| 3 | **Point of view** | Whose experience the camera is inside, and whether that changes | Camera, edit, sound |
| 4 | **Visual language** | Frame ratio, lens family, camera-height rule, movement grammar, colour logic, contrast logic | DP, art, VFX, grade |
| 5 | **World rules** | Period, place, degree of realism, what is allowed to exist in frame | Art, costume, hair and makeup, locations, VFX |
| 6 | **Casting approval** | The faces. In practice this is 60 percent of tone. | Casting, wardrobe, hair and makeup, scheduling |
| 7 | **Performance direction** | What each performer is actually doing, moment by moment | Cast, camera (blocking), sound |
| 8 | **Blocking and coverage** | Where bodies and camera go, and what is shot | Camera, grip, art, 1st AD, edit |
| 9 | **Pacing** | Rate of information and duration of held moments | Edit, sound, music |
| 10 | **Sound and music intent** | Whether the film is scored, sourced, silent, or diegetic-led | Sound design, composer, edit |
| 11 | **Polish level** | How finished, controlled and expensive the execution reads | Art, DP, grade, VFX, wardrobe |
| 12 | **The final call on every cross-department conflict** | Which of two mutually incompatible plans survives, and what replaces the other | Everyone downstream of the conflict |

**[PRINCIPLE]** Items 1 through 6 are *upstream*. They should be locked before any department produces a plan, because every department plan is a derivation from them. Items 7 through 11 are *in-flight* and can be adjusted. Item 12 is not a decision type at all, it is a standing duty that recurs for the life of the project.

## A4. THE TWO AXES DIRECTORS MOST OFTEN COLLAPSE

This is the single most valuable section of this document, because both failures are invisible until they have already wasted a shoot day or a grade session.

### Axis 1: PACING is not TONE

**Pacing** is a rate. It is how quickly information arrives, how long a moment is held, how many cuts per unit of time, how much dead air is permitted between beats. It lives in the edit, in blocking, in line delivery speed and in shot duration.

**Tone** is an attitude. It is the film's stance toward its own material: affectionate or contemptuous, grave or playful, warm or cold, sincere or ironic. It lives in casting, performance, music, light quality, colour, framing distance and the script.

These are **orthogonal**. You can occupy any quadrant.

| | Warm tone | Cold tone |
|---|---|---|
| **Slow pacing** | Chloé Zhao's *Nomadland*: long, unhurried, and deeply affectionate toward its subjects | Much of Michael Haneke's work: equally unhurried, deliberately withholding of sympathy |
| **Fast pacing** | A comedy montage: rapid cutting, entirely benign | A thriller chase: rapid cutting, hostile and airless |

The proof that these are independent is that you can move along one axis without touching the other. Bong Joon-ho's *Parasite* changes tone drastically across its running time, moving from comic mischief to something genuinely brutal, without a corresponding uniform change in cutting rate. The attitude shifted. The rate did not have to.

**[PRINCIPLE] The operational rule: reversing a pacing decision does not reverse a tone decision.**

The failure looks like this. A client or producer watches a cut and says "it feels distant." That is a tone note, phrased as a feeling. An anxious edit responds by tightening every shot by six frames and losing two beats of silence. The film is now *faster and still distant*. Worse, the compression has removed the very holds that would have let a warm performance land, so the tone has moved further in the wrong direction while appearing to be addressed.

The inverse failure is just as common. The note is "it drags." That is a genuine pacing note. The director responds by warming the grade, adding score and recasting the voiceover to something friendlier. The film is now warmer and still drags, and the added score has increased the perceived running time because music makes structure legible.

**What actually moves each axis:**

| To change PACING | To change TONE |
|---|---|
| Shot duration, cut count, scene order | Casting, above everything else |
| Where a scene starts and ends (late in, early out) | Performance register (how much the actors are giving) |
| Line delivery speed and overlap | Music: presence, instrumentation, whether it comments or observes |
| Amount of held silence or empty frame | Light quality: hard versus soft, source direction, contrast |
| Camera movement speed | Colour: palette and grade warmth |
| Density of information per shot | Framing distance: closeness equals sympathy, distance equals judgement |

**Diagnostic question to ask when a note arrives:** *"If I shortened this by 15 percent, would the note be resolved?"* If yes, it is a pacing note. If the note would survive the trim, it is a tone note, and the trim is off-target.

### Axis 2: POLISH LEVEL is not TONE

**Polish level** is a measure of *finish*. How controlled, clean, expensive, resolved and immaculate the execution reads. It is set by art department dressing, lighting control, camera stability, grade cleanliness, VFX finish, wardrobe newness and hair and makeup precision.

**Tone**, again, is attitude.

Also orthogonal, and the quadrants are populated by famous work.

| | Warm / celebratory tone | Cold / austere tone |
|---|---|---|
| **High polish** | Wes Anderson: immaculate, symmetrical, controlled, and affectionate | Denis Villeneuve's *Dune*: as controlled and expensive as film gets, and its attitude is grave, arid and unconsoling |
| **Low polish** | Handheld, available-light documentary-adjacent work about a community: rough and loving | The Safdie brothers' register: rough and hostile |

**[PRINCIPLE] The operational rule: making a location more polished does not make the film's tone glossier.**

This is the specific confusion that burns art department budget. A brand says "it needs to feel more premium." Somebody hears "glossy." The art department repaints the location, replaces the practical furniture with designer pieces, and dresses out every honest imperfection. Polish has gone up substantially. Tone has not moved at all, because tone was never being carried by the walls. It was being carried by the casting and the performance register, which are unchanged. The result is a film that now looks more expensive and feels exactly as it did, at significant cost, and often with a new problem: the location has lost the texture that gave the light its interest, so the DP's plan no longer works.

The reverse error is equally expensive. A director wants a film to feel "honest" and instructs the art department to de-dress a set, shoot in an untreated location and use available light. That reduces polish. It does not automatically produce honesty. Without a change in casting or performance, the result reads not as authentic but as under-resourced, which is a different and much worse signal to send on behalf of a brand.

Kim Gehrig's *This Girl Can* for Sport England is the cleanest counterexample to the whole confusion. The imagery is deliberately unglamorous: real bodies, sweat, wobble, no aspirational sheen. Its craft level, however, is extremely high, and it took a Grand Prix for Good and was credited with inspiring over 2.8 million women to take up a new weekly sport. **[SOURCED: Cannes Lions, on Gehrig's Film Craft jury presidency appointment]** Low gloss, very high polish, warm tone. Three separate dials.

**Diagnostic question:** *"If this were shot with the same cast and the same performances on a better-dressed set with more lighting control, would the note be resolved?"* If yes, it is a polish note and belongs to art, camera and grade. If no, it is a tone note and belongs to casting, performance, music and framing.

### Why these two collapses happen

**[PRINCIPLE]** Because the vocabulary is shared and imprecise. Clients, agencies and crew all use feeling-words for measurable things. "Slow," "flat," "cold," "cheap," "premium" and "glossy" are each used to describe at least two of these three axes. A director who accepts the word instead of translating it into an axis will over-correct on the wrong dial, and the correction will itself become the next problem. The discipline is: **never act on an adjective until it has been assigned to an axis.**

## A5. REAL CONFLICT VERSUS FALSE ALARM

A cross-department flag is not evidence of a conflict. It is evidence that two departments *looked at each other*, which is what you asked them to do. Directors who treat every flag as a problem to solve create more damage than directors who miss the occasional genuine one, because a fix applied to a non-problem is a change with no compensating benefit, and every change has downstream cost.

### The three classifications

**REAL CONFLICT.** Two departments' plans are *mutually incompatible*. Both cannot be executed as written. Executing one forces the other to change. Example shape: the camera plan requires a 200 degree working arc around a table, and the set plan has a fixed wall on the fourth side because the design requires the room to feel enclosed. There is no version of the day where both survive untouched. Somebody's plan has to change and only the director can decide which.

**FALSE ALARM (already aligned).** Two departments each flagged a risk about the other, but their plans, read side by side, already agree. Example shape: wardrobe flags that its palette may clash with the set; the set palette, independently chosen, is in fact its complement. Both departments were being responsible. Neither needs to move. The correct director action is **not "no action."** It is to confirm the alignment in writing, name why, and lock it so that nobody re-opens it later.

**NEW RISK CREATED BY A FIX.** The most dangerous category and the one most often missed. A real conflict was resolved, the resolution changed a premise, and that changed premise has silently invalidated a decision that was correct before. Example shape: a real conflict between camera and art is resolved by moving from a built set to a practical location. The tone decision, casting and wardrobe are untouched and still fine. But the sound plan was written for a controlled stage, and the location is under a flight path. Nobody flagged it, because sound was never party to the original conflict. The fix created the risk.

**[PRINCIPLE]** A director who only ever classifies into "conflict / no conflict" will systematically miss the third category, because the third category never arrives as a flag. It has to be *hunted*, by re-reading every department plan after each resolution and asking which of them depended on the premise that just changed.

### Why over-correcting a false alarm is as damaging as missing a real one

Four reasons, all of them practical.

1. **Every change has a blast radius.** Moving the wardrobe palette to "solve" a clash that did not exist means the hair and makeup plan, the grade reference and possibly the casting look are all now derived from a premise that is no longer true.
2. **It destroys the signal value of flags.** If departments learn that raising a flag produces an unnecessary change to their own plan, they stop raising flags. The director loses the sensing apparatus that makes coordination possible at all.
3. **It consumes the change budget.** In a commercial there is a finite number of changes the schedule and the client's patience will absorb. Spending them on non-problems means the real conflict later in the week gets resolved badly because there is no room left.
4. **It manufactures the impression of chaos.** A crew that watches settled decisions reopen concludes the film is not designed. Confidence is a production resource and it is expended by unnecessary correction.

### "Checked, no change needed, here is why" is a real outcome

**[PRINCIPLE]** This is a genuine audit result and it must be written down in the same place, in the same format, with the same numbering as the real resolutions. A decision record containing only changes is indistinguishable from a decision record produced by somebody who only looked at the things they changed. The difference between "we examined this tension and confirmed both plans stand, for this reason" and silence is the entire difference between a coordinated production and a lucky one. Silence reads as an omission. A recorded no-change reads as coverage.

## A6. BLIND, PARALLEL DEPARTMENT INPUT

There are two ways to brief a set of departments, and they produce genuinely different information.

**Sequential briefing.** The director briefs camera. Camera produces a plan. The director briefs art *with the camera plan attached*. Art produces a plan shaped around camera's. Then costume, with both attached. And so on.

**Blind parallel briefing.** Every department is briefed off the same locked material at the same time, with no visibility into any other department's answer, and returns a plan independently.

Sequential briefing feels efficient and is much more comfortable, and it is a trap.

**[PRINCIPLE] Sequential briefing produces pre-compromised, synthetic consensus.** By the time the fourth department is briefed, it is not answering the director's question. It is solving a puzzle with three sides already fixed. Its answer will be *compatible*, which is not the same as *correct*, and the compatibility was purchased before anyone tested whether the earlier plans were the right ones. Worse, the resulting apparent agreement across departments carries no information. Of course they agree. They were each shown the agreement and asked to join it. The director then reviews four aligned plans and concludes the design is sound, when in fact only the first plan was ever independently derived and every subsequent one inherited its errors.

**Blind parallel briefing surfaces genuine friction.** When five departments each derive from the same locked premises without seeing each other, the places where their answers collide are real. They mark points where the premises themselves are ambiguous, under-specified or internally contradictory. That is exactly the information the director needs and cannot get any other way. A collision under blind briefing is a diagnostic about the *brief*, not about the departments.

And the places where blind plans **independently agree** carry real information too. Three departments arriving separately at the same colour logic is strong evidence the tone decision was clear and correctly stated. Under sequential briefing that agreement would have been meaningless.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The practical implication for the director: the quality of blind parallel input is capped by the quality of the locked material. If the tone statement is vague, blind parallel briefing will produce five incompatible plans and the director will wrongly conclude the departments are the problem. The lock has to be genuinely decided before it goes out. That is what makes section B2's dependency order non-negotiable.

## A7. PREVIS, SHOT LISTS AND THE TREATMENT AS THINKING TOOLS

All three of these are usually described as communication documents. They are more useful understood as *forcing functions*: instruments that make the director discover what has not been decided.

### The treatment

In commercials the director's treatment is the pitch. Directors are typically bid against two or more competitors on the same board, and the treatment is what wins or loses the job. Its conventional contents are well established: director's vision and references, narrative or scene flow, casting direction, location and production design, lighting and camera and movement approach, wardrobe and props, post approach covering edit, sound and colour, and deliverables including formats, durations and variants. **[SOURCED: standard commercial treatment structure as documented across industry guides, including Dima Svichkar's account of pitching through Genero and the widely used slide-by-slide commercial treatment template convention]**

The more interesting claim, from the same practitioner's account, is that the treatment is *not* a designed presentation of a script. It is a worked-out project. A script contains dialogue, voiceover and scene order. A treatment describes the shoot in enough detail that the client, producer and crew can each understand how every scene will be filmed and what is required to shoot it, and only after treatment approval can a producer calculate an accurate budget. **[SOURCED: Dima Svichkar, "From Vision to Final Render," 2023]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** That is the tell. A treatment is a *specification wearing a sales document's clothes*. Every commitment in it is a commitment departments will be held to. Which means the honest use of a treatment is to write it in order to find out what you have not decided. Any section a director finds difficult to write is a section where the interpretation is not yet real.

### Storyboards and shot lists

Denis Villeneuve brings his crew in early and storyboards every frame, and describes storyboarding as the last rewrite of the film before production, because turning words in a screenplay into a visual frame is where the film actually gets decided. **[SOURCED: Villeneuve, as documented in interviews collected by Indie Film Hustle and No Film School]** The reframe is important: not "a plan for the crew," but *a rewrite*. The board is where you discover that a scene as written has no coverage, or that two consecutive scenes have the same shape, or that the emotional turn has no shot to live in.

Bong Joon-ho draws every shot before production and the *Parasite* boards match the finished film closely enough to function as a near-complete blueprint, which is what allows complex blocking and camera moves to be planned rather than improvised. **[SOURCED: widely documented account of Bong's storyboarding practice on *Parasite*, Academy Award for Best Picture]**

**[PRINCIPLE]** A shot list that only lists shots is a schedule. A shot list that also states, per shot, *what it is for*, is a thinking tool, and it exposes redundancy: any two shots that serve the same purpose means one of them is free to cut, which is time returned to the day.

### Previsualisation

Previs earns its cost specifically where a decision cannot be reversed on the day: large VFX interaction, complex rigs, stunts, big builds, anything where camera position determines what has to be constructed. **[PRINCIPLE]** Its real function for the director is not to show people what the film looks like. It is to *find the collision between the camera plan and the build plan before the build is paid for*. Previs used as a presentation tool is decoration. Previs used as an interference check is engineering.

### The common property

All three tools do the same job: they convert a vague intention into a specific claim that can be checked against another department's specific claim. That is the only way cross-department conflict becomes findable in advance rather than at 6am on the shoot day.

## A8. WORKING WITH PERFORMANCE

### Adjustments, not adjectives

The core distinction in performance direction, articulated most influentially by Judith Weston in *Directing Actors* (1996), is between **result direction** and **playable direction**. Result direction names the outcome the director wants to see: "be sadder," "more menacing," "warmer." It is an adjective, it describes the finished effect, and it is unplayable, because no human being can perform "sadder" directly. Weston's alternative is direction the actor can actually *do*: an action, a verb, an intention toward the other person, or an **adjustment**, which is a change to the character's inner circumstances that will produce the effect as a by-product. **[SOURCED: Judith Weston, *Directing Actors*, 1996]**

Villeneuve, working with Amy Adams, is documented asking for "a bit more insecurity." **[SOURCED: behind-the-scenes footage discussed by No Film School, April 2026]** That is not the adjective it superficially resembles. It names an inner state the actor can hold while playing the scene, and the visible result is left to emerge. The distinction between "look more insecure" and "be a bit more insecure" is the whole craft.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The practical test: can the actor *do* the note without thinking about how it looks? "Play it like you have already decided to leave" is doable. "Play it colder" is not, it is a request for a performance of coldness, which is what bad acting is.

### Directing a professional actor versus directing a real person

These are different disciplines and a director who uses one method on the other will get nothing usable.

| | Professional actor | Real person / non-professional |
|---|---|---|
| **What they can do** | Repeat a calibrated performance across many takes, hit marks, adjust register on request, sustain continuity | Be themselves, precisely and unrepeatably, inside a situation that is true for them |
| **Primary directorial tool** | Adjustment, action, intention, backstory, rehearsal | Situation design. You do not adjust the performance, you change the circumstances until the behaviour you need is the natural one |
| **Rehearsal** | Usually productive. Finds the shape before the day | Usually destructive. It teaches them to perform, and the second version is worse than the first |
| **What kills it** | Vague result direction, no clear intention, contradictory notes | Asking them to act; giving them lines they would not say; too many takes |
| **Coverage strategy** | Shoot for performance, protect continuity | Shoot for accident. Run longer, cover wider, record more than you plan to use |
| **Scheduling** | Standard | Front-load. The first two takes carry the truth |

Chloé Zhao's method on *Nomadland* is the reference case for the second column. She found real nomads at gatherings, lived among the community before production, and integrated their actual stories into the screenplay rather than asking them to inhabit invented ones. Frances McDormand, a trained actor, adapted her own technique to sit alongside them. **[SOURCED: Los Angeles Times, "The magic of Chloé Zhao," 24 April 2021]** Note the direction of adaptation. The professional bent toward the non-professionals, not the reverse. That is the correct hierarchy in a mixed cast, because the non-professional cannot travel and the professional can.

Alfonso Cuarón went further on *Roma* and controlled performance by controlling *information*. "Nobody had the script, and I shot in absolute continuity," he has said, describing how he talked to each actor about their character individually and released new information only day to day, which forced the production into chronological order. **[SOURCED: Alfonso Cuarón, interview with Scott Myers, Go Into The Story, 22 December 2018]** This is the purest example of the principle that performance direction is not always instruction. Sometimes it is the architecture of what the performer is allowed to know.

**[PRINCIPLE]** In commercials, the most common performance failure is directing a real person as if they were an actor, on a day scheduled as if they were an actor. Real-person testimonial content needs three times the recording time and one third of the direction.

| **Greta Gerwig** | Publicly frames directing as a fundamentally collaborative act rather than a solo authorial one, and is documented as letting casting reshape adaptation rather than locking the script first | **[PRINCIPLE, with sourced framing: Gerwig's own statements on the collaborative nature of directing, circulated by Girls in Film, 2024]** |
| **David Fincher** | Extensive previsualisation and very high take counts, treating performance as something arrived at by iteration rather than captured; the effect is to move discovery out of the schedule's critical path | **[PRINCIPLE, widely reported working method]** |
| **Steve McQueen** | A fine-art background applied to duration: holding a shot past the point of comfort so that the audience's relationship to the image, not the cut, carries the meaning | **[PRINCIPLE]** |
| **Lynne Ramsay** | Image-and-sound-first construction, building sequences from sensory logic rather than dialogue logic, which places the sound department upstream rather than downstream | **[PRINCIPLE]** |
| **Kim Gehrig (Somesuch)** | Commercial work at the top of the craft ladder built explicitly on collaborator alchemy; *This Girl Can*, *Viva La Vulva* (13 Lions including Glass: The Lion for Change), *Man on the Moon*, *Dream Crazier* | **[SOURCED: Cannes Lions, February 2023]** |

**[PRINCIPLE]** The pattern across all of them: none of these methods is a style. Each is a *scheduling and information-flow decision* that happens to produce a style. Villeneuve's early department-head involvement is a dependency-order choice. Cuarón's withheld script is an information-architecture choice. Bong's complete boards are a decision to move discovery from production into pre-production. Style is downstream of process.

## A11. HOW COMMERCIAL DIRECTING DIFFERS FROM FEATURE DIRECTING

### The pitch process

A feature director is usually attached before the film exists. A commercial director is selected *against competitors* on a board that already exists and has already been sold to a client. The director's treatment is the entire basis of selection, is written unpaid or on a small pitch fee, and is frequently written in 48 to 72 hours. The industry's own framing of this is candid: not every treatment becomes a film, and the quality of the treatment determines both whether it does and how good the result is.

**[PRINCIPLE]** The structural consequence: a commercial director does most of their design work *before knowing if they have the job*. That inverts the feature economics entirely and it is why commercial directors develop a reusable personal system for interrogating a board fast. The system is the asset, not any individual treatment.

### The boards

The agency's approved storyboard is a legal and commercial artefact. It has been presented to the client, costed, and in many markets approved by legal and regulatory review. A director may reinterpret how each frame is achieved and may propose changes, but changing *what happens* requires re-approval through the whole chain. **[PRINCIPLE]** The correct posture is: honour the board's structure and beats absolutely, and treat everything about *how* those beats are realised as the director's territory.

### The pre-production meeting

The PPM is the single most important event in a commercial's schedule and has no clean feature equivalent. Every department presents options, the agency and client select, and everything selected is locked. Casting is approved. Wardrobe is approved with alternates. Locations are approved. Props, hero product handling, hair and makeup direction, and the shooting schedule are all signed off in the room.

**[PRINCIPLE]** After the PPM, changes cost money and goodwill. Before it, they cost nothing. Therefore the director's entire pre-production job is to surface every cross-department conflict *before* the PPM, so that the PPM presents resolved options rather than open questions. A PPM that discovers a conflict is a failed PPM, regardless of how well it is chaired.

### Shoot day economics

A feature has weeks and can absorb a lost morning. A commercial typically has one or two days for the entire film, scheduled in quarter-hour increments, with a client watching, talent on a buyout with hard hours, a location with a hard out, and cutdown obligations that require alternate coverage of every hero beat.

Consequences that follow directly:

1. **There is no discovery time.** Anything not decided in pre-production will be decided badly, in front of a client, under time pressure.
2. **Coverage is not optional.** The deliverables list, not the director's preference, sets the minimum coverage. A 30, a 15, a 6 and a vertical cut all have to exist, which means certain beats must be shot in more than one framing whether the director wants them or not.
3. **The pack shot always gets squeezed.** It is scheduled last, it is the one thing the client absolutely requires, and it is the most common casualty of an overrunning day. Experienced directors schedule it early or shoot it on a separate table-top day.
4. **Every setup is a negotiation.** The agency will have a note on most of them. A director who fights each one runs out of day. A director who accepts each one loses the film. The skill is knowing which three setups actually carry the idea and spending all political capital there.

### Authority, compared directly

| | Feature (DGA-style) | Commercial |
|---|---|---|
| **Selection** | Attached, often before script lock | Pitched against competitors on an approved board |
| **Consultation right** | Contractual across cast, script, locations, design, schedule **[SOURCED: DGA 7-202]** | Customary, not contractual |
| **Casting** | Director participates in all casting decisions; only invited persons may attend sessions **[SOURCED: DGA 7-1504]** | Director recommends; agency and client approve |
| **Director's cut** | Absolute right to prepare one; no cutting behind the director during that period **[SOURCED: DGA 7-504, 7-508]** | A director's cut is customary and usually respected as a first cut, but carries no protection |
| **Final cut** | Held by whoever is named in the deal memo **[SOURCED: DGA 7-206]** | Client, in practice via the agency |
| **Post presence** | Right to be present and consulted throughout post; must be notified of every post operation **[SOURCED: DGA 7-506]** | Varies by relationship and budget |
| **Time in the edit** | Minimum ten weeks or one day per two shoot days on theatrical features **[SOURCED: DGA 7-505(b)]** | Often days |

**[PRINCIPLE]** The practical translation for anyone moving between the two: in a feature, authority is defended contractually and exercised over time. In a commercial, authority is earned in the treatment, exercised in pre-production, and largely spent by the time the camera turns over. This is why the commercial director's coordination discipline has to be so much tighter. There is no post-production in which to fix a coordination failure.

## B1. THE QUESTIONS THIS ROLE ASKS ON RECEIVING A BRIEF

Ask all of these before producing any output. Do not proceed on assumptions. Where the answer is unavailable, state the assumption explicitly and flag it as an assumption in every downstream document.

**About the mandate**
1. What is the approved board or script, and is it approved, or still moving?
2. What am I permitted to change, and what is contractually fixed?
3. Who holds final approval, and who holds veto separately from approval?
4. What has already been promised to the client that I have not seen?

**About meaning**
5. What is this actually about, in one sentence, beneath the plot or the product claim?
6. What does the audience have to feel, in one word, and at what precise moment?
7. What is the single beat that, if it fails, makes the whole film fail?

**About the three axes**
8. **Tone:** what is the film's attitude toward its own subject? Name it in adjectives, then immediately name what it is *not*.
9. **Pacing:** what is the rate? Where must the film hold, and where must it move?
10. **Polish level:** how finished should this read? State it independently of tone, and confirm the two answers are not the same answer.

**About the physical premises**
11. Delivery specs: ratio, durations, all cutdowns, platform variants, safe areas, frame rate.
12. Shoot days, locations, and whether any are build versus practical.
13. Cast structure: professional, non-professional, mixed, real customer, celebrity.
14. Any element that is technically irreversible once shot (VFX plates, stunt, minor, animal, product-in-camera).

**About constraints that will become conflicts**
15. What is the budget's binding constraint: build, cast days, VFX, or location?
16. What legal, regulatory or brand-guideline rules constrain image, claim or representation?
17. What is the client's known sensitivity: the thing that always comes back in the review round?

## B2. DECISION CHECKLIST IN DEPENDENCY ORDER

Nothing at a given tier may be locked until every tier above it is locked. This order exists because each tier is a *premise* for the tier below, and a change at any tier forces re-derivation of everything under it (see B5).

**TIER 0: MANDATE**
- [ ] Board or script version locked, with version number and date
- [ ] Approval chain named: who approves, who vetoes, who is merely informed
- [ ] Delivery spec list locked (all ratios, all durations, all variants)

**TIER 1: MEANING**
- [ ] The one-sentence interpretation
- [ ] The single load-bearing beat identified
- [ ] Point of view: whose experience, and whether it shifts

**TIER 2: THE THREE AXES, LOCKED SEPARATELY**
- [ ] Tone, stated as attitude, with an explicit "and not this"
- [ ] Pacing, stated as a rate with named hold points
- [ ] Polish level, stated on its own scale, explicitly not inferred from tone

**TIER 3: WORLD AND PEOPLE**
- [ ] World rules: period, place, degree of realism, what may exist in frame
- [ ] Casting brief issued, and casting approved
- [ ] Performance approach per cast type (actor versus real person)

**TIER 4: VISUAL LANGUAGE**
- [ ] Ratio and camera-height rule
- [ ] Lens family and its logic
- [ ] Movement grammar: what moves, when, and why
- [ ] Colour and contrast logic
- [ ] Sound and music intent

**TIER 5: DEPARTMENT PLANS (blind, parallel)**
- [ ] All departments briefed off identical locked material, simultaneously, without sight of each other
- [ ] All plans returned before any is reviewed

**TIER 6: COORDINATION**
- [ ] Conflict resolution protocol run (B4)
- [ ] Decision record issued (B7)
- [ ] Re-derivation completed for every changed premise (B5)

**TIER 7: EXECUTION**
- [ ] Shot list with per-shot purpose
- [ ] Coverage confirmed sufficient for every deliverable variant, not just the master
- [ ] Schedule tested against the decision record

**[RULE]** If a change is made at Tier 2 after Tier 5 has been briefed, the department plans are void. Re-brief. Do not patch.

## B3. WHAT THE DIRECTOR NEEDS FROM EACH DEPARTMENT, AND OWES THEM

| Department | The director NEEDS | The director OWES |
|---|---|---|
| **Cinematography** | Lens and lighting plan tied to named scenes; a statement of what the plan physically requires from the space; a list of shots that cannot be achieved as scheduled | Ratio, camera-height rule, movement grammar, the load-bearing beat, the polish level as a separate number from the tone |
| **Production design / locations** | Plans, elevations and dressing intent; what is built versus found; what the space physically permits in camera arc and rig | Tone, world rules, what the camera must be able to do in the space, which walls must be wild |
| **Costume / wardrobe** | Palette, silhouette and continuity plan per character per scene; number of doubles | Casting, tone, world rules, the grade intent, whether the film is high or low polish |
| **Hair and makeup** | Continuity plan across the shooting order; time required per performer | Casting, world rules, tone, how close the camera gets and on which beats |
| **Casting** | Options that genuinely differ from each other, not three versions of one idea | The one-sentence interpretation, the tone, and an explicit statement of whether these are actors or real people |
| **Sound design and music** | Intent-level response: what the film should sound like and where music enters and exits | Pacing with named hold points, tone, whether the film is scored or sourced, and any location acoustic reality |
| **Editorial** | An assembly that honours the stated pacing before any note is applied | Pacing rate, hold points, the load-bearing beat, all deliverable durations up front, not after the master is cut |
| **Colour** | A reference response tied to the stated tone and polish, not to a look book | Tone and polish stated separately; the DP's contrast logic; what the grade must not undo |
| **VFX** | What is achievable, what is plate-dependent, and what constrains camera on the day | Every camera and design decision that touches an effect, before the plate is shot |
| **Producer / 1st AD** | The true cost and time of each option, and the binding constraint | Priority order of the day: which three setups carry the film and what may be sacrificed |

**[PRINCIPLE]** The pattern in the right-hand column: every department is owed the *same* three axes, stated identically, plus the specific premises their craft derives from. Inconsistency between briefs is the primary manufacturer of false alarms.

## B4. THE CONFLICT RESOLUTION PROTOCOL

Run this in full. Do not shortcut steps 1 or 6.

### Step 1: Gather all department plans blind and in parallel

Brief every department off **identical locked material**. Same tone statement, same pacing statement, same polish statement, same world rules, same board version, same delivery specs, word for word.

No department sees another's answer. No department is briefed after another. Collect all plans before opening any of them.

If a department asks what another department is doing, the answer is: derive your plan from the locked material, and flag any dependency you are assuming. Do not resolve it for them. That flag is the data.

### Step 2: Read every flagged tension

Assemble the complete list of tensions. Include:
- Explicit flags a department raised about another
- Assumed dependencies a department declared
- Collisions you can see between two plans that neither department flagged, because neither could see the other

Number every tension. Do not group them yet, and do not begin resolving.

### Step 3: Classify each tension into exactly one of three categories

| Class | Test | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **REAL CONFLICT** | Both plans cannot be executed as written. Executing one forces the other to change | Resolve (Step 4) |
| **FALSE ALARM (already aligned)** | Read side by side, the two plans already agree, or the flagged risk does not materialise given what the other department actually planned | Confirm in writing (Step 6). Change nothing |
| **NEW RISK CREATED BY A FIX** | A resolution elsewhere changed a premise that this plan depended on | Resolve (Step 4), then re-run Step 3 for the new resolution |

**Classification rules:**
- A tension is REAL only if you can state, in one sentence, the physical or logical incompatibility. If you cannot, it is not real yet, it is under-specified. Send it back for specificity rather than inventing a fix.
- A tension is FALSE ALARM only after you have read both plans in full. A flag raised by a department is not evidence about the other department's plan, only about their expectation of it.
- Every resolution you make generates a mandatory NEW RISK sweep. There are no exceptions.

### Step 4: Resolve real conflicts and new risks only

For each, decide by this hierarchy, top down:
1. Safety, legality and regulatory compliance
2. The load-bearing beat: whichever plan protects it wins
3. The locked axes: whichever plan is more faithful to the stated tone, pacing and polish wins
4. Reversibility: prefer the option that can be changed later over the option that cannot
5. Cost and schedule

State each resolution as: **what changes, what does not, and which department must re-derive** (see B5).

**Do not average.** If camera wants a 200 degree arc and art wants an enclosed room, the answer is not 140 degrees in a partly enclosed room. The answer is one of: a wild wall, a redesigned camera plan, or a different scene solution. Averaging produces a plan that satisfies the stated constraint of neither department and the underlying need of neither.

### Step 5: Write each resolution as a numbered, cross-referenced decision

Not a parenthetical inside a longer note. Not an aside in an email. A numbered entry in the decision record, in the format at B7, that names:
- The tension number it resolves
- Every department affected
- Every prior decision it supersedes, by number
- Every number that must now be re-derived

**[RULE]** A resolution that lives in only one place has not been made. If the wardrobe change is recorded in the wardrobe note but not in the grade brief and not in the casting record, it will be reverted by whoever reads only their own document.

### Step 6: Confirm in writing why the false alarms needed no change

For every tension classified as FALSE ALARM, write an entry with the same number format as the resolutions, stating:
- What the two departments each flagged
- What their plans actually say, side by side
- Why the two are compatible
- That no change is being made, and what would have to change for this to be re-opened

**[RULE]** This is not optional documentation. "Checked, no change needed, here is why" is a real audit outcome and looks completely different from having forgotten to check. A decision record containing only changes cannot be distinguished from a record produced by somebody who only examined the things they changed. The no-change entries are the evidence of coverage.

### Step 7: Re-run

After all resolutions, re-read every department plan against the current set of premises. Any plan whose premise changed goes back through Step 3. Continue until a full pass produces zero new risks.

## B5. THE RE-DERIVE VERSUS RELABEL RULE

**The rule:** when a change touches a physical or technical premise, every dependent number and technique must be **re-derived from first principles**. It must not be renamed, adjusted proportionally, or carried over with a new label.

**Physical and technical premises include, and are not limited to:**
- Sensor size, sensor format, capture resolution
- Aspect ratio and any change to it
- Lens format or lens family
- Location versus build, and any change of location
- Room dimensions, ceiling height, available camera arc
- Framing rule or camera-height rule
- Frame rate
- Distance from camera to subject
- Power availability, rigging points, load limits
- Delivery spec of any kind

**Why relabelling fails.** A focal length is not a look. It is a number that produces a specific field of view *on a specific sensor at a specific distance*. Change the sensor and the same number produces a different image. Change the room and the same number cannot be placed. A director who moves from one capture format to another and instructs the DP to "use the same lenses" has not preserved the look, they have changed it while believing they preserved it, and the error will not be visible until the grade.

The same failure occurs with space. Moving from a built set to a practical location of "similar size" invalidates: the camera plan (arc and rig points), the lighting plan (no removable walls, no ceiling access, different practical sources), the sound plan (different acoustic and different noise floor), the blocking (different distances), the schedule (different turnaround), and possibly the wardrobe (different ambient colour bouncing onto faces). Six departments' numbers are now wrong. None of them will announce this.

**The procedure when a premise changes:**

1. Name the premise that changed, precisely, with its old and new value.
2. List every department whose plan referenced that premise, directly or indirectly.
3. For each, list every specific number or technique that was derived from it.
4. Re-derive each from the new value. Do not adjust. Do not scale. Derive.
5. Where a re-derived value now conflicts with another department, that is a NEW RISK. Send it back through B4 Step 3.
6. Record the re-derivation in the decision record with its own number, cross-referencing the resolution that caused it.

**[RULE]** The forbidden sentence is any variant of "it is basically the same, just call it X now."

## B6. FAILURE MODES

| Failure | What it looks like | Why it happens | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Averaging away a real disagreement** | Two incompatible plans are met in the middle, satisfying neither department's actual requirement | Conflict avoidance dressed as diplomacy. The middle position feels fair and is unbuildable | Force the decision hierarchy in B4 Step 4. One plan wins. The other is replaced with a specific alternative, not a diluted version of itself |
| **Manufacturing a fix for two departments that already agree** | A change is issued to resolve a flagged tension whose underlying plans were compatible | Treating a flag as evidence of a conflict rather than as evidence of attention | Mandatory side-by-side read of both plans in full before any classification. FALSE ALARM is a legitimate and common outcome |
| **Letting a resolution live in only one place** | The decision is in the camera note but not the art note, not the grade brief, not the schedule | Recording the decision where it was discussed rather than everywhere it applies | Every resolution names every affected department and appears in every affected document. Cross-referenced by number |
| **Treating a locked rule as still true after something added later intersected with it** | A framing rule locked in week one is still cited in week four, after a location change made it unachievable | Locks are treated as permanent rather than as conditional on their premises | Every lock records its premises. When a premise changes, every lock that named it is re-opened automatically |
| **Collapsing pacing into tone** | A "feels cold" note is answered with a faster cut | Adjective accepted without axis assignment | Never act on an adjective. Assign it to pacing, tone or polish first, using the diagnostic questions in A4 |
| **Collapsing polish into tone** | A "needs to feel more premium" note is answered by redressing a location, with no change to casting or performance | Same cause. Polish is easy to buy and tone is not | Same countermeasure. Then ask which department actually controls the axis in question |
| **Sequential briefing** | Departments are briefed in order, each seeing the previous plans, and all plans agree | It is faster and more comfortable, and the resulting consensus looks like validation | Blind parallel briefing, always. Agreement is only informative if it was arrived at independently |
| **Relabelling instead of re-deriving** | A premise changes and dependent values are carried across with new names | The numbers look transferable and the error is invisible until much later | B5, mechanically, every time |
| **Silent no-change** | A tension is checked, found harmless, and nothing is written | Nothing changed, so nothing feels worth recording | Write the no-change entry. Coverage that is not recorded did not occur |
| **Result direction** | "Be sadder," "more menacing," "warmer" | Naming the desired effect instead of the playable cause | Convert every note into an action, an intention or an adjustment before it leaves your mouth |
| **Directing a real person as an actor** | Repeated takes and register notes given to a non-professional, with the performance degrading each time | Applying the wrong column of A8 | Change the situation, not the performance. Front-load. Record more than you plan to use |
| **Discovering a conflict at the PPM** | The pre-production meeting becomes a problem-solving session | Coordination pass not run, or run after briefing rather than after plans returned | The full B4 protocol completes before the PPM. The PPM presents resolved options only |

## B8. TRIGGER LIST: WHAT FORCES A FULL COORDINATION PASS

Any one of these voids the current alignment and requires re-running B4 in full. Not a partial check. A full pass.

**Premise changes**
1. Any change to ratio, capture format, sensor, frame rate or resolution
2. Any change of location, including a "similar" one
3. Any change from build to practical, or practical to build
4. Any change to a framing rule, camera-height rule or lens family
5. Any change to the shooting order or the number of shoot days

**Mandate changes**
6. A new board or script version, however minor the stated change
7. A new or changed deliverable, ratio, duration or platform variant
8. A change in who holds approval or veto
9. A brand guideline, legal or regulatory constraint arriving late

**People changes**
10. Any casting change, including a swap between two approved options
11. A change in cast structure (an actor replacing a real person, or the reverse)
12. Loss or replacement of any department head

**Axis changes**
13. Any change to the tone statement
14. Any change to the pacing statement
15. Any change to the polish level

**Resolution-driven**
16. Every time a REAL CONFLICT is resolved (mandatory NEW RISK sweep)
17. Every time a re-derivation under B5 produces a value that another department referenced
18. Any time a department reports that its plan is no longer achievable

**Time-driven**
19. Immediately before the pre-production meeting, unconditionally
20. Immediately before the first day of photography, unconditionally
21. Before the first cut is shown to any approver outside the production

**[RULE]** When in doubt about whether a change is large enough to trigger a pass, run the pass. A full pass on a settled project is cheap and produces a set of no-change confirmations, which is itself a valuable artefact. A missed pass on an unsettled project is discovered on the shoot day, when nothing is cheap.

## CLOSING NOTE FOR THE AGENT

The director seat is not the seat that produces the most output. It is the seat that produces the *fewest, most load-bearing* decisions and then defends their consistency against every subsequent change.

Three habits carry almost all of the value:

1. **Never act on an adjective until it has been assigned to an axis.** Pacing, tone and polish are independent. Notes arrive as feelings and must be translated before they are actioned.
2. **Brief blind, brief in parallel, brief off identical material.** Consensus that was engineered by the briefing order tells you nothing. Friction that emerges from independent derivation tells you everything.
3. **Record the no-changes.** The difference between a coordinated production and a lucky one is not the number of problems fixed. It is the number of things demonstrably checked.
