# FILM CRAFT BIBLE 10: SOUND DESIGN, MIX, AND MUSIC

**Roles covered:** Production Sound Mixer, Sound Designer, Sound Editor, Foley Artist, Re-Recording Mixer, Composer, Music Supervisor, Music Editor
**Scope:** Features, short films, commercials, branded content, social cutdowns, VO-led short-form
**Status:** Reusable craft reference. Contains no client-specific or project-specific material.

**Sourcing convention used throughout this document:**
- **[SOURCED]** = attributable to a named practitioner, a published interview, a guild or standards body, or a measurable industry figure.
- **[PRINCIPLE]** = general craft consensus, teachable and defensible, but not tied to one named source.

# PART A: CRAFT REFERENCE

## A1. What The Audio Department Actually Is

Sound is the only department that can change what an image means without changing the image. A shot of a person standing still in a corridor is a different shot depending on whether we hear an empty room, a distant argument, a heartbeat, or nothing at all. Picture tells the audience what is there. Sound tells them what it feels like to be there.

Walter Murch coined the term "sound designer" while working on *Apocalypse Now*, and the credit was invented precisely because the existing credits (sound editor, mixer, recordist) described technicians executing a plan rather than an author creating one. **[SOURCED]** Murch's most useful working distinction is that dialogue has to be decoded by the audience while music simply is, that the two are processed by different parts of the brain, and that sound effects are the bridge between them. **[SOURCED]** That single idea explains most mix decisions: dialogue costs the listener cognitive effort, music costs them none, and effects can be pushed toward either pole depending on how literal you make them.

Randy Thom, longtime design director at Skywalker Sound, wrote the essay that most schools still teach on this, *Designing a Movie for Sound*. His argument is that sound has to be considered from the script stage, that using sound merely to enhance visuals is not collaboration, and that a film only comes alive when each craft influences every other craft. His sharpest line is that the biggest myth about sound design and composing is that they are about creating great sounds, and that this is not true, or at least not true enough. **[SOURCED]**

The practical translation: **the audio department's job is not to make good sounds. It is to decide what the audience should be paying attention to at every moment, and then to make that unavoidable.** **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A2. The Roles, What Each Owns, And Where The Authority Ends

### A2.1 Production Sound Mixer
The senior sound role on set. Owns the capture of everything recorded during photography: microphone selection and placement strategy, gain staging, wireless channel management, timecode sync, isolated (ISO) tracks per performer, and the sound report that tells post what happened. Works with the boom operator, whose principal responsibility is microphone placement, getting the mic as close to the action as possible without entering frame. **[SOURCED]** The production mixer also captures **room tone**, a passage of the location's own silence, which post uses to fill gaps and to make edits invisible. **[SOURCED]**

**Owns:** what gets recorded, the quality of the capture, calling a take compromised by noise.
**Does not own:** whether a shot happens at all, whether a noisy location is used, whether a line is re-performed later. Those belong to the director and the first AD. The production mixer's real authority is the right to say "that take is unusable" out loud, on the day, before the set is struck.

### A2.2 Sound Designer
The author of the non-literal sound. Creates sounds that do not exist to be recorded, or transforms recorded sounds into something that carries meaning beyond its source.

Ben Burtt's method on *Star Wars* is the canonical demonstration. The lightsaber came from the warm hum of an idling film projector, which he knew as a projectionist, layered with the buzz picked up by a microphone passed near a television picture tube, and then given movement by waving a mic near the speaker to produce a Doppler shift. R2-D2's voice was Burtt's own voice combined with an ARP synthesizer plus performed mechanical elements including whistling through a plumbing tube. **[SOURCED]** The lesson is not "use weird sources." The lesson is that a designed sound is **built from a stack of real recordings chosen for their emotional character, not their literal accuracy.** **[PRINCIPLE]**

Gary Rydstrom, who worked under Burtt at Skywalker Sound and holds seven Academy Awards for work including *Jurassic Park*, *Terminator 2*, and *Saving Private Ryan*, extends the same principle into naturalism: the most terrifying dinosaur is assembled from animals the audience has heard before, so the ear believes it. **[SOURCED]**

Nicolas Becker's work on *Sound of Metal* shows the third mode, sound as subjective point of view. Becker and director Darius Marder built a consistent vocabulary linking sound to picture from inside the character's head, and Becker captured the deaf and cochlear-implant perspective by rigging extremely sensitive microphones directly to bodies rather than recording in open air and filtering afterward, on the reasoning that underwater and in-body hearing arrives through bone and tissue. He has also said that sequences were removed because they made the language inconsistent, since consistency of the sonic language mattered more than any individual effect. **[SOURCED]** That is the most transferable idea in this whole bible: **a sound system that is consistent beats a sound moment that is brilliant.**

**Owns:** the sonic palette, the designed signature sounds, the world's rules.
**Does not own:** picture length, cut points, or the decision that a designed sound exists at all if the director has not agreed to it.

### A2.3 Sound Editor and Supervising Sound Editor
The organisational and craft spine. Splits the soundtrack into its working layers: dialogue and ADR, hard effects, backgrounds and ambiences, foley, and music. Cuts each element to picture, cleans production dialogue, and prepares the material so the mixer has options rather than problems.

Skip Lievsay, the Coen brothers' longtime sound man, describes *No Country for Old Men* as "a very good collaboration in minimalism," almost "sound design haiku," a minimum number of sounds made to tell the story. **[SOURCED]** Restraint here is a supervising decision, not a mixing decision. You cannot mix your way out of a track that has forty elements when it needed four.

### A2.4 Foley Artist
Performs, in sync to picture, the human-scale sounds a body makes in a space: footsteps, cloth movement, prop handling. Gary Hecker performs feet in pace with each actor's performance, sometimes over a scatter of coffee grounds to add grit, and stresses that weight distribution is the whole game, because the character's weight is what the audience actually reads. **[SOURCED]** Heikki Kossi, the Finnish foley artist behind *Ad Astra* and *Sound of Metal*, works the same way with props chosen for character rather than literal match. **[SOURCED]**

Foley is not sound effects. Effects establish the world. **Foley establishes that a body is in it.** Without foley, actors look like they are floating over the location. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A2.5 Re-Recording Mixer
Works at the end of the pipeline, after picture lock, once every specialist has delivered. Receives the material as **stems**, premixed groups organised by department: dialogue (including ADR), music (score plus licensed tracks), effects (hard effects, backgrounds, design), and usually foley. The classic delivery set is the D-M-E stems: dialogue, music, effects. **[SOURCED]**

The workflow is normally premix (or predub) then final. In the premix, each department is balanced internally for consistency and given position and level, with the explicit goal of handing the final mixer as many creative options as possible. In the final, the mixer sets the relationship *between* departments, which is where the storytelling happens. **[SOURCED]**

**Owns:** the moment-to-moment hierarchy of attention, the dynamic range, the loudness compliance, the deliverable formats.
**Does not own:** whether an element exists. If a scene needs a sound that was never cut, the mixer cannot invent it at the last minute without cost.

The Cinema Audio Society recognises production sound mixer and re-recording mixer as distinct award categories across live action, animated, documentary, and television, which is a useful reminder that capture and balance are different professions with different skills. **[SOURCED]** The Motion Picture Sound Editors' Golden Reel Awards split the editorial side further still, into dialogue/ADR, effects/foley, and music editing, across feature, broadcast, and games. **[SOURCED]**

### A2.6 Composer
Writes original music. The composer's authority is over the musical material: theme, instrumentation, harmony, tempo, and the emotional argument the music is making. The composer does not own where the music starts and stops (that is a spotting decision agreed with the director and editor) and does not own the final level of the music in the mix.

### A2.7 Music Supervisor
Sources, clears, and licenses existing music. Owns the rights position: sync licence (the composition, from the publisher) and master licence (the specific recording, from the label or owner), plus term, territory, media, and exclusivity. **[SOURCED]** A music supervisor who delivers a track without a cleared rights position has delivered nothing.

### A2.8 Music Editor
Cuts music to picture, builds the temp track, conforms score to picture changes, and manages the tempo map. In advertising this role is frequently absorbed by the editor, which is exactly why advertising music is so often badly conformed.

## A3. The Decision Set

### A3.1 Diegetic vs non-diegetic
- **Diegetic:** exists in the world of the film. Characters could hear it. A radio, a door, a phone, a room.
- **Non-diegetic:** exists only for the audience. Score, VO from outside the scene, a designed emotional swell.

The interesting work lives on the boundary. Music that begins non-diegetic and is revealed to be coming from a car stereo, or a designed effect that starts as a real sound and morphs into score, gives you a free change of register. Ren Klyce and David Fincher used a strict version of this rule on *The Killer*: when the audience is inside the character's point of view there is no voiceover, but there is very loud source music from his earbuds. **[SOURCED]** The rule created the style.

### A3.2 The sound palette
A palette is a written, finite list of the sonic materials this piece is allowed to use, and the materials it is forbidden to use. It should name texture families (metallic, organic, breath-based, synthetic, room-based), a tonal centre if there is one, and the exclusions. "No synth risers. No stock whooshes. No sub-drops on cuts." An exclusion list is worth more than an inspiration list, because it is falsifiable. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A3.3 Room tone and ambience beds
**Room tone** is the specific silence of one location. **Ambience** (or a bed) is a constructed environment layered underneath a scene. The difference matters: room tone makes edits invisible, ambience makes places exist. A cut with no room tone under it produces a hole, and the audience hears the hole even when they cannot name it. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A3.4 The use of silence
Silence is the most expensive item in the mix, because it only works if you have spent the preceding thirty seconds establishing what the audience is going to lose. *No Country for Old Men* is the reference case: in the most gripping sequences what audiences mostly hear is a suffocating quiet, and by compelling the audience to listen harder the film made its own sound design visible. **[SOURCED]**

Operationally: **silence is not the absence of a decision, it is a cue like any other, and it has a start frame and an end frame.** **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A3.5 Density and the Law of Two and a Half
Murch's observation is that something changes between two and three simultaneous similar layers. Beyond about two and a half comparable elements, the ear stops resolving them individually and starts hearing texture. He also notes that when several people are walking on screen you do not need to sync every footstep, because trying to correlate every sound with every image loses the big picture. **[SOURCED]**

The working rule: **at any instant, decide which one element the audience is listening to, which one is supporting, and let everything else be texture.** If three things are all competing for foreground, none of them is in foreground. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A3.6 Dynamic range
Dynamic range is the distance between your quietest and loudest moment. In a cinema you have a lot of it. On a phone speaker in a noisy room you have almost none. A mix with a wide dynamic range played on a phone means the quiet parts vanish and the loud parts distort. Every mix decision has to be made against a stated playback target, not against the mix room. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A3.7 The mix hierarchy
For narrative work the default priority is dialogue, then effects, then music, with the order violated deliberately at specific moments for effect. For VO-led commercial work the default is VO, then music, then effects, with the same rule about deliberate violation. Fincher's note to Klyce is the useful corrective: sound design is not ninety-six channels all at eleven, it is detail and nuance, things you might not notice until the second or third viewing. **[SOURCED]**

## A4. Sound As A Narrative Device: The Single Designed Sound

Most short-form work does not need a sound *world*. It needs one designed sound that carries a story beat, placed exactly.

A chime. A click. A notification. A single indrawn breath. A lock disengaging. A machine settling. These are the moments where sound does the work that picture cannot, because the picture is showing a face and the sound is telling you what just changed.

Three rules govern this:

1. **The sound must be authored, not selected.** A stock notification tone is a stock notification tone. A designed one has a chosen fundamental pitch, a chosen attack, a chosen decay, and a chosen relationship to the score's key. Burtt's method applies at any scale: build it from real material chosen for feeling. **[PRINCIPLE, method SOURCED to Burtt]**

2. **The sound must be located at a frame.** Not "somewhere around the reveal." A specific frame of a specific shot. This is the single most common failure in short-form briefs.

3. **"There is an approval chime somewhere" is not a specification.** It is a wish. A specification reads: *approval chime, shot 14, frame 09 of the shot (00:00:21:14 in the master timeline), placed one frame before the on-screen state change so the ear leads the eye, pitched to the tonic of the score, decay tail 600ms running under the next cut.* That is buildable, checkable, and arguable. The wish is none of those things. **[PRINCIPLE]**

Why one frame before rather than exactly on? Because a sound placed precisely on the visual event often reads as slightly late, since the eye is already tracking the change. Leading by one or two frames tends to feel locked. This is a judgement to test on the day, not a law, but it is the right first guess. **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A5. Frame-Exact Sync Points, And Why They Are Shared Property

A sync point is a contract between two departments. It says: at this frame, this thing happens in picture and this thing happens in sound, and neither department may change it unilaterally.

Locking a sound to a frame means:
- The frame is expressed in timecode against a named, versioned master cut.
- The shot is identified, not just the timecode, so the point survives an upstream trim.
- The intended relationship is stated (on the frame, one frame early, on the first frame of the incoming shot).

This is a shared editor and sound commitment because both departments can break it. If the editor trims two frames off the outgoing shot, the sync point moves. If sound retimes the effect's attack, the sync point moves. Either change is legitimate on its own and catastrophic if unannounced.

The professional habit is a **sync point register**: a numbered list of every frame-locked event, owned jointly, with a version number attached to the cut it refers to. When the cut version changes, every entry in the register is re-verified, not assumed. **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A6. Music: Score, Licensed Track, Or Library

| Route | What you get | What it costs | When it is right |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Original score** | Music written to your exact structure, owned or licensed cleanly, thematic material you can reuse | Time, composer fee, needs a brief and a lock | Anything with a distinct emotional arc, anything that will run for years, anything needing cutdowns |
| **Licensed track** | Instant cultural recognition, an existing fanbase, a pre-formed emotional association | Sync licence plus master licence, term, territory, media, exclusivity, and a fee that scales with all of them | When the recognition IS the idea |
| **Production library** | Speed, low cost, predictable clearance | Non-exclusive, so a competitor may use the same cue; generic by construction | Fast-turnaround, low-stakes, or high-volume social work |

On licensing economics: sync and master are two separate permissions from two separate owners. Most sync deals are non-exclusive unless a premium is paid, and national campaigns often require exclusivity specifically to keep the track out of a competitor's ad. Advertising favours all-in buyouts (one lump sum, no backend) because brands want predictable costs. Territory drives price sharply, with worldwide rights costing multiples of a single-territory licence, and perpetuity costing more than a defined term. **[SOURCED]**

### A6.1 Working from a locked picture vs writing to the script
Both are legitimate, and they produce different music.

Writing **to the script, before picture**, gives the film music with its own architecture rather than music that follows edits. Hildur Guðnadóttir prefers to come in early. On *Joker*, Todd Phillips sent her the script and asked what she thought it sounded like, and she began writing immediately from the script. **[SOURCED]** Jóhann Jóhannsson worked the same way with Denis Villeneuve, starting conversations in pre-production, visiting the *Sicario* set, and writing one of *Arrival*'s main themes during the first week of filming, before seeing any footage, working from the script's ideas about language and time. **[SOURCED]** On *Dunkirk*, Christopher Nolan wrote the script in a musical shape and gave Hans Zimmer a recording of his own ticking watch to build into the music, and Zimmer ran three Shepard scales on three different timescales to match the film's three interleaved timelines. **[SOURCED]**

Writing **to a locked picture** gives you precision and hit points, at the cost of the music becoming subordinate to the edit's existing rhythm. Trent Reznor and Atticus Ross came onto *The Social Network* after shooting, with Fincher already in rough cut; Reznor read the script twice and watched the first forty minutes to absorb the pacing before writing. Their working method was two simultaneous live performances, Reznor playing violin and acoustic guitar into a mic feeding loopers and modular synths performed by Ross, improvising for an hour and then editing the recording into sections. **[SOURCED]** That is a useful model for short-form: **generate material, then edit material, rather than composing linearly to picture.**

Nicholas Britell's *Moonlight* process shows the third route, transformation. Barry Jenkins asked him to score with "chopped and screwed" southern hip hop in mind, so Britell wrote a straight piano and violin piece for the child, then slowed and pitched that same recording down roughly three octaves for the fight scene so the theme returns as a sub-bass rumble. **[SOURCED]** One theme, processed, carries a character across a whole film. In advertising terms, that is exactly how you build a music asset that survives a three-year campaign.

### A6.2 Tempo mapping, and why music constrains the edit
A tempo map assigns a BPM (and therefore a grid of beats and bars) to the timeline. Once music is on a grid, every cut either lands on the grid or fights it. This is the constraint most editors underestimate.

If the music is 120 BPM, a beat is 0.5 seconds, which at 25fps is twelve and a half frames. That means the edit's available cut points are now roughly every twelve or thirteen frames if you want cuts to feel musical. Trimming a shot by four frames is no longer a free move, it either breaks the musical relationship or forces a music edit. **[PRINCIPLE]**

The consequences, stated plainly:
- **Choosing music early constrains the edit.** That can be good, it gives the piece a spine.
- **Choosing music late constrains the music.** The composer inherits a rhythm that was set by picture and must accommodate it.
- **You cannot have both freedoms.** Someone decides which one is fixed, and it should be decided consciously by the director, in writing, before either department commits.

### A6.3 The temp track problem
A temp track is existing music laid under the offline to indicate mood and tempo. It is now near-universal, and composers are virtually never handed a picture without one. The pathology is called **temp love**: the director hears the temp so many times that no original score can displace it. Kubrick's rejection of Alex North's *2001* score in favour of the temp is the extreme case. **[SOURCED]**

Mitigation, in order of usefulness:
1. Temp with the *function* rather than the *record*. Choose temp that has the right shape and energy, not the record you wish you could afford. **[PRINCIPLE]**
2. Write down what the temp is doing (tempo, instrumentation, where it lifts, where it drops) so the composer is briefed on function, not asked to imitate. **[PRINCIPLE]**
3. Get an original sketch into the cut early, even a rough one, so the ear has an alternative before the temp becomes load-bearing. **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A7. Mixing For VO-Led Short-Form

A full-runtime voiceover changes everything, because it removes the mix's most powerful tool: the option to let something else be foreground.

What changes:

- **The VO is the spine, and its timing is the piece's timing.** The final read's pacing effectively sets the runtime. If the read comes in three seconds long, either the picture stretches or lines are cut. This is not a mixing problem, it is a structural problem that surfaces in the mix.
- **Everything else is scheduled around the breaths.** The gaps between phrases are the only places music and effects can be foreground. Those gaps are the piece's only dynamic range.
- **Ducking becomes structural rather than corrective.** Ducking is automatic or manual attenuation of music and effects under the voice. Done badly (a fast automatic sidechain) the music audibly pumps and the piece sounds cheap. Done well, the music is written or edited so that it *thins* where the VO sits, and the ducking only has to do a few dB of work. **[PRINCIPLE]**
- **Breathing room costs the edit.** A VO needs air before a key line and a tail after it. If the picture cuts hard on the last syllable, the line has no landing. Buying that air means either a longer shot or a slower cut, and that is a direct, unavoidable cost paid by the editor. **[PRINCIPLE]**

The three-way negotiation is always the same: **the VO wants air, the editor wants pace, the music wants a bar structure.** All three cannot win. The director decides which two bend.

Practical mix targets for VO-led work:
- Keep the music's midrange (roughly 1kHz to 4kHz) clear of the voice, ideally by arrangement rather than by EQ. A composer told about this in advance writes a thinner middle. A composer told after the fact gets EQ'd. **[PRINCIPLE]**
- Effects that must be heard should sit in the VO's gaps or outside its frequency range.
- Any designed hero sound should land in a breath, not under a word.

## A8. Delivery And Platform Reality

### A8.1 Sound-off viewing
A large share of social video is played muted. Widely cited figures put Facebook video around 85% watched without sound and LinkedIn around 80%, with Instagram materially lower, around 40%. Captioned video is associated with substantially higher completion, and a meaningful share of viewers turn sound on *because* the captions caught them. **[SOURCED, industry-reported figures, treat as directional not exact]**

The consequence is not "sound does not matter." It is: **the piece must survive muted, and must reward unmuting.** Which means the story cannot depend on an audio-only reveal, and the sound design should give a distinct payoff to the minority who hear it. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A8.2 Loudness standards
Loudness is measured in LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale), integrated over the programme.

| Context | Integrated target | True peak ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| European broadcast (EBU R128) | -23 LUFS, tolerance about ±1 LU | -1 dBTP |
| US broadcast (ATSC A/85) | -24 LKFS | -2 dBTP typical |
| YouTube, Spotify and most social | around -14 LUFS | -1 dBTP |
| Podcast / Apple | around -16 LUFS | -1 dBTP |

EBU R128 also caps loudness range (LRA) at 20 LU, and broadcast compliance is typically a hard delivery requirement rather than a recommendation, whereas streaming targets are normalisation behaviours you are mixing to meet rather than legal obligations. **[SOURCED]**

The practical rule: **a single master cannot serve broadcast and social.** A -23 LUFS broadcast master uploaded to social will be normalised up and sound flat and over-limited. A -14 LUFS social master delivered to broadcast will be rejected. Two masters, minimum. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A8.3 Format and speaker reality
Cinema and premium streaming may want immersive (Dolby Atmos or 5.1) plus a stereo fold-down. Everything else wants stereo, and much of it will be heard on a single phone speaker with essentially no bass below about 300Hz.

Two non-negotiable checks: **the mono fold-down** (does anything phase-cancel or disappear) and **the phone-speaker check** (is the dialogue or VO still intelligible with no low end). In immersive workflows the equivalent trap is the binaural headphone fold-down, where dialogue routinely gets buried by music and effects that were perfectly clear in the room. **[SOURCED]** Anything that only works in the mix room is not finished.

## A9. How Advertising Sound And Music Differ From Feature Work

| Dimension | Feature | Advertising |
|---|---|---|
| Runtime | 90 to 180 minutes | 6, 15, 20, 30, 60 seconds, plus vertical cutdowns |
| Structure | Acts, sequences, scenes | Hook, build, turn, product, end-card sting |
| Music decision | Composer engaged for months | Often decided in the brief, before a frame is shot |
| Rights | Usually work-for-hire, in perpetuity | Term, territory, media, and exclusivity all negotiated and priced |
| Mix versions | A handful of language and format deliverables | Often dozens, across durations, aspect ratios, and platforms |
| Sonic goal | Immersion | Attribution and recall |

Specific things advertising sound must do that features do not:

**The 30-second structure.** A 30-second spot has roughly four sonic moves in it, not forty. Typically: an attention event in the first two seconds, a bed that establishes tone, one designed hero sound at the story turn, and the end-card sting. Anything else is decoration.

**The end-card sting and the audio logo.** The sting is the branded musical or designed punctuation at the logo. Effective audio logos are short (roughly two to three seconds), distinctive, flexible across contexts, and materially more effective when they include the brand name. The commonly cited reference points are Intel's five-note figure, the Netflix "ta-dum," and the NBC chimes. Industry research consistently reports that sound is processed faster than image (figures around 0.3 seconds versus around 1.2 seconds are widely quoted) and that brands with strong sonic assets show measurably higher brand power in Kantar BrandZ data. **[SOURCED, vendor-published research, treat the specific percentages as directional]**

**Sonic branding as an asset, not a decoration.** An audio logo is designed once and deployed across years, so it must survive being played on a phone speaker, in a shopping centre, at the end of a 6-second bumper, and stretched over a 60-second brand film. Design it at the shortest duration first, then extend. **[PRINCIPLE]**

**Cutdowns.** The 6-second version is not the 30 with the middle removed. It is a separate mix with its own hierarchy, usually VO plus sting plus one effect. Plan the cutdowns while designing the hero, or you will discover the hero sound only works at 30.

**Recognition of the craft.** Cannes Lions awards audio work through the Audio & Radio Lions and the Entertainment Lions for Music, the latter including a category specifically for use of original composition for a brand, with craft and execution weighted at 30% of the judging criteria. **[SOURCED]** The specialist advertising sound houses (in London, studios such as Wave, Factory, 750mph, and String and Tins) exist because commercial audio post is a distinct discipline with its own turnaround, versioning, and client-attendance culture. **[SOURCED]**

## A10. Vocabulary Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Room tone** | The recorded silence of a specific location, used to fill gaps and hide edits |
| **Ambience / bed** | A constructed background environment layered under a scene |
| **Foley** | Human-scale sounds (feet, cloth, props) performed in sync to picture |
| **ADR** | Automated (or additional) dialogue replacement, re-recorded in a studio to picture |
| **Spot session** | The meeting where director, editor, composer, and sound decide exactly where every cue and key effect starts and stops |
| **Stem** | A premixed group of one department's audio (dialogue, music, effects), delivered separately for later rebalancing |
| **D-M-E** | The standard dialogue / music / effects stem set |
| **Premix / predub** | The stage where each department is balanced internally before the final mix |
| **Sting** | A short musical or designed punctuation, typically at a logo or a turn |
| **Ducking** | Attenuating music and effects under a voice so the voice stays foreground |
| **Sweetening** | Adding and refining effects to make a track feel richer or more real than the raw capture |
| **Temp track** | Provisional existing music used in the offline to indicate mood and tempo |
| **Temp love** | The condition where a director can no longer accept anything but the temp |
| **Needle drop** | Use of a pre-existing commercial recording |
| **Sync licence** | Permission to use a composition with picture, granted by the publisher |
| **Master licence** | Permission to use a specific recording, granted by the recording's owner |
| **LUFS / LKFS** | Loudness units relative to full scale, the integrated loudness measurement |
| **dBTP** | Decibels true peak, the inter-sample peak ceiling |
| **LRA** | Loudness range, the spread between quiet and loud across a programme |
| **Shepard tone** | An auditory illusion of endlessly rising or falling pitch |
| **Diegetic / non-diegetic** | Sound that exists in the story world / sound that exists only for the audience |
| **Worldize** | Playing a sound through a speaker in a real space and re-recording it, to give it that space |
| **Hit point** | A frame where music is required to land on a picture event |

# PART B: AGENT OPERATING MANUAL

This half is written so an AI subagent can occupy the audio department's chair: ask its questions, make its decisions in the correct order, defend its boundaries, and produce its document.

## B1. Opening Questions On Receiving A Script, VO, And Shot List

Ask these before proposing anything. Refusing to answer any of them is itself information.

**About the delivery reality (ask first, because it constrains everything):**
1. What are the exact deliverables? Durations, aspect ratios, platforms, languages.
2. What is the loudness spec for each? Broadcast, social, cinema, in-store?
3. Will this ever be heard on anything other than a phone speaker?
4. Is there a captioned, sound-off version, and does the story survive muted?

**About the picture:**
5. Is the cut locked? If not, when, and what version number am I working to?
6. Is there a frame-numbered timeline with shot IDs, or only a video file?
7. What is the runtime, to the frame?
8. Which shots contain screen events, UI, or VFX that need audio, and are those events frame-numbered?

**About the voice:**
9. Is the VO the final read or a scratch? If scratch, when is the final, and is the casting locked?
10. Was the read timed against this cut, or was the cut timed against the read?
11. Is there air before and after each key line, or does picture cut hard on the last syllable?
12. Are there language versions, and does the translated read run longer? (It usually does.)

**About music:**
13. Is music original, licensed, or library, and who has decided?
14. If licensed: what term, territory, media, and exclusivity are budgeted?
15. Is there a temp, and how attached is the director to it?
16. Is there an existing brand audio logo or sonic asset that must appear, and in what form?
17. Is the edit allowed to move to fit the music, or must the music fit the edit? Get an answer, in writing.

**About tone and intent:**
18. What is the emotional register, stated in nouns rather than adjectives? What does the audience feel at second 3, second 15, and on the end card?
19. Which single moment is the piece actually about? That is where the hero sound goes.
20. What is forbidden? Name the sounds and music styles this piece must not use.

## B2. Decision Checklist In Dependency Order

Run in this order. Later items cannot be settled before earlier ones without rework.

1. **Delivery targets fixed.** Platforms, durations, loudness specs, mono and phone-speaker requirement. Everything downstream is judged against this.
2. **Register and palette agreed.** Texture families, tonal centre, exclusion list. One page, approved by the director.
3. **Music route decided.** Original, licensed, or library. If licensed, rights position confirmed *before* the track is cut into anything anyone will see.
4. **Tempo and grid decided, or explicitly deferred.** If the music route is original and tempo is set, the editor now knows the frame grid. If deferred, state that the edit is authoritative and the composer will conform.
5. **VO final read received and timed.** No mix approach is real against a scratch read.
6. **Spotting pass.** Walk the locked cut with the director and the editor. Mark every cue in and out, every hero sound, every silence, every hit point. Produce the sync point register.
7. **Hero sound designed and located.** One or two, no more, each at a named frame.
8. **Ambience and foley plan.** Which spaces exist, which bodies exist.
9. **Sync point register signed off jointly by editor and sound.** Versioned to the cut.
10. **Premix.** Departments balanced internally.
11. **Final mix against the primary delivery target.**
12. **Fold-downs and derivatives.** Mono check, phone check, alternate loudness masters, cutdowns.
13. **Stem delivery and archive.** D-M-E plus any splits the client will need for future versioning.

## B5. Standard Tensions And Typical Director Resolutions

### B5.1 Editor vs Sound: the frame-sync tension
**The tension:** the editor's job is rhythm, so trimming two or four frames is a normal, cheap move. Sound's job includes frame-locked events, so a two-frame trim can silently break a designed sync point and nobody notices until the mix.

**Typical resolutions:**
- **Hard lock with a change list.** Picture locks; any change after lock is issued as a formal change note with frame counts. This is the feature standard and it works.
- **Protected regions.** Certain shots containing sync points are declared untrimmable. The editor gets total freedom everywhere else.
- **Sound conforms, on a clock.** The editor keeps trimming freedom, and sound re-conforms, but the producer accepts a stated cost per re-conform. This is honest and usually the cheapest option in short-form.

The wrong resolution is "we will just fix it in the mix," because a broken sync point is not a level problem.

### B5.2 Editor vs Sound vs VO: the breathing room tension
**The tension:** the VO needs air before and after key lines, the editor needs pace and has a fixed runtime, and the music needs its bars to complete. All three are competing for the same seconds.

**Typical resolutions:**
- **Cut a line.** The most common and usually the best. If the runtime is fixed and the read needs air, the script is too long. Removing a phrase buys air everywhere.
- **Take the air from the top.** Establishing shots are almost always more compressible than the VO's landing beats.
- **Let the music resolve past the last frame.** Rather than forcing the music to land exactly on the end card, let the tail run under the logo hold. Cheap, and usually invisible.
- **Re-record the read to a timing target.** Only viable if the VO is not yet locked.

Director's call, and it should be made explicitly, because whichever way it goes one department is absorbing a cost.

### B5.3 Composer vs Editor: who is authoritative
**The tension:** if music is written first, the edit must respect the grid. If picture is locked first, the composer must hit points that were not composed for.

**Resolution:** the director declares, in writing, before either starts, which one is authoritative. Jóhannsson writing an *Arrival* theme in the first week of filming and Reznor and Ross coming in over a rough cut are both correct methods, but they are different methods and cannot be chosen retroactively. **[SOURCED as method examples]**

### B5.4 Sound vs Client: the "make it louder" note
**The tension:** a client compares the piece against a competitor's ad that is aggressively limited and asks for more level. Loudness normalisation on every major platform means the extra level is removed on playback and only the crushed dynamics remain.

**Resolution:** demonstrate. Play both at matched LUFS. The note almost always dissolves. If it does not, deliver a compliant master and a separate louder master and let the platform decide.

### B5.5 Sound vs Music Supervision: the uncleared track
**The tension:** a temp track becomes beloved and was never clearable at the budget.

**Resolution:** never present a track to a client that has not been price-checked for the required term, territory, media, and exclusivity. This is a process rule, not a taste question.

## B6. Failure Modes And Under-Specification Tells

Treat each of these as a blocking defect. State the defect, state what is missing, propose the specific replacement.

| Tell | Why it is a defect | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| "There is an approval chime somewhere" | A designed sound named but never located. Unbuildable and untestable. | Demand shot ID, timecode to the frame, and the intended lead or lag |
| "Make the music epic / premium / cinematic" | An adjective brief. Adjectives are unfalsifiable, so revisions are infinite. | Convert to: tempo range, instrumentation, reference function (not reference record), where it lifts, where it thins for VO, what it must not sound like |
| A mix planned without a stated delivery platform | Dynamic range, loudness, and format are all undecidable | Refuse to premix until the platform list and LUFS targets are written down |
| Working to a scratch VO | Read timing sets the piece's rhythm. Everything built on scratch is provisional. | Mark all work provisional in writing and re-derive after the final read |
| Sound briefed after picture lock | The most common structural failure. Sound inherits problems it could have prevented. | Randy Thom's argument applies: bring sound in at script stage **[SOURCED]** |
| No room tone recorded on location | Every dialogue edit will have an audible hole | Flag on the day, not in post. In post the only fix is synthesis or ADR. |
| Screen events discovered at the mix | VFX or motion added a UI event nobody sonified | Require a frame-numbered screen-event list before lock |
| Forty sound elements in a 30-second spot | Density beyond Murch's two and a half. Nothing is foreground. **[SOURCED]** | Cut to four moves: attention event, bed, hero, sting |
| An audio logo delivered only as a stereo mixdown | Cannot be re-tempoed, re-pitched, or extended | Require stems and the session's tempo |
| Only one master for all platforms | Broadcast and social loudness targets are incompatible | Two masters minimum, three if cinema is involved |
| Temp track presented to client without clearance pricing | Guarantees either a budget blowout or a disappointed client | Price-check before any client sees it |
| A cue list with no out points | Music that never ends is music that has no shape | Every cue has an in frame and an out frame |

## B8. Trigger List: What Forces A Re-Open

For each upstream change, distinguish what must be **genuinely re-derived** (the underlying decision is now invalid) from what can be **merely relabelled** (the decision holds, only its coordinates changed). Confusing these two is how sound departments either waste weeks or ship broken work.

| Upstream change | Genuinely re-derive | Merely relabel |
|---|---|---|
| **Pacing pivot** (the cut's rhythm changes materially) | Tempo map, cue in and out points, every hit point, ducking automation, the density plan (a faster cut supports fewer simultaneous layers) | Palette, register, forbidden list, delivery targets |
| **VO recast** | Everything downstream of the read: mix hierarchy, ducking depth, air allocation, cue timings, whether lines still fit. Treat the entire mix approach as invalid. | Hero sound design (the sound itself survives), palette, music route |
| **VO re-read, same voice, same script** | Line-by-line timings, air allocation, ducking automation, any cue whose in point was tied to a line | Palette, hierarchy, hero sound build, music selection |
| **Runtime change** | Cue structure and music arrangement (a 30 cut to 20 is not a 30 with 10 seconds removed), sting placement, which sonic moves survive, loudness measurement (integrated LUFS changes with duration) | Palette, register, hero sound build |
| **Shot added** | Ambience for the new space, foley for any body in it, whether the added shot creates a new screen event, sync point register verification for every point after the insertion | Everything before the insertion point, if timecodes are re-issued rather than assumed |
| **Shot removed or trimmed** | Every sync point after the change, room tone continuity across the new join, whether a cue's hit point still exists at all | Palette, hero design, music route |
| **New screen event needing audio** (VFX or motion adds a UI state, reveal, or transition) | Whether it competes with the existing hero sound (two hero moments cancel each other), its frame position, its place in the density budget, its pitch relationship to the score | The overall palette, provided the new sound is drawn from it |
| **Platform or delivery change** | Loudness targets, dynamic range, limiting, fold-down checks, whether low-frequency content survives, whether the story still works muted, master count | The creative content of the mix, mostly. This change is usually technical, but if the new platform is phone-only it can also invalidate any sub-bass-dependent hero sound. |
| **Music route change** (original becomes licensed, or the reverse) | Tempo map, cue structure, all hit points, the arrangement's relationship to VO frequency space, rights position and its cost | Palette, hero sound, ambience plan |
| **Licensed track fails clearance** | The entire music plan. Do not attempt a "similar" track. Re-derive the music function from the register, then source. | Sound design layers, which are independent of music selection |
| **Aspect ratio or vertical version added** | Nothing in sound, technically. But confirm whether the vertical cut has a different runtime or shot order, in which case treat it as a runtime change plus shot changes. | Usually everything, if the audio timeline is identical |
| **Director changes the tonal register** | Palette, exclusion list, music brief, hero sound build, ambience character. This is the most expensive change and it should be resisted after lock. | Delivery targets, sync point locations (the events still happen where they happen) |

**The standing rule for all triggers:** when any change arrives, the first action is not to fix audio. It is to re-verify the sync point register against the new cut version and report which points survived, which moved, and which no longer have an event to attach to. Everything else follows from that list. **[PRINCIPLE]**

## Closing Note

The audio department is the last department to receive material and the first one blamed when a piece feels cheap. That asymmetry is structural, and the only defence against it is specificity: a written palette, a numbered sync register, a cue list with out points, a stated loudness target, and a hero sound located at a frame rather than described as a vibe.

Randy Thom's point stands as the summary. The job is not creating great sounds. It is being in the room early enough that the sounds have something to do. **[SOURCED]**
