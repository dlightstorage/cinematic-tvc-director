# FILM CRAFT BIBLE 12: THE SCRIPT SUPERVISOR

**Role:** Script Supervisor / Continuity Supervisor / Continuity / "Script"
**Scope:** Features, television, short films, commercials, branded content, and by extension any long document set that has to stay internally consistent
**Status:** Reusable craft reference. Contains no client-specific or project-specific material.

**Sourcing convention used throughout this document:**
- **[SOURCED]** = attributable to a named practitioner, a published book, a guild or union record, a peer-reviewed study, or a documented industry tool.
- **[PRINCIPLE]** = general craft consensus, teachable and defensible, but not tied to one named source.

# PART A: CRAFT REFERENCE

## A1. What The Script Supervisor Actually Is

Every other department on a set is producing something. Camera produces images. Wardrobe produces looks. Sound produces tracks. The script supervisor produces nothing that ends up on screen. The entire output of the role is a record and a warning.

That is the point. The script supervisor is the only person on a set whose full-time job is noticing what does not match. Everyone else is looking at the thing they are making. The script supervisor is looking at the relationship between the thing being made now and every other thing that has already been made or is still to be made.

The formal description is unusually clear for a film craft. The script supervisor oversees the continuity of the motion picture including dialogue and action within a scene, keeps detailed notes on every take, ensures that wardrobe, props, set dressing, hair and makeup stay consistent from scene to scene, and tracks the production unit's daily progress. The script supervisor is a department head, and is described as the editor's and the writer's representative on set as well as a direct aide to the director and the director of photography. **[SOURCED]**

The single most useful sentence in the whole job description is this one: it is the script supervisor's job to make sure that the film can be cut together after shooting has concluded. **[SOURCED]** Not "make sure it looks right." Make sure the assembly is possible at all. The script supervisor is a feasibility guarantor working in advance of a problem that will only become visible weeks later, in a room they will not be in.

Two structural facts follow from that, and they are the two facts that make this craft portable to document work:

1. **The failure is deferred.** A continuity error costs nothing on the day. It costs everything in post, when the fix is either expensive, ugly, or impossible.
2. **The person who will suffer is absent.** The editor is not on set. The script supervisor is the editor's proxy, arguing on behalf of somebody who cannot see the problem yet.

## A2. What The Role Owns, And Where Its Authority Ends

### Owns outright

| Owned | Meaning |
|---|---|
| The continuity record | The authoritative account of what was actually shot, in what state, in what order |
| The lined script | The master document of what coverage exists for every line and every beat |
| Facing pages and the editor's log | The per-take narrative handed to post |
| Screen direction and the axis | The role is the go-to person for determining the axis of a scene **[SOURCED]** |
| Eyeline calls | Where an actor looks off-camera so that coverage cuts together |
| Script timings | Estimated runtime before the shoot, tracked runtime during it |
| The current version of the shooting script | Including logging every deviation made by actors or director on the day **[SOURCED]** |
| Slate and take integrity | Consistent slate numbering across picture and sound, sound roll noted, MOS takes flagged **[SOURCED]** |
| Daily progress reporting | Pages, scenes and minutes shot, scenes completed, retakes, wild tracks. The script supervisor is the official timekeeper on any set **[SOURCED]** |

### Does not own

| Not owned | Owner |
|---|---|
| Whether to accept a mismatch | Director |
| Whether a shot exists at all | Director and DP |
| What the wardrobe or prop actually is | Costume, props, art |
| Whether to reshoot | Director and producer |
| The cut | Editor and director |
| Schedule decisions | First AD and producer |

**The authority is informational, not executive. [PRINCIPLE]** The script supervisor never stops a take. The script supervisor reports the mismatch, states the cost of it, states which shot it will collide with, and then the director decides. A script supervisor who tries to enforce continuity as a rule becomes an obstacle. A script supervisor who reports continuity as a consequence becomes indispensable.

This is the single most important behavioural rule in the whole craft and it transfers exactly to document auditing: **you are not the person who decides. You are the person who makes sure the decision is made knowingly.**

## A3. The Artifacts

The craft is defined by its paperwork. Learn the artifacts and you have learned most of the discipline.

### A3.1 The lined script

The script supervisor's copy of the shooting script, with a vertical line drawn down the page for every camera setup. Each line marks where that setup starts and stops, carries a short note of the shot description, and indicates whether the dialogue was on camera for that setup. The result is that an editor can look at any line of dialogue and instantly see how many angles cover it and what those angles are. **[SOURCED]**

Conventionally, a solid line means the action or dialogue is seen on camera in that setup, and a wavy or squiggled line means it was covered but off-camera or not usable as picture for that moment. **[PRINCIPLE]**

The lined script is a **coverage map**. Its real function is to answer, in one glance, the question "what do I have for this moment?" That question is the whole reason the artifact exists.

### A3.2 Facing pages

The facing page sits opposite the lined page. It carries the take-by-take detail: slate and take numbers, take duration, lens, camera, description of the action, screen direction, whether the take was circled, why a take was no good, and the director's comment. Modern continuity software generates Facing Pages, Lined Script, Editor Report, Timecode Log and Daily Progress Report from a single data entry pass. **[SOURCED]**

The lined script tells you what exists. The facing page tells you what state it is in. **[PRINCIPLE]**

### A3.3 The continuity report and the daily editor's log

Per take, the script supervisor logs: duration (traditionally with a stopwatch), position of the principal actors, screen direction of movement, key actions performed, lens used, and any variable specific to that production. With multiple cameras the script supervisor keeps separate notes for each camera. Every take is marked as no good, hold, or print, following the director's call. **[SOURCED]**

The distinction between "no good," "hold," and "print" is worth pausing on. It is a three-state confidence rating. Not binary. A hold is "usable but not preferred," which is exactly the category most document reviews fail to record and most badly need.

### A3.4 The daily progress report

Actual times shooting and breaks started and stopped, pages, scenes and minutes shot today, the same figures for yesterday, the total script, and the amounts remaining. Plus scenes completed, retakes, and wild tracks. **[SOURCED]**

Note the shape of that report. It is not a list of what happened. It is a **reconciliation**: today, cumulative, total, remaining. Every number is stated against the total it is a fraction of. That format exists precisely because the naked figure "we shot four pages" is meaningless and the reconciled figure is not.

### A3.5 Continuity stills

Photographic reference for wardrobe, props, hair, makeup and set state. Historically Polaroid, now digital and phone cameras, taken at the start and end of each day's shooting so that any department can check today against a previous day. **[SOURCED]** Contemporary continuity software locks screen grabs automatically to the correct take so that images do not need manual sorting. **[SOURCED]**

### A3.6 Pre-production reports

Before a foot is shot, the script supervisor produces a one-line continuity synopsis: for each scene, the time of day, the day in story order, and a one-line summary. Departments use it to work out the most advantageous shot order and to keep production, wardrobe, set dressing, hair and makeup in sync about the progression of time within the story. **[SOURCED]**

This is the artifact people most often skip on small jobs, and it is the artifact that prevents the most expensive class of error. It is a **story-time index**, and without it nobody can tell whether two scenes shot three weeks apart are supposed to be the same afternoon.

## A4. Timing The Script

The script supervisor may time the script, which is of enormous benefit to the director and producer, and will often attend the table read. **[SOURCED]**

### Before the shoot

Timing a script means reading it aloud, or as close to aloud as makes no difference, at performance pace, with pauses, business, and silence included. Dialogue is timed by delivery, not by word count. Action lines are timed by imagining the action played. The output is a per-scene time and a total.

The rough industry heuristic that one script page equals roughly one minute of screen time is a **budgeting** tool, not a timing tool. **[PRINCIPLE]** It is wrong scene by scene in both directions: a page of rapid overlapping dialogue can run forty seconds, and a half page of wordless action can run three minutes. The script supervisor's timing exists precisely because the page-count heuristic is unreliable at the level of the individual scene.

### During the shoot

Every take gets a duration. Continuity software times a take automatically from the moment the operator hits "roll take" to the moment they hit "cut take." **[SOURCED]** Those durations accumulate into a running estimate of assembled length, which is what lets a producer know on day eleven that the film is running long, at a point where scenes can still be cut or compressed rather than after the fact.

### Why this matters most on fixed-runtime work

On a feature, runtime is elastic within reason. On a broadcast commercial, a title sequence, a trailer, a station ident, or a sponsored piece with a contractual duration, runtime is a hard specification. **[PRINCIPLE]** A thirty-second spot is thirty seconds. There is no version where it is thirty-four and the client accepts it.

The consequence is that on fixed-runtime work, timing stops being a report and becomes a **constraint that governs shooting decisions**. If the timing says the piece is at thirty-eight seconds with the current beat structure, then either eight seconds of intent leaves the script before the camera rolls, or eight seconds of coverage is being shot to be thrown away, and someone should say which is happening out loud.

## A5. Matching: The Actual On-Set Decision Set

Matching is the practice of ensuring that action shot in one setup can cut against action shot in another.

| Matching class | What is tracked | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| Action match | The exact point in a gesture at which a line is spoken, an object is picked up, a body turns | Actor picks up the cup on "yes" in the wide and on "no" in the close |
| Prop state | Level of liquid, length of cigarette, open or closed, which hand, which pocket | Glass full in the wide, half empty in the reverse |
| Wardrobe state | Buttons, sleeves, collar, jacket on or off, scarf tucked or loose, jewellery | Jacket buttoned in the master, unbuttoned in the single |
| Hair and makeup | Parting, strands, sweat, blood, dirt, tear tracks, deterioration over a story day | Blood dries and darkens between setups within one story moment |
| Set dressing | Chair angle, door state, curtain, what is on the table | Door closed on the exit, open on the arrival |
| Body position | Where the actor is standing at the top and bottom of the take, weight on which foot | Actor ends the wide at the window and starts the close at the desk |
| Screen direction | Direction of travel and of gaze | Character exits frame right, enters the next shot frame right |
| Eyeline | Height and side of an off-camera look | Two characters both appear to look camera left, so they are not looking at each other |

The professional discipline here is not memory. It is notation. A script supervisor who is relying on memory is going to fail on the day a setup is revisited after lunch, or after a company move, or after a weekend, or after four weeks. **[PRINCIPLE]** The note is the deliverable. The recollection is not.

## A6. Screen Direction, The Line, And Eyelines

### The 180-degree rule

An imaginary axis runs through the two subjects of a scene. Keep the camera on one side of it and the spatial relationship stays consistent from shot to shot: the first character stays frame right of the second. Cross it and the characters swap sides on the cut, which can disorient the viewer. It applies to movement as well as to dialogue: a character exiting frame left should enter the next shot frame right, and a car leaving right should enter left. Crossing the line is also called jumping the line. **[SOURCED]**

The script supervisor is the person who knows where the line is. On a set with a lot of setups, everybody else is thinking about their own shot. **[SOURCED]**

### Legitimate ways across

There are documented techniques for getting to the other side without a jolt: restage the movement, arc the camera across the line in a single shot so the audience is carried over, or insert a buffer shot taken along the line itself as a neutral bridge. **[SOURCED]**

### The rule is a rule with a known exception rate

Deliberate breaks are a real stylistic tool with a real lineage: Dreyer in *The Passion of Joan of Arc*, Kubrick in *The Shining*, Godard within the first five minutes of *Breathless*, and Ozu and Wong Kar-wai as habitual practice. **[SOURCED]**

More interesting for an auditor: the empirical literature is not as decisive as the folklore. Research indicates that crossing the line can degrade the accuracy of a viewer's spatial representation of a scene and their memory for where objects were, but does not appear to damage memory for the narrative or the order of events, and a 2019 study in *Communication Research* by Kachkovski, Vasilyev, Kuk, Kingstone and Street found that while viewers can spot violations, the violations had a negligible effect on enjoyment. **[SOURCED]**

**The lesson for an auditor is not "the rule does not matter." It is "know the actual cost of the specific rule you are enforcing, so you can rank findings honestly." [PRINCIPLE]** An auditor who reports every deviation at the same severity is reporting noise.

### Eyelines

An eyeline is the direction and height of an off-camera look. If two characters are supposed to be looking at each other, their eyelines have to be complementary: one looks camera left and slightly up, the other camera right and slightly down, at heights consistent with where they actually stand. The script supervisor sets and holds these so that the coverage of a scene cuts together and the audience never doubts who is looking at whom. **[SOURCED]**

Eyelines are the most instructive continuity category for document work, because an eyeline error is **invisible in the single shot**. Look at either shot alone and nothing is wrong. The error exists only in the relationship. That is exactly the shape of the most dangerous document error, which is covered in A8.

## A7. The Taxonomy Of Continuity Error

Four families. Naming them matters, because each family is caught by a different search.

### A7.1 Physical

Props, wardrobe, hair, makeup, set dressing, body position, food and drink levels, damage and wear. The classic and most reported class. One of the earliest recorded examples is in Chaplin's *The Property Man* from 1914, where the Tramp loses his hat in one room and it is instantly back on his head as he enters the next. **[SOURCED]**

Physical errors are the easiest to spot and the least damaging. Audiences forgive them, and the minor ones often survive into the final cut through simple indifference. **[SOURCED]**

### A7.2 Spatial

Screen direction, crossing the line, eyelines, geography of a location, which door leads where, relative positions of people and objects. Caught only by holding the whole space in mind, not by looking at one frame.

### A7.3 Temporal

Time of day, story day, the passage of time within a scene, elapsed time between scenes, seasonal and weather markers, deterioration that should progress in one direction only. Story day is the governing concept: the fictional day a scene takes place on, tracked independently of the shooting date. Continuity software carries explicit chronology fields and lets the breakdown be exported by script day precisely because this axis needs its own view. **[SOURCED]**

Temporal errors are the ones that ruin credibility quietly. A shadow that shortens across a supposedly continuous conversation reads as wrong before a viewer can say why.

### A7.4 Logical

The most serious family. A character knows something they have not yet been told. A character refers to an event that has not happened. A rule stated in one place is contradicted by behaviour somewhere else. Editing errors of this type are explicitly recognised in the literature: an error occurs when a character in a scene references a scene or incident that has not occurred yet, or of which they should not yet be aware, a formulation traceable to Pat P. Miller's *Script Supervising and Film Continuity*. **[SOURCED]**

This class has a long and dignified history. The Roman poet Horace coined the phrase for it in the *Ars Poetica*, complaining that even the great Homer nods off, and the *Iliad* duly contains a minor character killed in Book V who is alive to witness his son's death in Book XIII. **[SOURCED]**

**Physical errors are noticed. Logical errors are believed. [PRINCIPLE]** A viewer who spots a moved coffee cup laughs. A viewer who absorbs a logical contradiction simply comes away with a broken understanding of the story and does not know why it did not work. That asymmetry is the entire argument for why the logical family gets audited first and hardest.

## A8. Continuity As A Document Discipline

Everything above describes a person auditing a physical world against a written one. The same discipline applies unchanged to auditing a written world against itself. A production bible, a brand book, a specification, a treatment plus its shot list plus its budget, a rulebook plus its examples: all of these are document sets that can contradict themselves, and the contradiction is found the same way.

Three ideas do most of the work.

### A8.1 A fact that is DESCRIBED versus a fact that is used as a PREMISE

Every fact in a document set has two possible roles.

- **Described:** the document states the fact. "The hero wears a red coat." "There are six locations." "The palette is warm."
- **Premise:** other reasoning in the document depends on the fact being true. "Because the coat is red, the crowd is desaturated so she reads in the wide." "Because there are six locations, the schedule is six days." "Because the palette is warm, the night exterior uses sodium sources."

The description is one sentence. The premises can be scattered across a dozen sections written by different people at different times.

**The most common and most dangerous document error is changing the description without updating the reasoning built on it. [PRINCIPLE]** The coat becomes blue. Someone updates the wardrobe section, because that is the section that obviously mentions the coat. Nobody updates the cinematography note about desaturating the crowd, because that note never says the word "coat," it says "so she reads in the wide." The document is now internally incoherent, and worse, it is incoherent in a way that reads fine section by section. Both halves are individually well written. Only the relationship is broken.

This is the eyeline error, exactly. Neither shot is wrong. The pair is wrong.

**Operational rule: when any fact changes, do not search for the fact. Search for the reasoning that depended on it. [PRINCIPLE]** Searching for "coat" finds the description. Finding the premises requires asking "what did this fact justify?" and then hunting the justifications, which will use entirely different vocabulary.

### A8.2 A rule written early can silently stop being true

A document is written in order. A rule is written on page four when the document contains twelve things. On page thirty, a thirteenth thing is added. The thirteenth thing intersects with the rule in a way the author of page four could not have anticipated, because it did not exist.

Nobody made a mistake. The rule was correct when written. The addition is correct in itself. The document is now wrong.

**This is the single most under-audited failure mode in any long document, because both halves pass inspection individually. [PRINCIPLE]** A reviewer reading linearly will approve page four and approve page thirty. The error lives in the intersection, and the intersection is not written down anywhere.

Examples of the shape:
- A rule says "every item in this category uses treatment X." A later section adds an item to that category that cannot take treatment X.
- A rule says "the only exception is Y." A later section creates a second thing that is functionally an exception but is never called one.
- A rule says "these are always paired." A later addition introduces an unpaired instance.
- A rule says "there is never more than one per section." A later addition puts two in one section.

**The hunt is not "is this rule correct?" The hunt is "what has been added since this rule was written, and does any of it touch the rule's domain?" [PRINCIPLE]**

### A8.3 Described-versus-shown, in both directions

A production bible names things and shows things. The named list and the shown list are two different lists maintained by two different impulses, and they drift.

Two directions, and both must be run:
- **Named but not shown.** Something is referenced in prose, in a rule, in a table, in a caption, but does not exist in the inventory of actual items. A promise with nothing behind it.
- **Shown but not named.** Something exists in the inventory, the shot list, the asset set, but is never referenced by any rule, description or count. An orphan.

Orphans are usually worse. A named-but-missing item announces itself as a gap. A shown-but-unnamed item is silently outside every rule in the document, including the rules that should have governed it.

## A9. Cross-Reference Discipline: Trace The Entity, Not The Section

The instinct when reviewing a document is to read it from the top. That instinct catches typos and catches nothing else.

**Continuity is found by tracing one entity through every place it appears, simultaneously, rather than reading section by section. [PRINCIPLE]** This is precisely what the lined script does for a line of dialogue: it collapses "every setup that covers this moment" into a single visual answer, so the editor never has to hold six documents in mind at once.

The method:

1. **Enumerate the entities.** A character, a location, a number, a colour rule, a count, a named asset, a piece of vocabulary, a constraint, a date.
2. **For each entity, collect every mention across the entire document set.** Every table row, every prose reference, every caption, every heading, every appendix, every example.
3. **Line the mentions up side by side and compare them to each other, not to your memory of what they should be.**
4. **Record the trace even when it is clean.**

The reason this works and linear reading does not is that contradictions are relational. Two statements a hundred pages apart are individually fine. Only adjacency reveals the conflict, and adjacency has to be manufactured deliberately because the document does not provide it.

**Entity classes that always deserve a trace: [PRINCIPLE]**

| Entity class | Why it drifts |
|---|---|
| Any count or total | Anything added or removed invalidates it, and the total is rarely near the thing that changed |
| Any named item | Names get changed in the obvious place and not in the references |
| Any rule with the word "always," "never," "only," "all," or "every" | Absolute quantifiers are the most fragile things in any document, because one addition breaks them |
| Any range (first to last, chapters one to nine, shots 1 to 40) | Insertions in the middle break both the range and everything numbered after it |
| Any cross-reference by number or label | The target moves, the pointer does not |
| Any colour, size, material or technical specification | Stated in one place, relied on in five |
| Any date or duration | Downstream schedules encode it |
| Any pairing or mapping | Additions on one side leave the other side incomplete |

## A10. Why Counts Go Stale

A count is a fact with an expiry date that nobody writes down.

A headcount, a build count, a location count, a shot range, an item total, a page total, a day total: each of these is true at the instant it is written and becomes false the moment anything is added or removed anywhere in the document. The count is almost never near the addition. Somebody adds a fourteenth item in section nine. The sentence "there are thirteen items" is in the introduction, six thousand words away, and the table of contents says thirteen, and the summary says thirteen, and a downstream calculation multiplies thirteen by something.

This is not hypothetical even in tooling. Professional continuity software has a documented failure where the Daily Progress Report math comes out wrong and the fix is to reset the script changes log, because the report is computing totals against a stale change record. **[SOURCED]** Even a purpose-built system that exists specifically to keep counts correct can hold a stale total when its underlying record has drifted.

**Rules: [PRINCIPLE]**

1. **Every structural addition or deletion mandates a re-total.** Not a check that the count "still seems right." A re-count from the actual items.
2. **Re-total every place the count appears, not just the primary one.** Introductions, summaries, headings, tables of contents, captions, downstream arithmetic, and any range expressed as "X through Y."
3. **Ranges are worse than counts.** Adding an item mid-sequence breaks the range and renumbers everything after it, and every reference to any of those numbers is now wrong.
4. **Counts stated in prose are the ones that get missed.** A number in a table is visibly data. A number in a sentence looks like writing.
5. **If a count cannot be re-derived from the document itself, it is not a count, it is a claim.** Flag it.

## A11. Named Practitioners And The Literature

**Sarah Y. Mason.** The Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences credits Mason as the first script supervisor, having invented the craft of film continuity for the director Albert Parker on the 1918 film *Arizona*, where she was credited as "Continuity Girl." She went on to win the Academy Award for best screenplay adaptation, with her husband Victor Heerman, for the 1933 *Little Women*. **[SOURCED]** The origin of the craft in a person who also wrote scripts is not a coincidence. Continuity work is a writer's discipline applied to a producer's problem.

**Pat P. Miller,** *Script Supervising and Film Continuity* (Focal Press, third edition 1998). The standard reference text of the craft, and the source cited for the canonical definition of the role and for the recognised class of editing error in which a character references something they should not yet know. **[SOURCED]**

**Mary Cybulski,** *Continuity: A Script Supervisor's Manual* (Focal Press). The modern practitioner-authored manual, written from inside a working feature career. **[SOURCED]** Cybulski's own professional site documents a parallel body of unit stills photography across features including *Paterson*, *The Wolf of Wall Street*, *Wonderstruck*, *The Knick*, *Dark Waters*, *Can You Ever Forgive Me?*, *Billy Lynn's Long Halftime Walk* and *The King of Staten Island*. **[SOURCED]** The combination is telling: the same eye that logs continuity is the eye that frames a still, because both jobs are the job of noticing the state of a thing.

**Shirley Ulmer and C. R. Sevilla,** *The Role of Script Supervision in Film and Television: A Career Guide* (Communication Arts Books, 1987). **[SOURCED]**

**Melanie Williams,** "The Continuity Girl: Ice in the Middle of Fire," *Journal of British Cinema and Television*, 2013. The scholarly treatment of the role's history and its gendering. **[SOURCED]**

**Thelma McFate Preece.** Founded the Script Clerks Guild in 1937, the organisation from which IATSE Local 871 grew. **[SOURCED]**

**IATSE Local 871.** Chartered in 1958 with 131 members, originally covering only the script supervisor craft. Since 2007 it has been known as "IATSE Local 871, Script Supervisors/Continuity, Coordinators, Accountants and Allied Production Specialists Guild," and it now represents roughly 2,500 members across script supervision, coordination, accounting and allied production specialisms. The local runs ongoing member training on continuity software. **[SOURCED]**

### On the historical naming

Until the late 1930s and early 1940s the role in the American industry was typically called continuity clerk, script reader or script girl, and was often split between two people, one continuity clerk and one script girl. Practitioners were frequently uncredited. The gendered term has disappeared from credits and survives only in speech. **[SOURCED]**

### The tooling

The dominant modern tool is ScriptE, in the market since 2007 with over 5,000 users, which builds a database from the imported script and its revisions, holds the scene breakdown with explicit chronology and script day fields, times each take automatically from roll to cut, lines the script, logs slates, and then generates Facing Pages, Lined Script, Editor Report, Timecode Log, Daily Progress Report, timing reports, wild track reports and camera roll reports from that one dataset. It supports multi-unit workflows where several script supervisors merge their work into a single unified lined script, and its data now imports directly into media management tooling used by post. **[SOURCED]**

The structural lesson from the tooling is worth stating plainly: **one dataset, many views. [PRINCIPLE]** The lined script, the editor's report and the progress report are not three documents, they are three renderings of one record. Document sets that maintain the same fact in three independently written places are guaranteeing drift.

## A12. Vocabulary Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lined script** | The shooting script with a vertical line drawn for each camera setup, marking where that setup starts and stops and whether dialogue was on camera. The coverage map. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Facing pages** | The page opposite the lined page, carrying take-by-take detail: slate, take, duration, lens, description, screen direction, circled status, comments. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Continuity report / editor's log** | The daily record of every take with all technical and performance detail, sent to editorial each shooting day. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Daily progress report** | The reconciliation of the day: times, pages, scenes, minutes, retakes, wild tracks, today against cumulative against total against remaining. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Matching** | Ensuring action, props, wardrobe and position in one setup can cut against another. |
| **Screen direction** | The direction of movement or gaze as it reads on screen, left or right. |
| **Crossing the line / jumping the line** | Moving the camera to the opposite side of the axis between subjects, reversing their screen positions. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Buffer shot** | A neutral shot taken along the axis, used to bridge a legitimate crossing. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Eyeline** | The direction and height of an off-camera look, set so that coverage cuts together believably. |
| **Story day** | The fictional day on which a scene occurs, tracked independently of the shooting date. |
| **Pickup** | A partial retake of a portion of a scene rather than the whole thing, shot to fix or improve one section. |
| **Insert** | A close shot of a detail (a hand, an object, a screen) shot separately from the main coverage, to be cut in. |
| **MOS** | A take shot without synchronous sound, so marked on reports and slates. The origin is disputed, with "motor only shot" and the Germanic-accented "mit out sound" as the leading theories. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Wild track / wild sound** | Sound recorded without picture. Its counterpart, a picture take with no sound, is sometimes called a wild picture. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Circle take** | A take marked as preferred by the director or DP, flagged in the notes for editorial. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Print / hold / no good** | The three-state quality rating applied to each take. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Slate** | The identifier for a take, kept consistent between picture and sound. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Homeric nod** | A continuity error in a long work, from Horace's remark in the *Ars Poetica* that even the great Homer nods off. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Retcon** | A deliberate retroactive change to previously established facts, as distinct from an accidental contradiction. **[SOURCED]** |

## A13. How Commercial And Short-Form Continuity Differs From Feature Continuity

| Dimension | Feature | Commercial / short-form |
|---|---|---|
| Runtime | Elastic within a range | A hard contractual specification |
| Shoot length | Weeks to months, memory cannot be trusted | One to three days, memory feels trustworthy and is not |
| Number of story days | Many, requiring formal chronology tracking | Often one or none, but frequently ambiguous |
| Dedicated role | A department head, often with an assistant and a second unit counterpart **[SOURCED]** | Frequently no dedicated person at all, duties absorbed by the AD, producer or director |
| Approval layers | Director, then producer | Director, agency, client, and often a legal or compliance pass |
| Consequence of an error | A visible flaw in one film | A flaw multiplied across every cutdown, every aspect ratio, every platform version |
| The dominant error class | Physical and spatial | Logical and specification-level: a claim, a logo state, a legal line, a product configuration |

Three things change materially in short-form.

**First, the multiplication problem.** A commercial is rarely one deliverable. It is a hero cut, cutdowns, vertical and square reframes, subtitled versions, and market variants. A continuity error propagates into every one of them, and a fix has to be applied to every one of them. **[PRINCIPLE]** The audit therefore has to run per-deliverable, not once.

**Second, the specification classes that do not exist in drama.** Product configuration, packaging generation, logo lockup and clear space, mandatory legal or regulatory text, claim substantiation, and pricing. These behave exactly like props and wardrobe, in that they must be identical across every shot and every version, but their failure mode is legal rather than aesthetic. **[PRINCIPLE]**

**Third, nobody is doing this job unless it is assigned.** On a feature the role is a department head with a named person in it. On a short-form shoot the role is frequently unassigned, and the result is that everybody assumes somebody else is holding the through-line and nobody is. **[PRINCIPLE]** The single highest-value intervention on a short-form production is naming the person who owns continuity, even if that person has other duties.

# PART B: AGENT OPERATING MANUAL

This half is written for an AI agent operating as the script supervisor and continuity auditor for a document set: a script, a shot list, a production bible, a set of specifications, or any combination.

## B1. Intake: The Exact Questions This Role Asks

Ask these before auditing anything. If they cannot be answered, that is itself the first finding.

**Version and authority**
- What is the current version of every document in the set, and which one wins if two disagree?
- What changed since the last version, and who changed it?
- Is there a change log, and does it list every change or only the ones somebody thought were notable?
- Which facts are locked and which are still in flux?

**Structure**
- How many discrete items exist in each enumerable category, and where is each total stated?
- What are the numbered sequences and ranges, and where are they referenced?
- Which tables exist, and which entities appear in more than one table?

**Rules**
- Which statements are rules rather than descriptions? Specifically, which contain "always," "never," "only," "all," "every," "must," or "no more than"?
- For each rule, when was it written relative to the rest of the document?
- What is the scope of each rule, and is the scope stated or assumed?

**Chronology**
- What are the story days, and is every scene assigned to one?
- What is the intended order of events, and does anything reference an event out of order?
- What does each character or actor know at each point, and where were they told?

**Continuity carriers**
- Which entities recur across sections: characters, locations, colours, materials, numbers, named assets, technical specifications?
- Which facts are used as premises for reasoning elsewhere?

**Deliverables**
- How many deliverables does this produce, and does each one need its own pass?
- What is the fixed runtime or fixed length, and where is it stated?

## B2. THE CONTINUITY AUDIT PROTOCOL

Run in this order. Do not skip step 0. Do not collapse steps 3 and 4.

### Step 0. Build the register

Before reading for meaning, read for inventory. Produce four lists.

- **Locked rules.** Every statement that constrains something. Record the exact wording, the location, and the quantifier used.
- **Counts and ranges.** Every number that totals something or bounds something. Record the number, its location, and what it is counting.
- **Cross-referenced entities.** Every entity that appears in more than one place. Record every location.
- **Premise facts.** Every fact that other reasoning visibly depends on. Record the fact, and record where the dependent reasoning lives.

This register is the lined script. Everything after this step reads from it.

### Step 1. Establish the change set

Identify what has been added, removed, renamed or changed since the previous stable version. If there is no previous version, treat the whole document as the change set and note that the audit is a full pass rather than a delta pass.

For each change, record two things: the change itself, and the date or position of the change relative to the rest of the document. The relative position matters because it tells you which rules predate the change and are therefore candidates for silent breakage.

### Step 2. Trace each locked rule forward

For every rule in the register, ask: **what exists in this document now that did not exist when this rule was written, and does any of it fall inside the rule's domain?**

This is the highest-yield step in the entire protocol. Work rule by rule, and for each rule enumerate the members of its domain from the current document rather than from the rule's own examples. A rule that says "all six of these use treatment X" is checked by counting the current members, not by rereading the six.

Record for each rule: domain members found, whether each complies, and whether the rule's quantifier is still accurate.

### Step 3. Trace each premise fact backward and forward

For every premise fact, run both directions.

- **Backward:** where did this fact come from, and is the source still stating it that way?
- **Forward:** what reasoning, calculation, description or decision depends on this fact, and does that reasoning still hold?

Critically, **do not search for the fact's vocabulary.** Search for the reasoning that used it. The dependent reasoning almost never repeats the fact's words. Ask "what would somebody have written because of this fact?" and hunt that.

### Step 4. Re-total every count

For each count and range in the register, re-derive it from the current items. Never verify a count by reading it. Verify it by counting.

Then find every other location where that count or range is restated: introductions, summaries, headings, tables of contents, captions, prose, and any arithmetic downstream of it. Each of those is a separate finding if wrong.

For ranges, additionally check every reference to any individual number inside the range.

### Step 5. Table against table

List every table in the document set. For each pair of tables that mentions the same entity class, compare them row by row.

Check specifically:
- Does every entity in table A appear in table B where it should?
- Do the attributes agree where both tables state the same attribute?
- Do the row counts agree with each other and with any stated total?
- Has anything been added to one table and not the other?

Tables are where drift is most detectable and least detected, because a table looks authoritative and rarely gets reread once written.

### Step 6. Shown against named, both directions

Build two lists from the document set:
- Everything that is **named or described** in prose, rules, captions and headings.
- Everything that **exists** in the actual inventory: the shot list, the asset list, the item schedule, the deliverable list.

Then run:
- **Named but not shown.** Report every reference with nothing behind it.
- **Shown but not named.** Report every item that no rule, description or count governs.

### Step 7. Chronology and knowledge

- Assign every scene or section to a story day. Report any that cannot be assigned.
- Check that anything progressive moves in one direction only: damage, deterioration, weather, time of day, accumulated knowledge.
- For every piece of information a character or reader is shown to possess, locate where they acquired it. Report anything known before it is learned.

### Step 8. Spatial and relational

- For each pairing or relationship, check both halves. Additions on one side of a mapping frequently leave the other side incomplete.
- For anything with a direction, a side, an order or a hierarchy, check that it is consistent everywhere it is stated.
- Look for the eyeline case: two statements that are individually correct and jointly impossible.

### Step 9. Report with exact locations

Every finding gets a precise location. Section, heading, table name, row, and the exact quoted wording. A finding without a location is not a finding, it is an impression, and the person receiving it has to redo the search you already did.

## B3. The Two Gate Types

There are exactly two audit modes, and using the wrong one wastes the pass.

### Gate 1: The backward and forward trace gate

**When:** After any single change, every time, without exception.

**What it is:** Take the one thing that changed. Trace it backward to its source and forward to everything that depends on it. Re-total anything the change touched. Check the change against every rule whose domain it might now be inside.

**Scope:** Narrow in origin, wide in reach. One change, but you follow it as far as it goes.

**Why it is run every time:** Because the cost of a single change is never local. This gate exists to prevent the described-versus-premise failure described in A8.1.

### Gate 2: The narrow negative-brief gate

**When:** The document is long, mostly correct, and a specific rule or vocabulary has been superseded.

**What it is:** The auditor is given two things explicitly:
- The exact **old** vocabulary, values, phrasings or patterns to hunt for.
- The exact **new** rule those instances must now satisfy.

The auditor then searches only for the old, and evaluates every hit against the new.

**Why this works far better than "review this document" once a document is long:** A general review of a mostly-correct long document produces a low signal-to-noise ratio, because the auditor's attention is spread evenly across text that is almost entirely fine. Attention is a budget. A general brief spends it uniformly. A negative brief spends all of it on the places where an error is actually likely.

**Construct a negative brief like this:**

> Hunt for: [exact old term, value, pattern, or phrasing]
> Every instance must now satisfy: [exact new rule]
> Search these locations: [document set, including appendices, captions, headings, tables of contents]
> For each hit, report: location, current wording, whether it complies, and the required correction.
> Report the total number of hits found, including the ones that already comply.

Note the last line. The count of compliant hits is what proves the search actually ran.

**Choosing between the gates:** Gate 1 is mandatory and reactive, triggered by change. Gate 2 is targeted and proactive, triggered by a known superseded rule. A mature process runs Gate 1 after every edit and Gate 2 whenever a rule changes rather than a fact.

## B4. Why "Checked, No Change Needed, And Here Is Why" Must Be Written Down

**A report that lists only what changed is indistinguishable from a report by somebody who forgot to check the rest.**

This is the core epistemic point of the whole role. The reader of a continuity report cannot tell the difference between:
- "I checked sections four, seven and eleven, and they were fine, so I said nothing."
- "I never opened sections four, seven and eleven."

Both produce the same document. Both produce silence. Only one of them is work.

The set-side equivalent is instructive: the daily progress report does not only report what was shot. It reports what was shot, cumulatively, against the total, and against what remains. **[SOURCED]** The unshot pages are on the report. The absence is documented.

**Operational requirement: every continuity audit report contains an explicit CHECKED AND CLEAN section, listing every entity, rule and count that was traced, the locations traced, and the reason it was found to be correct. [PRINCIPLE]**

The reason matters and cannot be reduced to a tick. "Checked" is a claim. "Checked: the count of nine in the introduction was re-derived by counting the rows in the schedule table, which returned nine" is evidence. The second one can be audited. The first one cannot.

Three further benefits:
1. **It exposes coverage gaps.** An entity that is on neither the findings list nor the clean list was not checked, and this becomes visible immediately.
2. **It prevents rework.** The next auditor knows what has already been traced and how.
3. **It disciplines the auditor.** Knowing you will have to write down what you checked and why changes how you check.

## B5. What This Role Needs From Others, And What It Owes Them

### Needs

| From | Needs |
|---|---|
| Writer / author | The current version, and explicit notice of what changed rather than a silent replacement |
| Director / owner | A decision, not a debate, on every mismatch reported. And a ruling on which document wins when two conflict |
| Every contributing department | Their own state record: what the item actually is, what changed, what was substituted |
| Producer / project owner | The list of deliverables, so that the audit runs per deliverable rather than once |
| Editor / assembler | The reverse channel: what did not cut together, so the record can be corrected and the next pass improved |

### Owes

| To | Owes |
|---|---|
| **The editor** | The complete, honest, same-day record. Every take, every option, every problem. Including the takes that were bad and why, because "there is no clean version of this line" is a fact the editor needs on day one, not in week six |
| The director | A warning before the setup is struck, not after. A continuity note delivered after wrap is a complaint, not a note |
| Every department | Their own continuity state, in their vocabulary, so they can act on it |
| The producer | The honest progress and timing reconciliation, including the unwelcome number |
| The next auditor | A record of what was checked, not only what was found |

### On the editor relationship specifically

The script supervisor is the primary liaison between the director, who decides what is shot, and the editor, who is not present during filming but needs an exact record in order to cut. **[SOURCED]** Post begins using the reports right after the first shoot day. **[SOURCED]**

Everything about that relationship follows from one asymmetry: **the editor cannot see what was not shot, and cannot see why.** The rushes show what exists. They do not show that the good take was ruined by a plane, that the actor changed a line and the change was approved, that a mismatch was noticed and consciously accepted, or that a whole angle was abandoned for time. Those facts exist only in the script supervisor's notes.

**Therefore the note that matters most is the note about the problem. [PRINCIPLE]** A continuity report that records only clean data is a report that has withheld the useful half. The equivalent in document work is the note that says "this section and that section disagree, the disagreement was raised, and the decision was to keep both because X." Without that note, the next reader finds the contradiction and reopens a settled question.

## B6. Failure Modes And Under-Specification Tells

### Failure modes of this role

| Failure | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| **Reading linearly** | Reviewing section by section and pronouncing each fine | Trace entities across sections instead |
| **Searching for the fact instead of the reasoning** | Updating every mention of the changed term and missing every consequence of it | Ask what the fact justified, hunt the justifications |
| **Verifying counts by reading them** | "The document says nine, and nine is stated consistently" | Re-derive from the items every time |
| **Flat severity** | Reporting a typo and a logical contradiction at the same weight | Rank: logical, then temporal, then spatial, then physical |
| **Silent clean checks** | Reporting only errors | Write the CHECKED AND CLEAN section |
| **Enforcing rather than reporting** | Blocking a decision on continuity grounds | Report the consequence, let the owner decide, record the decision |
| **Auditing once for many deliverables** | One pass, many outputs | Run the audit per deliverable |
| **Trusting the change log** | Assuming the log lists everything | Diff the actual documents |
| **Checking only what changed** | Missing the rule that broke because of what changed | Gate 1 is a trace, not a spot check |
| **Reporting without location** | "There is an inconsistency in the wardrobe section" | Section, table, row, exact quotation |

### Under-specification tells

These are the signals that a document is not yet auditable and needs its author before it needs its auditor.

- **No stated total anywhere for an enumerable category.** If nothing says how many there should be, nothing can be wrong, and that is worse.
- **A rule with no stated scope.** "Always use X" with no statement of what "always" ranges over.
- **Two documents with no stated precedence.** Nobody has said which wins.
- **A count stated in prose only, never derivable from a list.** It is a claim, not a count.
- **Ranges instead of enumerations.** "Shots 1 to 40" with no list of the forty.
- **Passive attribution of decisions.** "It was decided that" with no decider and no date, meaning the decision cannot be reopened or defended.
- **No story-day or chronology assignment.** Nothing can be checked temporally.
- **Examples that do not appear in the inventory.** The example was invented to illustrate the rule and no real member of the category was tested against it.
- **A section that no other section references.** Either an orphan or a section whose dependencies were never written down.
- **Version numbers that do not increment on change.** Every subsequent audit is now unbounded.

## B8. Trigger List: Every Change That Mandates A Continuity Pass

Each row states the change and the specific things it forces you to re-check. Re-checking anything not on the list is optional. Re-checking everything on the list is not.

| Change | Forces re-check of |
|---|---|
| **An item is added to any enumerable category** | Every total for that category, every range covering it, every "all / every / always" rule whose domain now includes it, every table listing the category, every downstream calculation using the total, the table of contents, and any summary |
| **An item is removed** | The same list, plus every cross-reference pointing to the removed item, plus every pairing that item was half of |
| **An item is renamed** | Every mention of the old name including captions, headings, file names, table rows and prose; plus every rule that referenced it by name; plus any alphabetical or logical ordering it participated in |
| **A rule is changed or replaced** | Every existing member of that rule's domain against the new rule; every place the old rule is restated or paraphrased; every exception previously carved out of the old rule; every example that illustrated the old rule. Run a negative-brief gate with the old vocabulary |
| **A value, colour, size, material or technical spec is changed** | The description itself, and separately every piece of reasoning that used the old value as a premise. Search for the consequence, not the value |
| **A section is inserted or reordered** | Every numbered reference after the insertion point, every range, every "see section X" pointer, the table of contents, and any narrative that depends on order |
| **Anything is renumbered** | Every reference to every affected number, everywhere, including inside other tables |
| **A date, duration or runtime changes** | Every schedule derived from it, every downstream date, every "X days before Y" expression, and the total |
| **A person, character or role is added or removed** | Headcount, every table listing people, every assignment or ownership mapping, every scene or section they appear in, every rule about who does what, and any "all of the above" statement |
| **A location is added or removed** | Location count, schedule, any geography or travel logic, every scene assigned to it, and any rule scoped by location |
| **Two documents are merged** | Every entity that appears in both, every rule that appears in both with different wording, every count, and precedence between the two |
| **A deliverable or version is added** | The whole audit, run again for that deliverable. Do not assume a pass on the hero version covers the variants |
| **A previously flagged finding is fixed** | The fix itself, plus everything the original error had already propagated into, plus whether the fix introduced a new collision |
| **A decision is reversed** | Every change that was made because of the original decision, tracked backward. Reversals are the most under-traced change type of all |
| **Any external constraint changes (legal, platform, regulatory, client)** | Every element governed by the constraint in every deliverable, plus any claim or statement that depended on the old constraint |
| **Time passes and nothing changes** | Nothing. Absence of change is not a trigger. This row exists to make the point that the trigger list is exhaustive and finite, and that an audit run without a trigger is a Gate 2 pass with no brief, which is the least productive thing this role can do |

## Closing Note

The craft summarised in one line: **the script supervisor is the person who holds the relationship between things while everybody else holds the things.**

On a set that means knowing that today's close-up has to cut against a wide shot filmed four weeks ago in another city. In a document it means knowing that a sentence on page four and a table on page thirty cannot both be true. Neither problem is visible from inside the thing you are looking at. Both are only visible to somebody whose entire job is the space between.
