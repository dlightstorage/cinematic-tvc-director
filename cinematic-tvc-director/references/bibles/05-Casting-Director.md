# PART A: CRAFT REFERENCE

## A1. What the Casting Director Actually Is

The casting director is the person who decides **who the audience will be asked to believe**.

Every other department builds the world. Casting supplies the only element of that world the audience will read involuntarily and instantly, in under a second, before a single line is spoken: a human face and the body it sits on. A wrong lens choice costs you a degree of elegance. A wrong face costs you the premise.

Juliet Taylor, who ran Marion Dougherty Associates in New York from 1973 and went on to cast more than forty Woody Allen films plus Spielberg's *Schindler's List*, described the skill as "a sort of intuition for not only spotting somebody's talent, but for putting together the chemistry of a whole picture, how each thing fits in." She also described the job in interviews as "a little bit like being a therapist. You have to learn to really appreciate people for what they are, whether you like them or not." Both halves of that are the job: pattern recognition about human beings, and the emotional labour of handling them.

The craft has a specific historical shape. Marion Dougherty, who died in 2011 and is the subject of Tom Donahue's documentary *Casting By* (assembled from over 240 interviews), is generally credited with the founding move: she cast **for acting ability and specificity rather than for physical type**. Working out of New York theatre from her own casting company founded in 1963, she pushed unglamorous, textured, non-leading-man faces into leading roles. The most cited instance is *Midnight Cowboy*, where she championed Jon Voight opposite Dustin Hoffman when director John Schlesinger wanted someone else. That is the origin point of everything modern casting claims as craft: the argument that the right person is not the same as the right type.

## A2. What the Role Owns, and Where the Authority Stops

This distinction matters more in casting than in almost any other department, because casting is the department most often blamed for a decision it did not make.

### Owns outright

| Domain | Specifics |
|---|---|
| The breakdown | The written specification of every role, in the language that goes out to agents and platforms |
| The search strategy | Where to look, which markets, agency channel vs open call vs street scout vs specialist community |
| The pool | Who is submitted, who is invited, who is seen at all |
| The audition design | Which sides, which scenes, what direction is given, what is asked for in a self-tape, how many takes |
| The tape itself | Framing, slate, sound, file naming, the order the director watches in |
| The shortlist and its argument | Which three to five names go forward per role, and the stated reason for each |
| Chemistry and combination logic | Which pairings and ensembles are tested against each other |
| Availability and deal reality | Schedule conflicts, quotes, holds, avails, first refusals, the negotiation frame |
| Compliance | Minors and work permits, union status, conflicts and exclusivity, usage rights, consent for real people |
| The room | The tone and safety of the audition environment |

### Does not own

- **The final choice.** Casting directors recommend. The director decides. On advertising, the director decides and then the agency creative directors and the client approve, and the client can veto for reasons that have nothing to do with performance.
- **The script.** Casting can flag that a role as written is uncastable, or that two roles are indistinguishable, but rewriting is the writer's and director's.
- **The budget envelope.** The producer sets what the cast can cost. Casting owns the consequences.
- **The schedule.** The 1st AD owns shoot days. Casting owns whether a chosen person is actually free on them.
- **Wardrobe, hair, makeup, physical transformation.** Casting can specify what needs to be achievable. Other departments deliver it.
- **Performance on the day.** Once the actor is on set, direction of performance is the director's.
- **[craft principle]** Casting also does not own the *strategy* behind who the film is for. In advertising this is the sharpest boundary: the brand strategist owns the audience segment, and casting owns the human being who will speak to that segment. Those are two different objects, and confusing them is the single most common failure in commercial casting (see A7).

## A3. The Recognition Architecture: CSA, Artios, and the Oscar

Understanding how the craft is recognised tells you what the industry believes the craft actually is.

- The **Casting Society** (long known as the Casting Society of America, CSA) has presented the **Artios Awards** for excellence in casting since the mid-1980s, with the first awards produced in 1984. Members are honoured across more than twenty-five categories spanning film, television, theatre, **commercials**, short films and short-form series, in simultaneous ceremonies in New York, Los Angeles and London, and with expanded international categories reflecting the global spread of the profession. The existence of a dedicated **commercials** category is meaningful: the industry's own body treats commercial casting as a distinct discipline, not a lesser one.
- The Academy established a **Casting Directors Branch** in 2013.
- In 2024 the Academy's board of governors approved a **Best Casting** Oscar, first awarded at the **98th Academy Awards in 2026** for films released in 2025. It was the first new Oscar category since Best Animated Feature in 2002. The inaugural winner was **Cassandra Kulukundis** for *One Battle After Another*, over fellow nominees **Francine Maisler** (*Sinners*), **Nina Gold** (*Hamnet*), **Jennifer Venditti** (*Marty Supreme*) and **Gabriel Domingues** (*The Secret Agent*).

**[craft principle]** The ninety-year gap between the first Oscars and the first casting Oscar is the craft's central political fact. It explains why casting directors document their reasoning so heavily, and why a good casting plan is a written argument, not a list of names.

## A4. The Decision Set

### A4.1 Breakdown writing

The **breakdown** is the casting director's primary written artifact: a short character specification distributed to agents and casting platforms (Breakdown Services / Breakdown Express and Actors Access are the dominant North American channel; Spotlight is the UK equivalent). It is simultaneously a brief, a job listing and a filter.

Craft rules that recur across Backstage's and Breakdown Services' own guidance and across working practice:

1. **Describe the role, not the actor you imagine.** "Warm, dry, unimpressed by everyone in the room" is castable. "Think a young Bill Murray" narrows the pool to imitators.
2. **Be broad on age range and ethnicity, precise on emotional register.** An age range should be a range. A specificity of behaviour should be exact.
3. **State the arc, even if the arc is three seconds long.** A breakdown that omits how the character changes produces one-note submissions.
4. **Disclose everything non-standard up front**: special skills, dialect, nudity, stunt or physical demands, animal work, singing, driving, on-camera product handling, extreme weather, night shoots. Discovering a requirement after casting is a scheduling and legal problem, not a surprise.
5. **State the usage and the commitment in commercial breakdowns.** Term, territory, media, exclusivity and conflict category belong in the breakdown, because they determine who can even accept.
6. **Name the size honestly.** If it is a one-line featured player, say so. Mis-sized breakdowns waste the pool's trust, and the pool is a renewable resource only if it is treated well.

### A4.2 The search: who gets seen

The default channel is agency submission against a breakdown. Ross Lacy, whose Los Angeles commercial house has cast over 3,000 commercials with Clio and Cannes Lions credits and specialises in comedy, dialogue, beauty, kids, Spanish-language, voiceover and real people, has described the volume shape of commercial casting: a breakdown goes to agencies, agents submit, and the casting director narrows something on the order of 2,500 to 3,000 submissions down to 50 to 100 actors brought in to read per role.

The channel widens the moment the role cannot be found in the pool. Francine Maisler's *Sinners* search is the clean modern example: tasked with finding a young man who could sing and play the blues, her team went beyond agency submissions to music schools, colleges and blues clubs across the South, New York and Chicago, and put breakdowns out in London while accepting self-tapes from anyone who could plausibly play American. Miles Caton, who had never acted, surfaced from that widened net; his tape was recorded in a very dark room at eighteen, and Maisler's account is that the voice "took your breath away."

Avy Kaufman's *Life of Pi* search for Ang Lee ran three months through roughly 3,000 international video submissions before she travelled to India and found Suraj Sharma. Ellen Lewis's *Forrest Gump* search for young Forrest ran through open calls to the point where the production was close to pushing, and the boy who was finally found at a Mississippi open call became the basis for Tom Hanks's accent.

**[craft principle]** The pattern is consistent and worth internalising: **when the role is genuinely specific, the pool is not where you find it.** Widening the search is not a fallback, it is the method.

### A4.3 The audition and the self-tape

**Sides** are the extracted scene pages an actor reads from. Modern breakdown platforms let casting upload a script, auto-extract roles and scenes, mark up digitally and distribute sides only to invited actors.

The **self-tape** has replaced the room as the default first round. Nina Gold, whose team scales to about six people depending on the project, describes the work as heavily logistical and tape-driven: "It really is very labour-intensive. It doesn't just all happen in isolation for that particular TV show or film. You're constantly amassing knowledge and thoughts and opinions on people. It's a constant process." On remote auditioning she notes the practical upside directly: it allows many more actors to have a go and lets you audition more people. Avy Kaufman has publicly noted the downside of Zoom auditions, which is the loss of the room.

Standard self-tape specification, per SAG-AFTRA's own self-tape guidance and Breakdown Services documentation:

| Element | Standard |
|---|---|
| Framing | Moderate close-up, roughly chest to just above the head, unless the casting director specifies otherwise |
| Orientation | Landscape / horizontal |
| Background | Plain, solid colour; a curtain reads better than a flat wall because the folds give depth |
| Slate | Name, height where relevant, agency, role, sometimes a full-body shot |
| Instructions | The casting director's written audition instructions govern framing, take count, file naming and what is being sought |
| Delivery | Secure platform (Eco Cast, Breakdown Express) or controlled file share, to prevent script leaks |

**[craft principle]** The casting director owns the tape as an *edited object*. The order the director watches in, what is trimmed, whether a redirect take is included, and what is deliberately withheld are all authorial choices. A shortlist is a cut, not a folder.

### A4.4 The callback

The callback is where the director enters. It is not a repeat of round one. It is where the director tests **direction-taking**: can this person change what they are doing when asked, and does the changed version get better. Debra Zane, whose credits include *Traffic*, the *Ocean's* films and *The Hunger Games*, has said the audition itself is her favourite part of the process, describing it as isolating the very best actors who are most clearly appropriate for the roles.

### A4.5 The chemistry read

A **chemistry read** puts two or more candidates together to test the relationship rather than the performance. It is a callback-stage tool, used mainly on leads and on any pairing the story structurally depends on. Not every project uses one; it is largely down to the director's or showrunner's preference.

Sarah Halley Finn, who has cast the entire Marvel Cinematic Universe since *Iron Man* in 2006, is explicit about using it structurally: she has described bringing actors together in a room to play a scene to see how they connect, and specifically had Tom Holland do a chemistry read with Robert Downey Jr. because that relationship was going to be load-bearing.

What is actually being read: comfort, rhythm compatibility, whether one performer's energy makes the other better or smaller, and physical tells of genuine engagement (open body language, heightened mutual awareness, real laughter). **[craft principle]** The failure mode is testing chemistry between two people who are both excellent and finding that neither is worse, which tells you nothing. Test contrasting pairings, not similar ones.

### A4.6 Holds, avails, pins, offers

The vocabulary of the endgame is deliberately imprecise because of union rules, which matters practically.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Avail / avail check** | A non-binding request that the performer keep the shoot date open. A courtesy, not a booking. |
| **Pin** | Informal signal that the performer is one of the final two or three for the role. Production wants the time held but is not ready to contract. |
| **First refusal** | Used primarily on commercials. The final decision is not made; the performer agrees to contact casting before accepting another booking on the same dates, giving the original production the first opportunity to book. |
| **Hold** | The stronger form. Casting directors technically cannot "hold" performers under SAG rules, which is precisely why "pinning" and "on avail" exist as language. |
| **Book / offer** | The actual commitment. |

On commercials, the typical rhythm is that selections from the callback go to the client the following day, and approved performers can be booked as early as the afternoon after callback or as late as several days into the shoot depending on shoot-day count.

## A5. Casting a Type Versus Casting a Person

This is the philosophical core of the craft and the thing an agent most often gets wrong.

**Casting a type** starts from a category ("late-thirties professional woman, warm, urban") and finds the best available instance of that category. It is fast, defensible to clients, low-risk, and produces work that looks like other work.

**Casting a person** starts from a specific human being's particular strangeness and reshapes the role slightly around it. It is slower, harder to sell, and it is where every memorable performance in the history of the medium came from.

Marion Dougherty's whole legacy is the second mode: casting on acting ability and specificity rather than physical type. Avy Kaufman states the same logic from the other side: "Usually, one person's really right for it and, for me, that's all. It doesn't mean you're not a good actor, but you're right for it."

Cassandra Kulukundis's *One Battle After Another* work is the current benchmark for mixing modes: she paired first-time film actors and non-actors with Leonardo DiCaprio, Sean Penn and Benicio Del Toro, and the film drew four acting nominations. For the character Danvers she showed prospective actors documentary footage of a real law enforcement agent, James Raterman; when no one could replicate his register, she tracked down Raterman himself and cast him. Her stated working principle: "I love actors, and I like creating a world where everyone can feel comfortable enough to make mistakes. I love discovering people that no one else has seen before, teaching them, and making them feel comfortable about the process."

**[craft principle]** The practical resolution is not to pick one mode. It is to decide, role by role, which mode that role is in, and to say so in writing. A brief that says "type-cast the two office colleagues, person-cast the lead" is a decision. A brief that is silent has defaulted to type for everything.

## A6. Casting for Advertising Specifically

Advertising casting has its own logic, and importing feature logic wholesale produces expensive mistakes.

### A6.1 The audience segment is not the cast list

The single most persistent error in commercial casting is the assumption that the person on screen must be a literal member of the target segment. It is a reasonable-sounding assumption and it is wrong often enough to be dangerous.

The on-screen person can occupy several different relationships to the target segment:

| Relationship | On-screen person is | Works when |
|---|---|---|
| **Mirror** | Demographically the target | The message is "this is for someone like you," category is low-consideration, and recognition is the job |
| **Aspiration** | Slightly ahead of the target in status, age, competence or looks | The purchase is identity-driven and the buyer wants to become something |
| **Authority** | Not the target at all; an expert, a professional, a maker | The barrier is credibility rather than desire |
| **Beneficiary** | The person the target buys *for* (a child, a parent, a partner) | The buyer's motivation is care rather than self-interest |
| **Antagonist / foil** | The person the target is not, played for contrast or comedy | The strategy is differentiation from a rival behaviour |
| **Absent** | Nobody, or hands only, or a voice | The product is the protagonist and any face would compete with it |

**[craft principle]** State the relationship explicitly for every on-screen role before casting anyone. "Mother, 30s, target segment" is not a casting spec. "Mother, beneficiary relationship, she is the person the buyer is buying for, so she must read as someone worth spending on rather than as the buyer herself" is a casting spec.

### A6.2 Relatability versus aspiration as opposing strategies

These are genuinely opposed, and a brief that asks for both usually gets neither.

Published brand-strategy analysis frames the tension consistently: aspirational branding's power sits in the balance between inspiration and attainability, and pushing too far into the unrealistic causes disengagement while staying too grounded loses the allure. The commonly cited rule of thumb is that aspirational advertising performs best when the on-screen person sits only slightly ahead of the target on the dimension being sold, so the well-cast neighbour outperforms the movie star for most everyday categories. Research on out-group portrayal points the same way: some degree of similarity between viewer and on-screen figure is required, or the viewer is alienated rather than inspired.

The two poles are visible in real category practice. Luxury and prestige categories have historically cast at the top of the fame and beauty distribution (the Lux model of casting only major stars). Categories built on everyday trust have deliberately cast non-celebrities and non-models (the Dove model). Sportswear frequently runs both simultaneously, using an elite athlete for aspiration and a relatable narrative frame to bridge back to the ordinary viewer.

**Practical decision rule [craft principle]:** ask what the ad is asking the viewer to feel about themselves. If the answer is "seen," cast relatable and cast close to the segment. If the answer is "capable of more," cast aspirational and cast one notch ahead. If the answer is "reassured," cast authority. Write the answer down before writing any breakdown, because it determines age, grooming, wardrobe register, accent and even how much the person should be allowed to be beautiful.

### A6.3 The client approval reality

Feature casting ends with the director. Commercial casting ends with a chain: casting director, then director, then agency creatives, then agency account leadership, then the client marketing team, then sometimes the client's legal, regional or global brand custodians. Each layer can veto and each layer optimises for different risk.

**[craft principle]** Design the presentation for the chain, not for the director alone. Present three genuinely different options per role rather than three variations of one option, because a chain that is shown near-identical choices will pick on irrelevant grounds. Always carry a documented second choice for every approved role, because approval can be reversed late by a layer that was not in the room.

## A7. Real People / Street Casting Versus Professional Actors

**Street casting** (also called real-people casting) sources non-professionals from the world rather than from agencies. Jennifer Venditti founded her New York agency JV8INC in 1998 and built the modern version of this practice, street scouting worldwide for advertising, fashion and film. Her stated basis is documentary: her background is documentary filmmaking, and she attributes the career to a lifelong obsession with people-watching, describing herself as "an insatiably curious person who loves humanity." On *Marty Supreme*, her fourth collaboration with director Josh Safdie and seventh with a Safdie, she had to fill over 150 roles that had to read as early 1950s, and she used 1950s street photography as her reference frame in order to look outside the established acting pipeline even for core roles.

Victoria Thomas, who casts for Tarantino among others, articulates the adjacent discipline for period and historically loaded material: sourcing from untapped pools to avoid tokenism, and casting to "get the world right" rather than forcing diversity for its own sake.

### What each mode costs you

| | Professional actors | Real people / street cast |
|---|---|---|
| **Buys you** | Repeatability, direction-taking, stamina across takes, dialogue capability, emotional control on cue, contract and usage clarity, agent-mediated logistics | Unrepeatable authenticity, faces the audience has never seen, texture that reads as truth, sometimes a genuine skill (a real welder welding) |
| **Costs you** | The audience may recognise the face or the type; performance can read as performance; higher fee and quote structure | Slower days, fewer usable takes, dialogue is often unusable, availability chaos, no agent to solve problems, consent and rights must be handled from scratch, wardrobe and continuity risk |
| **Right when** | Dialogue-driven, multi-scene, emotionally graduated, tight schedule, complex blocking, heavy usage terms | Face-driven, near-silent or few words, single-scene, documentary register, period texture, crowd worlds, any role where recognisability would break belief |
| **Mitigation** | Cast against type; cast unfamiliar working actors over familiar ones | Reduce their dialogue; shoot more coverage; pair them with a professional who can carry the scene's spine; schedule them early in the day |

**[craft principle]** The hybrid is usually correct and is what the current award-winning work actually does. Kulukundis pairing non-actors with Oscar winners, Maisler finding a non-actor lead but surrounding him with professionals, Venditti sourcing 150 real faces around a professional core. The rule of thumb: **the more a role has to say, the more professional it should be. The more a role has to simply be true, the more real it can be.**

## A8. Ensemble Casting and the Load-Bearing Math

Every cast is a system. Changing one element changes what every other element has to do.

Sarah Finn gives the clearest working statement of this. She has described thinking about ensemble in relational terms, asking "How do these characters interact? How do they relate to each other?" And she describes a specific structural consequence discovered on the first *Iron Man*: because Robert Downey Jr. was likely to turn to anyone at any moment and improvise something funny, they changed their approach so that **anyone around him in a film is an actor, not an extra**. That is the single most useful ensemble principle in this document. The lead's working method determines the minimum capability of everyone in frame with them.

### The four tiers, defined by what they must be able to do

| Tier | Definition | Must be able to | Casting spec depth |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lead** | Carries the through-line. The audience tracks their state across the whole piece. | Take direction, sustain across takes, change register, carry dialogue, hold a close-up in silence | Full: history, register, contrast against other leads, chemistry-tested, deal and usage negotiated |
| **Featured player** | Named, seen clearly, has lines or a decisive action. The audience registers them as a person. | Deliver lines credibly, hit a mark, hold one clear intention, survive a close-up | Substantial: type-or-person decision, one-line interior life, wardrobe and framing implications noted |
| **Light-touch featured player** | Named or clearly seen but with a single beat: a glance, a reaction, a handover, one word. | Be interesting in one frame, take a simple redirect, not pull focus | Focused: physical read, energy, what the face must communicate in the one beat, whether they speak |
| **Background extra** | Populates the world. Not intended to be individually registered. | Be plausible in the world, repeat an action, not look at camera | Group-level: world description, count, age and type distribution, wardrobe register |

### How a supporting cast changes what the lead carries

**[craft principle]** A strong supporting cast reduces what the lead must explain. If the colleague is genuinely funny, the lead does not have to be funny and can be the still point. If the antagonist is genuinely threatening, the lead does not have to perform fear. If everyone around the lead is flat, the lead has to generate the entire tonal weather of the piece alone, which forces bigger performance, which reads as acting.

Practical consequence: **cast the supporting roles against the lead, not alongside them.** If the lead is warm, cast at least one cold. If the lead is fast, cast one slow. Sameness in an ensemble is the most common and least visible casting error, because each individual choice looks correct in isolation.

The tier boundary is not fixed by the script; it is fixed by the **frame**. A character written as background who receives a face-forward close-up has been promoted to featured player by the camera, regardless of what the script says. This is the single most important cross-department dependency in casting and is covered again in B3 and B7.

## A9. Age, Gender and Physical Attribute Decisions

Every attribute decision is a load-bearing wall. Changing one surfaces assumptions that were never written down.

Consider a role written as "father, 40s." Change it to "father, 60s" and the following silently break:

- **Wardrobe**: the register that read as contemporary now reads as either dated or deliberately classic, and the plan has to choose.
- **Makeup and hair**: a grooming plan built for one age either does nothing or does too much.
- **The relationship to the other character**: a 60s father with a 20s child is a normal family; a 60s father with a 40s child changes the whole scene's stakes.
- **The location**: a home dressed for a 40-year-old is now wrong.
- **Physical action**: whatever the shot list has them doing may now be implausible or may now be a story point.
- **The DP's framing**: lens choice and lighting decisions made for one skin and one face structure will not flatter another.
- **The brand strategy**: if this is advertising, the segment relationship (A6.1) may have flipped from mirror to authority without anyone deciding it should.

The same cascade runs for a gender change, a height change, a body-type change, an accent change or an ethnicity change. **[craft principle]** The rule is: **an attribute change is never a find-and-replace.** Every attribute change requires a re-read of the wardrobe plan, the makeup plan, the location, the props, the dialogue, and the relational geometry of the scene.

There is a positive version of this too. Deliberately changing one attribute is the fastest available diagnostic for finding lazy assumptions in a production document. If changing the gender of a role breaks the scene, the scene was relying on an unexamined stereotype and now you know where it is.

## A11. Vocabulary Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Breakdown** | The written character specification distributed to agents and platforms. The casting director's primary authored document. |
| **Breakdown Services / Actors Access / Spotlight** | The dominant distribution platforms for breakdowns and submissions (the first two North American, Spotlight the UK equivalent). |
| **Sides** | The extracted scene pages an actor reads from for audition. |
| **Self-tape** | An audition recorded by the actor to the casting director's written specification and uploaded. Now the default first round. |
| **Slate** | The on-camera introduction at the head of a tape: name, agency, role, sometimes height and a full-body turn. |
| **Redirect** | A note given mid-audition to test whether the actor can change what they are doing. The real content of most callbacks. |
| **Callback** | Second-round audition, usually with the director present. |
| **Chemistry read** | Callback-stage session pairing candidates to test the relationship rather than the individual performance. |
| **Avail / avail check** | Non-binding request that a performer hold a shoot date. |
| **Pin** | Signal that a performer is one of the final two or three. |
| **First refusal** | Commercial-side commitment that the performer will contact casting before accepting a conflicting booking on the same dates. |
| **Hold** | The strong form of a date commitment. Constrained by union rules, which is why "pin" and "avail" exist as substitutes. |
| **Session fee** | The commercial day rate, paid per eight-hour day worked or per commercial filmed, whichever is greater. |
| **Use fee / usage** | Payment for the commercial's actual airing and placement. Session fees are usually applicable against use fees. |
| **Holding fee** | A fee paid to hold a performer exclusive and prevent them appearing for competing products. Not automatic: there is no automatic exclusivity and no automatic right to non-consecutive use cycles. |
| **Buyout** | A single negotiated payment covering defined usage for a defined term and territory instead of recurring use fees. The dominant structure outside strict union markets. |
| **Conflicts** | The category of competing products a performer is barred from appearing for. The reason a performer may be unable to accept a job. |
| **Exclusivity** | Beyond the holding-fee level, additional fees can block a performer from appearing for other non-competitive products. |
| **MPU (Maximum Period of Use)** | The period during which a commercial may be used. Under the SAG-AFTRA Commercials Contract this is 24 months and is the same for every performer in the spot. |
| **Principal performer** | A performer whose work is individually identifiable and who is paid use fees. |
| **Extra performer / background** | A performer populating the world. Under the SAG-AFTRA Commercials Contract, extra performers do not receive residuals, because they are not held in exclusivity for the product. |
| **Featured extra** | A background performer who is individually visible enough to be registered by the audience. **[craft principle]** The moment the camera treats a background performer this way, the economics and the casting spec both change. |
| **Day player** | A performer engaged for one or a few days, typically a small speaking role. |
| **Quote** | The fee a performer was last paid, used as the anchor in negotiation. |
| **Open call** | A public audition open to anyone, not agency-filtered. The channel that found young Forrest Gump. |
| **Street casting** | Sourcing non-professionals directly from the world. |
| **Taft-Hartley** | The US mechanism by which a non-union performer can be engaged on a union job, used constantly in real-people casting. |
| **Equity / SAG-AFTRA** | The performer unions (UK theatre and US screen respectively) whose agreements govern rates, terms and exclusivity. |

---

## A12. How Commercial Casting Differs From Feature Casting

| Dimension | Feature | Commercial |
|---|---|---|
| **Decision chain** | Director decides, sometimes with studio input | Director recommends, agency creatives approve, client approves, and any layer can veto late |
| **What is being bought** | A performance | A performance **and** a defined set of usage rights across term, territory and media |
| **Economics** | Fee plus, at high levels, backend | Session fee plus use fees or a negotiated buyout; holding fees to preserve exclusivity |
| **Exclusivity** | Rarely an issue | Central. Conflicts can disqualify the best performer entirely, and breach of exclusivity by a principal is a serious contractual matter with real damages |
| **Term** | Perpetual | Bounded. Under the SAG-AFTRA Commercials Contract the MPU is 24 months and is uniform across the spot's performers |
| **Volume funnel** | Deep on few roles | Wide and fast: thousands of submissions narrowed to dozens of reads per role, per Ross Lacy's account of commercial practice |
| **Timeline** | Weeks to months | Days. Breakdown Monday, sessions midweek, callback, client presentation next day, book same week |
| **Dialogue load** | High | Often near zero, which is why real-people casting is far more viable |
| **What the audience brings** | Willingness to invest 100 minutes | Two seconds of attention and active resistance |
| **Recognisability** | An asset | Can be a liability if the face is associated with a competing brand or a prior campaign |
| **Reusability of the cast** | Irrelevant | Central: if the campaign may extend, cast people you can re-book, and negotiate options up front |

**[craft principle]** The commercial-specific consequence that trips up feature-trained thinkers: **the best available performer is sometimes uncastable**, not for talent reasons but for conflicts, quote, term or client-relationship reasons. Casting must therefore always run a live second and third choice, and must clear conflicts before, not after, presenting to the client.

# PART B: AGENT OPERATING MANUAL

## B1. Intake: The Exact Questions to Ask

When handed a script, treatment and shot list, ask these before writing a single breakdown. Do not proceed with silent assumptions; state any assumption you are forced to make.

**Scope and enumeration**
1. Give me the complete list of every human being who will appear on camera, including hands, backs of heads, reflections and anyone in a crowd who receives an individual shot.
2. Which of these are named in the shot list? Every named entity requires a spec.
3. What is the total headcount by tier: lead, featured, light-touch featured, background?

**Story and register**
4. What is the tonal register: naturalistic, heightened, comedic, documentary, mythic? This sets the acting style, which sets the pool.
5. For each role, what is the one thing the audience must believe about this person within their first frame?
6. Which relationships are load-bearing, meaning the piece fails if the chemistry is wrong?

**Advertising-specific**
7. Who is the target segment, and for each on-screen role, what is its **relationship** to that segment: mirror, aspiration, authority, beneficiary, foil, or absent?
8. Is the strategy relatability or aspiration? If the brief says both, force a choice.
9. Is there an existing brand cast, a returning character, or a campaign continuity requirement?
10. What is the usage: term, territory, media, exclusivity, conflict categories? What is the buyout structure?
11. May this campaign extend? If so, negotiate options now.
12. How many approval layers, and who has final veto?

**Production constraints**
13. What language(s) and accent(s)? Is dialogue synced, dubbed, or VO-over-picture?
14. Any minors, animals, stunts, nudity, driving, swimming, product handling or special skills?
15. What is the shoot schedule and which days does each role work?
16. What is the cast budget, split by tier?
17. Union or non-union, and in which territory?

**Frame reality**
18. From the DP and the shot list: for each role, how much of the person is actually seen, and in what shot size?
19. Are there any close-ups on people not currently specified as featured?

## B2. Decision Checklist in Dependency Order

Work strictly downward. Each step depends on the ones above it.

1. **Enumerate.** Extract every on-camera human from script, treatment and shot list. Produce the complete list before any judgement.
2. **Tier.** Assign each to lead, featured, light-touch featured, or background, using **frame size from the shot list**, not script prominence.
3. **Cross-check tiering against the shot list.** Any entity receiving a close-up or a face-forward frame is at minimum a light-touch featured player. Promote and flag.
4. **Set the segment relationship** (advertising only) for every on-screen role.
5. **Decide type versus person** for each role, and write the reason.
6. **Decide professional versus real** for each role, driven by dialogue load and repeatability demand.
7. **Design the ensemble against the lead**, checking deliberately for sameness across the supporting cast.
8. **Write the breakdowns.** Role, not actor. Range on age, precision on behaviour. Disclose all non-standard requirements. Include usage terms on commercials.
9. **Clear conflicts and exclusivity** before anyone is presented.
10. **Set the search channel per role**: agency submission, open call, street scout, specialist community, or targeted offer.
11. **Design the audition**: which sides, what redirect will be given, what the self-tape spec is.
12. **Run round one.** Cut and order the tapes as an authored shortlist, three to five per role with a written argument each.
13. **Callback with the director.** Test direction-taking, not repetition.
14. **Chemistry-test every load-bearing relationship**, using contrasting pairings.
15. **Present to the approval chain** with genuinely different options and a documented second choice per role.
16. **Pin, avail, or first-refusal** the finalists. Confirm real availability against the 1st AD's schedule.
17. **Book**, contract, and confirm usage, conflicts and options in writing.
18. **Hand off** to Wardrobe, Hair and Makeup, the 1st AD and Production with sizes, measurements, contact, call-time constraints and any physical notes.
19. **Hold the second choice live** until the shoot day is complete.

## B3. What Casting Needs From Other Departments Before It Can Lock

Casting cannot lock a single role without these. Requesting them is not optional; proceeding without them is the primary source of casting failure.

| From | What is needed | Why it blocks |
|---|---|---|
| **Director** | Tonal register, acting style, whether performance is naturalistic or heightened, whether improvisation is expected | Improvisation-heavy direction forces everyone in frame to be an actor, not an extra (the Finn rule). Register determines the entire pool. |
| **Director** | Which relationships are load-bearing | Determines what gets chemistry-tested |
| **DP** | Shot sizes per character, lens plan, how much of each person is actually in frame, whether faces are lit or silhouetted | Determines tier. A background performer with a close-up is a featured player. Determines how much specification each role needs. |
| **DP** | Skin-tone and lighting approach | Casting must not deliver a face the lighting plan cannot serve |
| **1st AD** | Shoot dates, day-by-day scene order, which roles work which days, turnaround and minors' hour limits | Determines availability, holds, and whether a preferred candidate is castable at all |
| **Wardrobe** | The costume plan, silhouette intent, whether costume is period, sizing latitude, whether anything is pre-built | Body type and size are casting constraints when garments exist already. Period silhouette constrains physique. |
| **Hair and Makeup** | The grooming plan, whether prosthetics, ageing, hair change, tattoo cover or facial hair is planned; lead time per look | Determines whether a physical attribute must be cast or can be created. Also sets earliest call time, which constrains who can work. |
| **Production Design / Locations** | The world of each location and who plausibly populates it | A location change creates a new population of characters that must be cast |
| **Producer / Line Producer** | Cast budget by tier, union status, territory, travel and accommodation allowance | Determines the achievable pool and whether out-of-market casting is possible |
| **Agency / Client** (advertising) | Segment definition, strategy pole (relatability vs aspiration), usage terms, conflict categories, approval chain, campaign extension intent | Determines the segment relationship, the deal, and who can legally accept |
| **Writer / Director** | Whether dialogue can be trimmed for a role | Enables real-people casting on roles that currently speak |

## B4. What Casting Owes Other Departments

| To | What is owed | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Director** | Shortlist per role with written argument, tapes ordered and cut, chemistry-test results, a clear recommendation with a stated second choice | Before callback and before every approval gate |
| **Wardrobe** | Full sizing and measurements, body notes, any physical constraint, confirmed as soon as booked | Immediately on booking, never later |
| **Hair and Makeup** | Current hair length and colour, facial hair, skin notes, tattoos and piercings, allergies, and whether the performer will agree to a change | Immediately on booking |
| **1st AD** | Confirmed availability, travel needs, minors' guardian and permit status, any hour limitation, agent contact | Before the schedule is published |
| **DP** | Advance notice of skin tone, height differentials within a pairing, eyeline problems, glasses or reflective jewellery | Before lighting prep |
| **Production Design / Props** | Any handedness, physical capability or size issue that affects props or set practicals | Before prep locks |
| **Producer** | Deal terms, quotes, usage, conflicts, options, and the total cast cost against budget | Before every booking |
| **Editor** | Notes on which performers give a consistent versus a variable performance, and which non-actors have limited usable takes | At wrap, with the cast list |
| **Everyone** | The complete cast list with tier labels, so no department discovers a face on the day | At lock |

## B5. The Per-Character Enumeration Discipline

**This is the non-negotiable core of the agent's behaviour.**

Rule: **every character named in the shot list must have a casting spec. No exceptions, including characters seen for a fraction of a second.**

If the shot list names an entity ("the vendor," "the child at the window," "the man who hands over the keys"), that entity is a casting decision. If it has no spec, the decision has been made by accident, on the day, by whoever is nearest.

Rule: **specification depth is proportional to screen time and frame size, but specification presence is not.** A one-frame character still needs a spec. It is just a short one.

| Tier | Minimum spec contents |
|---|---|
| **Lead** | Age range, gender, physical read, interior life in three sentences, register, dialogue and skill demands, segment relationship (advertising), type-or-person decision, professional-or-real decision, chemistry pairings to test, usage and conflicts, wardrobe and makeup implications, second choice |
| **Featured player** | Age range, gender, physical read, one-sentence interior life, register, dialogue demand, type-or-person decision, professional-or-real decision, wardrobe implication, second choice |
| **Light-touch featured player** | Age range, gender, physical read, the single thing the face must communicate in its one beat, whether they speak, whether they are professional or real |
| **Background extra** | Group-level: world description, headcount, age and gender distribution, wardrobe register, any individual who must be castable to featured standard because of the DP's plan |

Rule: **if the shot list and the character list disagree, the shot list wins and you raise the discrepancy.** The camera decides who is in the film.

Rule: **write the reason, not just the answer.** A spec that says "male, 30s, warm" is unfalsifiable. A spec that says "male, 30s, warm, because the lead is cold and the scene has no other source of warmth" can be argued with, which means it can be improved.

## B6. Standard Tensions and Typical Director Resolutions

| Tension | The conflict | Typical resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Casting vs Director** | Casting has found the more interesting person; the director wants the more familiar one | The director decides. Casting's obligation is to make the argument once, in writing, with the tape cut to show it. Dougherty's Voight advocacy is the canonical version of making the argument and winning. |
| **Casting vs Client** (advertising) | Client wants the safe, conventionally attractive, on-segment choice; casting and director want the specific person | Present three genuinely different options rather than three variants. Frame the specific choice in strategy language (which pole, relatability or aspiration, the campaign chose) rather than in taste language. If the client vetoes, the client wins, and casting logs the second choice. |
| **Casting vs Wardrobe** | Costumes are already built or a period silhouette constrains the body; casting has found the right performer in the wrong body | Whoever committed first wins on cost grounds. Escalate early, because a garment can sometimes be remade and a face cannot. |
| **Casting vs Hair and Makeup** | Should a physical attribute be cast or created | Default to casting it if it is structural (bone, height, body) and creating it if it is surface (hair, ageing, colour, facial hair). Check lead time: an elaborate created look changes the earliest call time and therefore who can work. |
| **Casting vs DP** | The DP's shot list promotes a background performer to a close-up | Casting must be told before the shoot, and the role is re-cast to featured standard. This is the most common and most expensive silent failure in the department. |
| **Casting vs 1st AD** | The preferred performer is unavailable on a scheduled day | The schedule usually wins on cost grounds, but flag early: a one-day scene move is often cheaper than the second-choice performance. |
| **Casting vs Producer** | The right performer is over budget or has a conflicting quote | Producer decides. Casting's obligation is to state precisely what is lost, in performance terms, by going to the second choice. |
| **Casting vs Writer** | A role written with dialogue would be better cast with a non-actor | Ask for the line to be cut or moved to another character. Very often the answer is yes, and the scene improves. |
| **Casting vs Casting** | Every individual choice is good but the ensemble is homogeneous | Casting owns this one. Re-derive the supporting cast against the lead, deliberately introducing contrast. |

## B7. Failure Modes and Under-Specification Tells

Scan for these before declaring any casting plan complete. Each one is a diagnostic that something upstream is unresolved.

1. **A character named in the shot list with no casting spec.** The most serious tell. Someone will be cast by default, on the day, badly.
2. **A "background extra" who receives a face-forward shot, a close-up, or a reaction cut.** The camera has promoted them and the paperwork has not caught up. This produces a non-performer holding a close-up.
3. **A crowd or ensemble described only by count** ("twelve people in the office"), with no world description, age distribution or wardrobe register. This produces a crowd that all look like the same person.
4. **The word "normal," "regular," "everyday" or "average" in a breakdown.** These are not specifications. They mean the person writing has not decided.
5. **The target segment restated as a casting spec** ("women 25 to 34, urban"). The segment is who is watching. The spec is who is on screen. See A6.1.
6. **Relatability and aspiration both requested for the same role.** Force a choice.
7. **Every supporting character sharing the lead's energy.** Nobody notices this until the edit, when the whole piece reads as one note.
8. **A role with dialogue slated for real-people casting.** Either cut the line or cast a professional.
9. **An attribute changed by find-and-replace**, with no corresponding re-read of wardrobe, makeup, location, props and relational geometry. See A9.
10. **No documented second choice** on an advertising job. Late client reversal is normal, not exceptional.
11. **Conflicts and exclusivity not cleared before client presentation.** The client falls in love with someone who cannot legally accept.
12. **Usage terms absent from the breakdown.** Performers accept and then renegotiate, or refuse late.
13. **A chemistry-critical relationship never tested**, because both candidates tested well alone.
14. **Casting locked before the DP's shot sizes are known.** Tiering is then guesswork.
15. **Minors, animals, stunts or special skills discovered after booking.** Every one of these has a permit, insurance or scheduling consequence.
16. **A "hero hands" or "product handling" role with no spec.** Hands are cast. So are backs of heads, feet, and voices.
17. **No stated reason attached to any choice.** An unargued cast list cannot be defended to a client or improved by a director.

## B9. Trigger List: What Forces a Re-Open

Casting is downstream of many decisions and upstream of several. The following changes require the plan to be re-opened. For each, the table separates what must be **genuinely re-derived** (thought through again from first principles) from what is merely **relabelled** (a documentation update).

| Upstream change | Genuinely re-derive | Merely relabel |
|---|---|---|
| **An ensemble is added** (a group appears where there was one person or none) | The whole ensemble contrast check against the lead. What the lead now has to carry, which usually decreases. Tiering of every new member. Whether any new member gets a frame that makes them featured. Budget by tier. | Headcount totals, call sheets, the enumeration table |
| **An age change on any role** | Wardrobe register, makeup and hair plan, location plausibility, relational geometry with every other character, segment relationship if advertising, physical action feasibility, the pool and search channel | The number in the breakdown |
| **A gender change on any role** | Every assumption the scene was silently making about that character. Dialogue that now reads differently. Relational geometry. Wardrobe from scratch. Whether the change creates or removes a sameness problem in the ensemble | Pronouns, name |
| **A framing rule change that puts previously-unseen faces on camera** (wider lenses, longer takes, a switch from insert-heavy to master-heavy coverage) | Tiering of every affected background performer. Whether the background world was specified at all. Whether the crowd can survive being seen. Budget, because featured performers cost more than background. Possibly the entire background sourcing approach | Nothing. This is a full re-open of the background plan and is the most commonly under-reacted-to trigger |
| **A location change adding a new world** | The population of that world from scratch: who plausibly exists there, at what ages, in what dress, in what numbers. New specs for any named entity in the new location | Location name in existing specs |
| **The director changes** | Tonal register, acting style, whether improvisation is now expected (which may promote every extra to actor), the shortlist arguments, possibly the entire pool | Contact details |
| **The strategy pole flips** (relatability to aspiration or the reverse) | Every segment relationship. Age, grooming, attractiveness register, accent, and how "finished" each person should look. Frequently the whole cast | The strategy line in the deck |
| **Usage, term or territory changes** | Who can legally accept. Conflicts clearance for every booked and shortlisted performer. Deal structure and total cast cost. Possibly the entire shortlist | Contract paperwork, if nobody's conflicts are affected |
| **A conflict is discovered on a booked performer** | That role, from the shortlist forward. Verify the second choice is still available and still conflict-clear | Nothing else, if the second choice holds |
| **The schedule moves** | Availability of every booked and pinned performer. Minors' hour limits. Whether the preferred performer is now castable or now lost | Dates on the cast list |
| **Wardrobe locks a built costume before casting** | Body-type constraints on the affected role, and therefore the pool | Sizing notes |
| **A dialogue line is added to a real-people role** | Professional-versus-real decision for that role. Very likely a re-cast | Nothing. Do not paper over this one |
| **Budget is cut** | Tier allocation: what moves from featured to light-touch, from professional to real, from cast to not-seen. What the lead must now carry alone as a consequence | Budget spreadsheet |

**[craft principle]** The general rule for re-opens: **anything that changes what the camera sees, or what the lead has to carry, is a genuine re-derivation. Anything that changes only a label is a relabel.** When in doubt, treat it as a re-derivation, because the cost of re-thinking a cast list is a day and the cost of a wrong face is the whole piece.
