# FILM CRAFT BIBLE 08: VO CASTING AND VOICE DIRECTION

**Role:** Voice-Over Casting Director / VO Director / Voice Director
**Scope:** Commercials, brand films, documentaries, explainers, social cutdowns, animation, trailers, corporate and institutional film, AI-generated video pipelines
**Status:** Reusable craft reference. Contains no client-specific or project-specific material.

**Sourcing convention used throughout this document:**
- **[SOURCED]** = attributable to a named practitioner, a published interview, a union agreement, an industry body, or published trade reporting.
- **[PRINCIPLE]** = general craft consensus, teachable and defensible, but not tied to one named source.

# PART A: CRAFT REFERENCE

## A1. What The Role Actually Is

There are two jobs bolted together, and most productions fail because they staff one and assume the other.

**Voice casting** is the search. It is the process of converting a written brief into a shortlist of specific human beings whose instruments and instincts can carry the copy. It is a matching problem with a very large candidate pool and very poor search terms.

**Voice direction** is the extraction. Once the performer is in the booth, it is the process of getting a specific read out of them, take by take, adjustment by adjustment, and then knowing which take is the one.

Andrea Romano, who spent more than three decades voice directing and casting animation, illustrates the scale of the casting half. She was known for taking her time on a single part, in some cases considering more than two hundred voices for one role, and over her career she directed more than ten thousand voice sessions across more than 1,600 television episodes, over fifty films, and around twenty games before retiring in 2017. **[SOURCED]** She also held a strong process conviction on the direction half: she preferred to record actors together in the same room rather than isolated, on the reasoning that a major part of acting is reacting, and she made a point of performing the read herself first so the cast could see that it was permissible to go that big. **[SOURCED]**

That is the honest job description. Not "find a nice voice." Narrow a very large field down to one instrument, then conduct a live performance that nobody else on the crew is qualified to conduct.

## A2. What The Role Owns, And Where Authority Stops

### Owns outright

| Owned | Meaning |
|---|---|
| The casting shortlist | Which voices are auditioned at all, and in what order they are presented |
| Read register | Announcer, conversational, interior, wry, documentary, and the blend between them |
| The direction in the booth | Every adjustment given to the performer, take by take |
| Take selection | Which take, and which portion of which take, is the selected read |
| Line-level phrasing | Emphasis, stress placement, where a sentence turns |
| Pace and breath architecture | How fast, and where the read breathes |
| Session structure | Warm-up, order of blocks, when to move on, when to go back |
| The pickup list | What needs to be re-recorded and why |

### Does not own

| Not owned | Owner |
|---|---|
| The words | Writer, then Director, then client. VO does not rewrite copy unilaterally. |
| Final runtime | Editor and Director |
| The mix, EQ, compression, final processing | Sound department |
| Whether VO exists at all in the piece | Director, at concept stage |
| Approval of the final voice | Director, then client |
| On-screen cast decisions | Casting Director for picture, and Director |
| Usage, buyout terms, and legal clearance | Producer and business affairs |

The boundary that matters most in practice: **the VO casting director owns the read, but not the runtime, and not the words.** The moment the read requires more seconds than the edit has, or requires different words than the script has, it stops being a VO decision and becomes a Director decision. **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A3. The Decision Set

Every VO brief resolves into the same ordered set of decisions. Under-specification anywhere in this list produces a casting session that wastes a day.

| Decision | The real question | Failure if left blank |
|---|---|---|
| **Function** | Is this voice a narrator, a character's interior voice, or a brand voice? | The performer does not know who they are, so they default to selling |
| **Person / POV** | First, second, or third person? | Everything downstream is guesswork |
| **Gender** | Which, and is it contrasting with the on-screen cast deliberately? | Defaults to whatever the writer heard in their head |
| **Age band** | Perceived age, not actual age | Casting the wrong life-experience weight behind the words |
| **Accent / dialect** | Which region, which social register, how strong | Wrong belonging signal; audience hears "not from here" |
| **Timbre** | Weight, rasp, brightness, breathiness, resonance | Casting on adjectives instead of on sound |
| **Pace** | Words per minute, and how much of the runtime the VO occupies | Editor and VO fight later, in the mix |
| **Read register** | Announcer, conversational, interior, wry, documentary | The single most common under-specification |
| **Language(s)** | One, or several, and which is primary | Translated scripts and mismatched casts |
| **Usage** | Where it runs, for how long, in which territories | Legal exposure and budget explosion |

### Read register in detail

This is the axis with the most craft literature and the least discipline in practice.

**The announcer read.** Polished, projected, authoritative, hard-sell. The delivery that dominated broadcast advertising for roughly forty years. Don LaFontaine is the definitive figure. He narrated over 5,000 film trailers and hundreds of thousands of television commercials over four decades, and his "In a world..." construction became so ubiquitous that it defined an entire promotional grammar for action and blockbuster material through the 1980s and 1990s. **[SOURCED]** LaFontaine died in 2008, and the trade observation since is stark: film trailers have almost entirely stopped using narrators at all. **[SOURCED]**

Why the announcer read collapsed is worth understanding, because it explains the whole current market. Modern audiences consume conversational speech all day through podcasts, livestreams, and creator video, so a theatrical announcer read now reads as disconnected from how people actually talk. Brands shifted toward trust, familiarity, and social realism rather than energy and volume. **[SOURCED]** The announcer read is not dead, but it is now a niche rather than a norm. It survives in sports, in some automotive retail advertising, and in deliberate pastiche where the point is that the audience recognises the convention. **[SOURCED]**

**The conversational read.** Speaking naturally, as in everyday conversation, without fanfare or embellishment. **[SOURCED]** This is the default register of contemporary advertising. The internal risk is that "conversational" is itself an under-specification: a conversation between whom, about what, in what mood.

**The authentic or unpolished read.** The current end-state of the conversational shift. According to the 2025 State of Voice Over report published by Voices.com, 67% of clients now request "authentic" or "natural" as the primary descriptor for commercial narration projects, and casting specs routinely ask for the performer with the least amount of polish. **[SOURCED]** This is a real casting instruction with real consequences: it favours performers who can leave a breath ragged, land a word slightly off the beat, and resist the trained instinct to smooth. It also has a failure mode, which is affected artlessness, a polished performer performing unpolishedness.

**The interior read.** The voice inside a character's head. Quieter, closer to the mic, lower energy, more air, and crucially it is not addressed to the audience. It is overheard. This register almost always demands the tightest mic and the most silence around it. **[PRINCIPLE]**

**The wry read.** Dry, slightly amused, holding the copy at a distance. Useful when the copy would be unbearable if delivered sincerely. Werner Herzog is the reference point for the extreme end of this: a gravelly tone, a strong accent, and dry wit, delivering narration that questions and probes rather than simply informs. **[SOURCED]**

**The documentary read.** Authority earned through apparent restraint. The traditional form is "voice of God," an omniscient unseen narrator providing context and cohesion. **[SOURCED]** The modern refinement moves away from omniscience toward presence. David Attenborough is the standard example: a gentle intimacy and a soft but excitable delivery that makes the audience feel they are standing beside the narrator observing, rather than being lectured from above. **[SOURCED]** The craft consensus across documentary narration writing is that the paramount trait is not timbre at all, but an audible and believable connection between the narrator and the material. **[SOURCED]**

## A4. The Grammatical Question That Determines Everything

Before gender, before age, before timbre: **what person is the script written in?**

This is not a stylistic footnote. It determines whether the voice can belong to an on-screen character at all.

**First person.** "I walked in and the room was already cold." The voice is a specific person with a specific history. If anyone is on screen, the audience will immediately try to attach the voice to a body. If the attachment is ambiguous, the audience spends the film solving a puzzle instead of watching. First person copy therefore either commits to a visible speaker or commits to keeping that speaker entirely off screen or unidentifiable.

**Second person.** "You walk in. The room is already cold." The voice addresses the viewer directly. This is the dominant mode in advertising copy for a well-documented reason: second person draws the reader in, makes the voice conversational, and lets the audience place themselves inside the scenario. The common copywriting guideline is that second person pronouns should carry the substantial majority of the copy, with first person used sparingly and mainly to establish authority or personality. **[SOURCED]**

The under-appreciated consequence is structural, not persuasive. **Second person copy deliberately frees the VO from being any on-screen character's voice.** If the copy says "you," the voice is not the person in frame, it is the person talking to the person in frame, and by extension to the viewer. That single grammatical choice dissolves the entire lip-sync problem, dissolves the gender-matching problem, and legitimises casting a voice that contrasts sharply with everyone on screen. Second person is the cheapest structural insurance policy available to a VO-driven film. **[PRINCIPLE]**

**Third person.** "He walks in. The room is already cold." The voice is outside the world, observing. This is documentary and fable register. It carries the highest authority and the highest coldness risk, and it is the register most likely to slip back toward announcer if the performer is not directed away from it.

**The operating rule:** if the person and POV of the script were never explicitly decided in writing, the VO brief is not ready to cast against. Ask before shortlisting, not after. **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A5. Lip-Sync As A Structural Production Decision

There is a rule that experienced VO-heavy productions apply almost reflexively: **never show the mouth of whoever is speaking the voice-over.**

This is commonly mistaken for a stylistic preference. It is not. It is a cost-and-risk rule, and it has three independent justifications.

**1. Sync is the most expensive form of continuity.** The moment a visible mouth is producing the VO words, every subsequent script change becomes a reshoot or an ADR problem rather than a booth pickup. If the mouth is not visible, a copy revision at the eleventh hour costs one hour of booth time. If the mouth is visible, the same revision costs a shoot day or an ADR session with visible sync risk. **[PRINCIPLE]**

**2. Casting becomes reversible.** With no visible mouth, the voice can be recast, re-aged, re-gendered, or re-languaged after picture is locked. With a visible mouth, the voice is welded to the face at the moment of photography.

**3. In AI-generated pipelines the rule hardens from advisable to near-mandatory.** Generative video models have converged on native audio only recently. Some current-generation models produce lip-synced dialogue and ambient audio in a single pass, but a large share of generated footage is still silent or produces audio whose mouth movement does not sync reliably, which is why an entire category of third-party lip-sync tools exists specifically to bolt synced mouths onto footage generated elsewhere. **[SOURCED]** Each of those bolt-on passes is another generation, another cost, another artifact risk, and another quality-control gate. A production that keeps every speaking mouth out of frame, or shows mouths only for non-speaking characters, removes that entire failure surface at zero cost.

**Practical implementations that satisfy the rule:** back of head, over the shoulder, hands and objects, walking away, faces turned to the light, mouths obscured by frame edge or foreground, wide shots where the mouth is too small to read, and the very common solution of simply never being on a face when a line lands. **[PRINCIPLE]**

**The corollary the VO casting director must state out loud:** if the Director wants a speaking on-screen character, that is not a voice-over. That is dialogue, it is a picture-casting decision, and it must be budgeted, scheduled, and cleared as such. Conflating the two is one of the most common and most expensive briefing errors in short-form work. **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A6. Breathing Room, Pacing, And The War With The Editor

A read is not words. A read is words and the silence around them.

**The arithmetic.** Conversational English narration lands roughly in the 140 to 170 words per minute band. An urgent retail read pushes past 180. An interior or documentary read sits at 110 to 130 and sometimes lower. The same script therefore has a runtime range of nearly two to one depending only on register. **[PRINCIPLE]** Any script handed to VO without a target duration is handing the VO casting director an unstated decision that belongs to the Director.

**The full-runtime problem.** When VO covers the entire runtime with no gaps, the edit loses its freedom. Every cut must now respect a sentence boundary, because cutting picture across a word inside a phrase creates an audible mismatch between the image rhythm and the speech rhythm. A wall-to-wall VO effectively converts the edit from a picture-led cut into an audio-led cut. That is a legitimate creative choice, but it must be a choice, not an accident. **[PRINCIPLE]**

**The structural tension, stated plainly.** The Editor wants frames. Every second of silence is a second of screen time that must be filled with something worth watching, and silence exposes weak footage. The VO wants air. A read without breathing room becomes a list, and a list is the fastest route back to announcer register regardless of who is performing. These two wants are directly opposed and they do not resolve themselves. **[PRINCIPLE]**

**The typical resolutions a Director will choose between:**

| Resolution | What it costs | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Cut copy | The writer's favourite line | Almost always the correct answer |
| Extend runtime | Media budget, client format constraints | When the format genuinely allows it |
| Speed the read | Warmth, authority, comprehension | Retail and urgency-led work only |
| Let picture carry silence | Requires footage strong enough to hold | Premium and brand-film work |
| Overlap VO with a music or sound-design bed | Reduces perceived silence without stealing seconds | Very frequently the practical compromise |

**The rule of thumb worth writing down:** copy should be cut until the selected read fits at the intended pace with silence still present. If the read only fits when the performer is pushed, the script is too long. Pushing the performer is not a fix, it is a symptom. **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A7. Casting The Voice Against The On-Screen Cast

Casting the voice to match the on-screen cast is the safe move. Casting it against them is usually the more interesting one.

**Deliberate contrast devices:**

- **Gender contrast.** A female voice over a male-led scene, or the reverse, immediately signals that the voice is not inside the scene. It buys distance and commentary for free.
- **Age contrast.** An older voice over young protagonists reads as retrospection, memory, or inherited wisdom. A younger voice over older subjects reads as inheritance, succession, or challenge.
- **Register contrast.** A calm voice over chaotic picture, or a warm voice over cold picture, creates the tension the copy alone cannot.
- **Accent contrast.** Belonging or outsiderness, depending on which way the contrast runs.

**The diagnostic use of a VO recast.** This is worth its own paragraph because it is one of the most useful practical tricks in the discipline. **When you change a single VO attribute, such as gender, and something else in the production document suddenly stops making sense, you have just located an unstated assumption.** If flipping the narrator to a different gender breaks a line of copy, that copy was secretly first-person and tied to a character nobody declared. If it breaks a shot description, the shot was secretly a sync shot. If it breaks the music brief, the music was written against an assumption nobody wrote down. Running this substitution deliberately, as a test, is cheap and catches structural errors that survive every other review. **[PRINCIPLE]**

## A8. Direction Technique: Adjustments, Not Adjectives

The core craft skill of voice direction is getting a different read without describing the read.

**Why adjectives fail.** "Warmer." "More confident." "Less salesy." These are evaluations of the output, not instructions for the input. The performer receives them as a verdict on the take they just gave, and their most common response is to add colour on top of the same underlying choice, which produces a more decorated version of the wrong read. The professional standard on the performer side reinforces this: a strong alternate take means committing fully to a new point of view, not slightly adjusting the previous take. **[SOURCED]** The director's job is to supply the new point of view.

**What actually works. The adjustment ladder, in ascending order of intervention:**

| Level | Instruction type | Example form |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Who** | "You are telling this to one person, not a room." |
| 2 | **To whom** | "Say it to someone who already agrees with you." |
| 3 | **Circumstance** | "You have just walked in from the cold and you are still catching your breath." |
| 4 | **Relationship** | "You know something they do not know yet, and you are not going to say it." |
| 5 | **Physical** | "Sit back. Hands off the desk. Do it on the exhale." |
| 6 | **Mechanical** | "Same read, drop the stress off the third word, put it on the last." |
| 7 | **Demonstration** | Perform it yourself, then let them find their own version |

Level 7 is contentious and should be used sparingly, but there is strong precedent for it. Romano's stated practice was that she would never ask a performer to do something she would not do herself, and that she performed it first specifically to give permission. **[SOURCED]** The risk is producing an imitation instead of a performance, which is why the demonstration should be followed by an explicit "now do it your way."

**Suppressing an unwanted register through direction.** The most common unwanted register in commercial and corporate work is what can be called the founder-explaining-the-product read: slightly too invested, slightly too pleased, upward inflection at the end of every clause, and an audible awareness that the words are selling something. Casting alone will not remove it, because the copy itself induces it. Almost any performer handed product copy will drift there by default. **[PRINCIPLE]**

Direction that removes it:

- Give the performer a listener who does not need convincing. Most of the sell-tone comes from an imagined sceptic.
- Move the stakes off the product and onto the person. "You are not telling them about the thing. You are telling them about the day."
- Kill the final upward inflection explicitly. Ask for statements that land down.
- Take the energy down one full level below what feels correct, then one more. Rooms consistently over-read.
- Have the performer read the copy as if reporting something that already happened, past tense, then return to the script text carrying that attitude.
- Read it once as pure information, deliberately flat, then once again immediately after. The second one is often the take.

## A9. Bilingual And Multi-Language Work

The most common and most expensive error in multi-language VO is treating the second language as a delivery format for the first language's script.

**Why the translated-script approach fails.** Translation transfers meaning. Localisation adapts content so it feels native. Transcreation reshapes messaging creatively, preserving intent while adapting tone, style, and impact. Copywriting creates original content directly for the target audience. **[SOURCED]** Advertising VO copy sits at the transcreation-to-original-copywriting end of that spectrum, not the translation end, because the persuasive load is carried by rhythm, idiom, and cultural reference, none of which survive literal transfer. The professional standard in the localisation industry is that each writer should be a native speaker working in-market who understands current cultural usage, with the brand voice and core message held constant while the cultural execution changes. **[SOURCED]**

**The four practical consequences for VO casting:**

1. **Each language needs its own writing pass, by a native writer, against the same brief.** Not the same script rendered into another language.
2. **Each language needs its own casting pass.** Voice qualities do not transfer across languages. A timbre that reads as warm and trustworthy in one language may read as sleepy or juvenile in another, because the reference set of respected public voices in each language is different.
3. **Do not chase a matched voice across languages.** Attempting to cast a second-language performer whose voice sounds like the first-language performer optimises for a similarity nobody in either audience will ever perceive, at the cost of casting the best voice in each market. The exception is genuine dubbing where a single visible character must sound continuous.
4. **Timing will not match, so plan for it.** Language pairs have materially different syllable densities. The same brief will produce different word counts and different runtimes. Either the edit gets separate versions, or the copy in the longer language gets cut. Deciding this before the session, not after, is the difference between a pickup and a re-record. **[PRINCIPLE]**

For dialects within a language, the same logic applies at reduced scale. A regional dialect is a belonging signal, and getting it approximately right is worse than not attempting it, because approximation reads as parody to native ears.

## A11. Vocabulary Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Read** | A single interpretive approach to the copy. Distinct from a take. Two takes can share a read. |
| **Take** | One recorded attempt. Numbered, logged, and referenced by number. |
| **Adjustment** | An instruction given between takes to change the read. Should specify input, not evaluate output. |
| **Room tone** | Thirty to sixty seconds of the silent recording space, captured at the session. Used by Sound to fill gaps and match edits. Non-negotiable deliverable. |
| **ADR** | Automated Dialogue Replacement. Re-recording dialogue to picture, in sync, after the shoot. Distinct from VO, and far more expensive. |
| **Wild lines** | Lines recorded without reference to picture and without sync obligation. Cheap, flexible, and the reason the never-show-the-mouth rule pays off. |
| **Scratch track** | A temporary VO, often recorded by the Director or an available voice, cut into the offline so the edit can be built to real timing before the real session. |
| **Pickup session** | A short return session to re-record specific lines after a script change, a client note, or a technical fault. Budget for one by default. |
| **Buyout** | A single fee paid in exchange for extended or unlimited usage rights instead of recurring residual payments. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Usage** | Where the recording runs, in which media, in which territories, for how long. The primary driver of VO cost, above talent quality. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Conflict / exclusivity** | A restriction preventing the performer from voicing competing brands or categories during the usage period. Open-ended and perpetual buyouts can disqualify a performer from competing work years later because the old recording technically still exists. **[SOURCED]** |
| **In perpetuity** | Forever. Treated in the performer community as a red flag term, because it removes the performer's future ability to work in the category. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Walla** | Background crowd voice. A specialised craft, not ambient noise. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Loop group** | The standing ensemble of performers who record walla, background dialogue, and incidental lines. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Session fee** | The payment for the recording session itself, separate from any usage payment. Under the 2025 SAG-AFTRA commercials structure, off-camera voice-over session fees sit in roughly the 450 to 500 US dollar range, with pension and health contributions of approximately 19.1% payable on top. **[SOURCED]** |
| **Slate** | The spoken identification at the head of a take or an audition file. |
| **Plosive** | The mic-overloading burst on hard consonants. A technical fault, fixed with position and a pop filter, not with direction. |
| **Sibilance** | Excessive hiss on S sounds. Partly instrument, partly mic choice, partly a de-esser problem for Sound. |

## A12. Economics: Usage, Buyouts, And Why Voice Costs What It Costs

The single most misunderstood fact about VO budgeting: **the fee is mostly not for the performance.** It is for the licence.

In the union framework, the session fee covers the recording and the use fees cover the exposure, with residuals payable each time the commercial airs or each time a usage cycle renews. For a national campaign running a year, residuals routinely exceed the original session fee many times over. **[SOURCED]** SAG-AFTRA members ratified the 2025 Commercials Contract by 96.90% to 3.10%, a three-year deal reported as delivering 218.4 million US dollars in new earnings and benefit plan contributions, and it introduced a revised structure around maximum use and holding fees alongside streaming use fee increases. **[SOURCED]**

Outside the union framework, the equivalent lever is the buyout. A buyout is a one-time fee exchanged for extended or unlimited usage rights instead of recurring payments. **[SOURCED]** The negotiating discipline on both sides is the same: a buyout must be scoped by media, territory, and term, because the actual value being transferred is exposure, and exposure without a defined limit is unpriceable.

**What this means operationally for the person casting the voice:**

- Ask for the usage terms before the shortlist, not after. Usage determines which performers are affordable at all.
- Never present a shortlist that includes performers whose quotes cannot survive the actual usage.
- Flag exclusivity and conflict early. A performer already voicing a competing category may be unavailable regardless of budget. **[SOURCED]**
- Assume a pickup session. Copy changes.
- Broad, undefined, or perpetual usage language costs more, not less, because a professional performer prices the loss of future category work into it. **[SOURCED]**

## A13. AI And Synthetic Voice

### Where the technology actually is

Synthetic voice crossed the practical usability threshold for short-form commercial narration during 2025 and 2026. Trade assessments place current-generation synthetic commercial voiceover at roughly 80 to 90% of human quality for short formats under thirty seconds, with broadcast-acceptable output achievable across major languages and turnaround compressed to around a day. **[SOURCED]** The market has also formalised: a major synthetic voice provider launched a licensed marketplace for the voices of named public figures, including a living actor whose AI replica was used to announce the partnership on the stated reasoning that the goal was to amplify rather than replace human creativity. **[SOURCED]**

The honest reading of that number is that the remaining 10 to 20% is not evenly distributed. It concentrates precisely in the places that matter most for premium work: sustained emotional arc across a longer read, the deliberately imperfect breath, ironic distance, and the specific quality that documentary narration writing identifies as paramount, which is an audible and believable connection between the narrator and the material. **[SOURCED]** A synthetic voice can convincingly deliver information. It struggles to convincingly deliver conviction.

### Consent, cloning, and union position

The 2025 SAG-AFTRA commercials contracts were the first to set out detailed provisions for consent when using AI-generated digital replicas and synthetic performers. **[SOURCED]** The material points:

- **Digital replica** is defined as a digital simulation of a real individual's voice, image, or likeness that so closely resembles the real thing that an average person could not distinguish the two. **Synthetic performer** is defined as a digitally created asset intended to read as a human performer, not recognisable as any identifiable real human, not voiced by a human performer, and not a digital replica. **[SOURCED]**
- Consent for creating or using a digital replica must be **clear and conspicuous**, given in a **separate signed writing** (a rider is sufficient), and must include a reasonably specific description of both the generated performance and the anticipated use. **[SOURCED]**
- Minimum payments and residuals apply when a replica is used in new campaigns or media. **[SOURCED]**
- Compliance with the contract requirements is stated to satisfy current informed-consent obligations under state law in California, Illinois, and New York. **[SOURCED]**
- In most jurisdictions a person's voice is protected under personality rights independently of any union agreement. **[SOURCED]**

### The operating position for a production

**Appropriate uses of synthetic voice:**

- Scratch tracks and animatics, so the edit can be built to real timing before a booth session exists.
- Internal previsualisation, pitch material, and concept tests clearly labelled as such.
- High-volume, low-emotion, information-carrying narration: interface prompts, procedural explainers, personalised variants at a scale no human session could produce.
- Language variants for internal comprehension checks, not for broadcast.
- Licensed voices, used strictly within the terms of an explicit written licence.

**Inappropriate or high-risk uses:**

- Any voice that resembles an identifiable real person without a separate, specific, written consent. This is the single largest legal exposure in the entire discipline.
- Emotional core narration in premium brand work, where the remaining quality gap is exactly the thing being bought.
- Undisclosed synthetic voice in work whose whole persuasive proposition is authenticity. The reputational downside is asymmetric.
- Any use where the delivery contract, the client's own AI policy, or the applicable union agreement has not been checked first.

**The disclosure default:** if a piece uses synthetic voice, the production should be able to say so without embarrassment. If saying so would embarrass the production, that is the answer to whether it should have been used. **[PRINCIPLE]**

# PART B: AGENT OPERATING MANUAL

This half is written so an AI subagent can occupy the role and behave like a competent VO casting director rather than a voice-adjective generator.

## B1. The Exact Questions This Role Asks When Handed A Script

Ask these in order. Do not proceed past an unanswered question by inventing an answer. Where an answer must be assumed, state the assumption explicitly and mark it as an assumption.

**Function and grammar**
1. Is there a voice-over at all, or is this dialogue delivered by a visible character? If any speaking mouth will be in frame, this is not a VO brief.
2. Is the voice a narrator, a character's interior voice, or a brand voice?
3. What person is the copy written in: first, second, or third? Was this decided, or did it happen?
4. If first person, whose voice is it, and is that person visible at any point?
5. Who is the voice speaking to: the viewer, another character, or nobody in particular?

**Timing**
6. What is the locked runtime?
7. Is the script locked, or still moving?
8. What is the target word count, and what pace does that imply?
9. Does the VO run wall to wall, or is it in blocks with picture-only passages between?
10. Where in the timeline does the VO start, and does the piece end on words or on picture?

**Attributes**
11. Gender, and is it matching or contrasting the on-screen cast?
12. Perceived age band?
13. Accent, dialect, and how strong?
14. Read register, stated against a named reference, not an adjective?
15. What register must be actively avoided, and why?

**Language**
16. How many languages, and which is primary?
17. Was each language written natively, or translated?
18. Do all language versions share one edit, or does each get its own?

**Production and commercial**
19. Where does it run, in which territories, for how long?
20. Is exclusivity or category conflict required?
21. Is synthetic voice permitted, prohibited, or undecided by the client or the delivery contract?
22. Who approves the final voice, and how many approval rounds exist?

## B2. Decision Checklist In Dependency Order

Each step depends on the ones above it. Do not reorder.

1. **Confirm VO exists and no speaking mouth is in frame.** If a mouth is in frame, escalate to Director before anything else.
2. **Lock the person and POV.** Nothing below this line is stable until this is written down.
3. **Lock the function.** Narrator, interior, or brand voice.
4. **Get the locked runtime and locked script.** Word count and pace fall out of these.
5. **Derive the pace band** from word count and runtime, and check that silence still exists at that pace. If not, return to the writer for cuts before casting anything.
6. **Set the read register** with at least one named reference performer or named reference piece.
7. **Set the anti-register.** Name the read you must not get.
8. **Set gender, age, accent** against the on-screen cast, deciding match or contrast deliberately.
9. **Run the substitution test.** Flip one attribute, usually gender. Note everything in the production document that breaks. Investigate each break as a possible unstated assumption.
10. **Confirm usage and budget envelope.** Filter the candidate pool by what usage the fee can actually cover.
11. **Build the shortlist.** Three to five voices, each with a stated reason and a stated risk, not a ranked list of favourites.
12. **Write the direction plan** before the session: the adjustment ladder for this specific copy, and the two or three alternate points of view to be tried regardless of whether the first read works.
13. **Run the session.** Capture room tone. Log takes by number against the reason for each.
14. **Select.** Nominate one selected read, one alternate with a different point of view, and flag any line that needs a pickup.
15. **Deliver to Sound and Editor** with the take log, the room tone, and the pickup list.

## B3. What This Role Needs From Other Departments

Nothing can be locked without these inputs. Request them explicitly and name the blocker if they are missing.

| From | Needed | Why it blocks |
|---|---|---|
| **Writer / Director** | The final locked script, with person and POV explicitly declared | Casting against a moving script produces a session that must be repeated |
| **Editor** | The locked runtime, the timing map of where VO sits and where picture is clear, and a cut with the scratch track in it | Pace is derived arithmetic, not taste. Without timing there is no pace |
| **Sound** | Mix intentions: how much music and sound design will sit under the VO, and where the VO is the only element | A voice cast for a bare mix will disappear under a dense one, and a voice cast for a dense mix will feel harsh when exposed |
| **Director** | The tonal register of the whole piece, stated against references, and the anti-register | The single most common under-specification in the entire discipline |
| **Picture Casting** | The on-screen cast: gender, age, accent, and screen presence | Contrast cannot be designed without knowing what is being contrasted against |
| **Producer** | Usage, territory, term, exclusivity requirements, and the budget envelope | Determines which performers are legally and financially available |
| **Producer / Legal** | The client's AI and synthetic voice policy, plus any delivery contract restrictions | Determines whether a whole category of solution is even permitted |

## B4. What This Role Owes Other Departments

| To | Owed | By when |
|---|---|---|
| **Editor** | A scratch track early, at the intended pace, so the cut is built to real timing rather than to a guess | Before the offline is built out |
| **Editor** | The selected read as clean, separated line stems, plus a take log | At delivery |

## B5. Standard Tensions And Typical Director Resolutions

| Tension | Whose position | Typical Director resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Breathing room versus frames** | Editor wants seconds back. VO needs air or the read becomes a list. | Cut copy first. Music bed second. Speeding the read is a last resort and usually the wrong one. |
| **Client wants a well-known voice, budget covers a working professional** | Producer sides with budget. Client sides with recognition. | Establish that usage, not fame, is the dominant cost. Reallocate from usage breadth before reallocating from talent. |
| **Writer's favourite line does not fit** | Writer defends the line. VO reports it will not land at pace. | Line goes, or the runtime goes. Director picks. VO does not delete copy unilaterally. |
| **Sound wants a drier, closer read; Director wants a bigger one** | Both are describing the same word differently. | Record both. Decide in the mix, not in the booth. This is cheap insurance and should be default practice. |
| **Client note says "more energy"** | Almost always means "I am bored," rarely means "faster." | Diagnose before executing. Boredom is usually a picture or structure problem being reported as a VO problem. |
| **A speaking mouth appears in frame late in the process** | Director or client added it. | This converts VO to dialogue. Escalate to a scheduling and budget decision immediately. Do not absorb it silently. |
| **Second language sounds worse than the first** | Usually a translated-script symptom, not a casting failure. | Rewrite natively before recasting. Recasting a bad script produces a better read of a bad script. |
| **Client asks for a synthetic voice to save budget** | Producer sympathetic, VO cautious. | Test it against a human read at the same session cost point. If the piece sells authenticity, decline and say why. |

## B6. Failure Modes And Under-Specification Tells

Treat each of these as a stop condition. Name the gap and request the missing decision.

| Tell | What it actually means | The question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| VO described only by adjectives: "warm, confident, premium" with no reference | Nobody has decided the read | "Name one existing piece whose narration is close, and one that is close but wrong." |
| No person or POV declared in the script | The grammar was never decided, so the voice's relationship to the picture is unknown | "Is this copy first, second, or third person, and was that a decision?" |
| A speaking on-screen character with a VO credit | Dialogue is being budgeted as VO | "Is this mouth in frame? If yes, this is a picture-casting and sync problem." |
| Script has no target duration | The pace decision has been silently delegated to whoever records first | "What is the locked runtime?" |
| Word count implies a pace with zero silence | The script is too long and nobody has admitted it | "Which forty words come out?" |
| The same script exists in two languages with identical structure and length | It was translated, not written | "Who wrote the second-language version natively?" |
| A single voice is expected to be recognisable, neutral, and distinctive at once | Contradictory brief | "Distinctive and neutral are opposites. Which one wins?" |
| Usage described as "everywhere, ongoing" | No usage decision has been made, and the budget is unpriceable | "Which media, which territories, what term?" |
| Casting brief lists only voices the client already likes | Reference-gathering has replaced casting | "What is the read, independent of who performs it?" |
| The VO exists to explain what the picture already shows | The VO is patching a structural weakness | "What does the audience learn from the words that the images do not carry?" |
| Every take sounds the same despite many adjustments | Adjectives are being given instead of adjustments | Switch to the adjustment ladder. Change who the performer is talking to. |
| Client keeps asking for "less salesy" and it never improves | The copy induces the sell-tone | Direct against it, and simultaneously flag the copy as the root cause. |

## B8. Trigger List: What Forces A Re-Open

When an upstream change occurs, work down the relevant column. **Re-derive** means the decision must be made again from scratch and may come out differently. **Relabel** means the document text changes but the underlying decision does not.

| Upstream change | Must be genuinely RE-DERIVED | Merely RELABELLED |
|---|---|---|
| **On-screen cast gender or age recast** | Match-or-contrast decision, gender and age of the VO, the substitution test, any first-person attribution, music brief coherence | Cast names in the document, shot list captions |
| **Script rewrite** | Word count, pace, whether silence still exists, person and POV (verify it did not drift), the difficult-line list, the anti-register if the tone of the copy moved | Script version number, date |
| **Runtime change** | Pace band, whether copy must be cut, VO coverage pattern, block structure, the entire read register if the change is large | Delivery spec, format label |
| **Language added or changed** | Native writing pass, casting pass for that language, word count and timing for that language, whether that version needs its own edit, accent and dialect strength within that language | Language field in the deliverables table |
| **Pacing pivot (slower or faster overall)** | Read register (pace and register are coupled, not independent), the shortlist itself, breathing-room negotiation with the Editor, music brief | Nothing. A pacing pivot is a real re-open. |
| **POV change (first to second person or the reverse)** | Everything below function in the checklist. This is the deepest re-open available. Casting, lip-sync exposure, contrast strategy, and every line of copy | Nothing |
| **A speaking mouth added to frame** | The entire premise. This ceases to be VO. Escalate to Director and Producer before any other work | Nothing |
| **Usage expanded** | Affordability of the entire shortlist, exclusivity and conflict clearance, contract terms, possibly the selected performer | Territory list, term dates |
| **Mix intention changed (bare to dense, or dense to bare)** | Whether the selected read survives, whether a re-record at a different energy is needed, notes to Sound | Mix version number |
| **Synthetic voice permitted or prohibited mid-project** | The solution class, budget, consent and licence documentation, disclosure position | Nothing if prohibited late. If permitted late, treat as an addition, not a replacement, and re-derive whether it is appropriate for this piece at all |
| **Editor requests seconds back** | Copy content (which words go), not the pace. Do not resolve a runtime problem by speeding the read | Timecodes |

## B9. Standing Behavioural Rules For The Agent Occupying This Role

1. **Never accept an adjective as a read specification.** Convert every adjective into a named reference and a stated point of view, or ask for one.
2. **Never proceed past an undeclared POV.** Person and POV are the root of the dependency tree.
3. **Default to no speaking mouths in frame.** If one appears, stop and escalate. It is a budget and schedule event, not a style note.
4. **Always name the anti-register.** The read to avoid is more useful direction than the read to achieve.
5. **Always run the substitution test.** Flipping one attribute is the cheapest structural audit available.
6. **Report copy-length problems before the session, never after.** A read that only fits when pushed is a script problem being disguised as a performance problem.
7. **Record the alternate point of view every time**, even when the first read works. It costs minutes and it prevents an entire re-record when a client note lands.
8. **Capture room tone every session, unprompted.**
9. **Deliver unprocessed audio.** Processing is Sound's decision, and it is irreversible if baked in.
10. **Treat usage as the primary cost driver**, not the performer's reputation.
11. **Never let a voice resemble an identifiable real person without separate, specific, written consent.** This is the hardest line in the document.
12. **Distinguish sourced fact from principle when advising**, and say which one is being relied on.
